The global shipping industry, a critical but carbon-intensive sector, is at a crossroads as the International Maritime Organization (IMO) grapples with the final details of its Net-Zero Framework (NZF). New analysis from InfluenceMap reveals a complex landscape: while broad public support exists across shipping and fuels sectors for a global climate deal, significant differences in advocacy positions on crucial elements – including carbon pricing, fuel standards, and emissions accounting rules – threaten to dilute the framework's effectiveness. As negotiations resume this month ahead of MEPC 85 (November 30) and the crucial adoption session on December 4, the outcomes will directly shape the trajectory of global maritime CO2 emissions for decades to come, impacting everything from individual ship CII ratings to overall company carbon liabilities under emerging schemes like the EU ETS.

## A Fragile Consensus on IMO's Net-Zero Framework

The IMO's 2023 GHG Strategy set ambitious targets: net-zero emissions by or around 2050, with cuts of at least 20% by 2030 and 70% by 2040 (compared to 2008 levels). This ambitious vision led to the proposed NZF, garnering high-level support from 13 companies and industry associations, including major players like the International Chamber of Shipping (ICS), representing over 80% of the world's merchant fleet, and the World Shipping Council (WSC), covering 90% of global liner capacity. Shipping giants like A.P. Moller-Maersk, CMA CGM, Hapag-Lloyd, and Mediterranean Shipping Company have also voiced support, emphasizing the need for regulatory certainty to drive the industry's energy transition.

However, InfluenceMap notes a concerning shift since the NZF's adoption was postponed in October 2025. Key associations, including ICS and WSC, have adopted "softer messaging," avoiding direct naming of the framework and instead asking governments to "consider all options." This subtle but significant change leaves ample room for less ambitious proposals to gain traction, risking an agreement that falls short of the net-zero trajectory agreed to in 2023. Such a weakening would have tangible consequences for achieving verifiable emissions reductions, directly impacting efforts to improve [company rankings](/companies.html) on environmental performance.

## Carbon Pricing: The Cost of Emissions

One of the most contentious points in the NZF is the proposed carbon price on ships that fail to meet the fuel standard. The chasm between industry proposals highlights the battle over who bears the cost of decarbonization. The ICS, advocating for a "low and narrow" approach, proposed a carbon price of merely USD 10–20 per tonne of CO2e, charged only on emissions from burning fuel, not from its production. In stark contrast, the WSC backed a carbon price high enough to make clean fuels competitive, illustrating this at a much more impactful USD 74 per tonne in 2030.

This disparity is critical. A carbon price of USD 10-20/tonne is unlikely to provide a meaningful economic incentive for shipping companies to invest in expensive low-carbon fuels or advanced propulsion technologies. For context, the EU Emissions Trading System (EU ETS), which now applies to shipping, has seen carbon prices fluctuate significantly higher, often hovering around €70-90 per tonne of CO2. This demonstrates that for a carbon price to genuinely drive behavior change and technological innovation, it must be substantial enough to alter the economics of fuel choices. A weak global price would undermine the IMO's stated goals and perpetuate the use of high-emission fuels, as tracked by [emissions data](/charts.html) from platforms like ours.

## Fuel Standards and Accounting Loopholes

Beyond carbon pricing, disagreements on fuel standards and crucial life-cycle accounting rules pose further threats. The NZF includes a fuel standard that tightens over time, requiring a reduction in fuel GHG intensity. Here, the ICS proposed a mere 30% cut in fuel GHG intensity by 2040, less than half the 65% in the approved NZF. The WSC, conversely, supported the IMO’s more ambitious GHG intensity targets.

Perhaps even more concerning are the efforts by oil and gas sector groups to manipulate the rules for counting emissions. Ipieca and SEA-LNG, representing oil, gas, and LNG fuel interests, have actively pushed for accounting rules that would make fossil fuels, particularly LNG, appear "cleaner" than they are. This includes advocating for replacing the IMO’s cautious default values for fossil fuels with potentially lower averages and allowing certificates to stand in for clean power. SEA-LNG, for instance, argued that LNG’s production emissions are lower than regulators assume. If successful, these lobbying efforts could significantly weaken the framework's practical application without altering its headline targets, making it easier for LNG-powered ships to appear compliant even if their full life-cycle emissions remain high. This directly impacts the accuracy of performance metrics like [CII ratings](/cii.html), potentially rewarding ships that are not genuinely decarbonizing.

## Global vs. Regional: A Battle for Regulatory Supremacy

InfluenceMap's research also highlights a strategic move by industry to leverage support for the IMO framework against existing regional regulations. ICS, WSC, SEA-LNG, and European Shipowners (ECSA) have openly advocated against the EU's FuelEU Maritime regulation and the EU Emissions Trading System (ETS). They call for the review, harmonization, or even withdrawal of these EU-level regulations following the passage of the IMO NZF.

This stance is highly problematic for effective global decarbonization. These EU regulations are already in force and are central to meeting the EU’s climate targets, providing tangible incentives and penalties based on actual EU MRV data. Undermining robust regional efforts in favor of a potentially diluted global framework risks creating a regulatory vacuum or, at best, a lowest-common-denominator approach that fails to drive the urgent emissions reductions needed. Such calls illustrate a preference for a single, potentially less stringent global rule over a patchwork of regional regulations that are already demonstrating an impact on actual carbon costs and operational decisions.

## What this means for shipping emissions

The ongoing negotiations at the IMO are a pivotal moment for global shipping decarbonization. While the broad support for an IMO climate deal is encouraging, the devil is truly in the details. A diluted framework, characterized by a weak carbon price, lenient fuel standards, and loopholes in emissions accounting, risks undermining the IMO’s own net-zero ambitions and hindering the industry's ability to achieve substantial CO2 reductions. Such an outcome would mean continued reliance on fossil fuels, slower technological adoption, and ultimately, higher cumulative emissions. For data-driven platforms like DirtiestShips.com, it means continued vigilance in tracking actual emissions data from sources like EU MRV against regulatory promises, ensuring transparency and accountability regardless of the policy landscape.
