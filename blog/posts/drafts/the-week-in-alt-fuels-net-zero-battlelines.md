The global shipping industry stands at a critical juncture as the International Maritime Organization (IMO) grapples with competing proposals for its Net-Zero Framework (NZF). These discussions, detailed in a recent Hellenic Shipping News article, will determine the trajectory of maritime decarbonization, directly impacting future CO2 emissions, compliance costs for shipowners, and the pace of investment in zero-emission fuels. The outcome of MEPC 85 in November will set the global standard, influencing everything from individual vessel [CII ratings](/cii.html) to the overall effectiveness of carbon pricing mechanisms like the EU Emissions Trading System (ETS).

### The High Stakes of IMO's Net-Zero Framework

The Net-Zero Framework, initially approved at MEPC 83, aims to put the "polluter pays" principle into practice, ensuring those responsible for emissions contribute to financing the transition in climate-vulnerable countries. However, the path to net-zero is now being debated through widely divergent proposals. At one end of the spectrum are calls for significantly strengthening economic measures, while at the other, some seek to remove greenhouse gas (GHG) pricing altogether. These decisions will have profound implications for future global shipping emissions and the integrity of data collected via systems like EMSA MRV.

### Divergent Paths: Ambition vs. Gradualism

Among the most ambitious proposals is Tuvalu's, which seeks to drastically accelerate decarbonization. It suggests dropping the 2028 step in the GHG fuel intensity (GFI) reduction targets, initiating a 6% reduction in 2029, and moving to a direct compliance target of 100% from 2029 to 2035. Crucially, Tuvalu proposes tripling the initial Tier 1 remedial unit price from $100/mtCO2e to $300/mtCO2e, with Tier 2 remaining at $380/mtCO2e. Such a high carbon price, significantly above recent EU ETS prices, would generate substantial revenue for zero-emission fuels (ZEFs) but would also result in the highest transport cost increases, as noted by Tristan Smith of University College London. This would rapidly shift [company rankings](/companies.html) towards those investing heavily in green technology.

In contrast, Brazil has tabled a more gradual approach. Its proposal eases compliance requirements for the first two years (2029 and 2030) before aligning with the approved NZF trajectory from 2031. To offset this softer start, it proposes tightening the long-term base target to 70% in 2041, rather than 65% in 2040. Meanwhile, the ACSA-UK (Australia, Canada, South Africa, UK) joint proposal largely maintains the current NZF, only delaying implementation by one year.

### The Risk of Weakening: Pricing vs. Surplus Units

The most concerning proposals for global decarbonization come from those seeking to dilute the framework's economic teeth. Liberia's submission, supported by several other nations, notably removes GHG pricing entirely. Instead of mandatory remedial units, it relies solely on a market-based surplus unit (SU) trading mechanism, where ships can transfer, bank, and borrow SUs to meet GFI targets. As Opportunity Green highlights, this approach would weaken the stable price signal crucial for long-term investment in genuinely zero-emission fuels, exposing compliance costs to the volatility of carbon markets. Moreover, without a dedicated fund, there would be no clear mechanism to support a just transition for developing countries. This stands in stark contrast to the direct financial incentive provided by carbon pricing schemes like the EU ETS, which aims to drive tangible emissions reductions.

Japan's proposal also seeks to soften GFI reduction targets from 2030 and replaces payments into the Net Zero Fund with a "direct contribution" mechanism. This allows shipowners to either buy surplus units or contribute directly to approved projects. While addressing concerns about the IMO managing large revenue flows, experts like Tristan Smith argue it could weaken incentives for zero- and near-zero-emission fuels by favoring cheaper technologies like LNG and biofuels, complicating governance and introducing uncertainty. This directly undermines the push for significant reductions in actual emissions, which are meticulously tracked in our [emissions data](/charts.html).

### Implications for Operators and Emissions Tracking

The outcome of these debates will fundamentally reshape the operating landscape for the global shipping fleet. A robust, globally consistent carbon price mechanism, similar to the ambition seen in Tuvalu's proposal, would provide a clear and strong signal for investment in decarbonization technologies. Conversely, proposals that remove or weaken GHG pricing could create a fragmented regulatory environment, where companies navigating different regional schemes (like the EU ETS) face varying carbon costs and compliance burdens. This complexity would impact the efforts of shipowners to improve their [CII ratings](/cii.html) and manage their overall emissions footprint.

Regardless of the final framework, transparent and accurate emissions data, like that derived from EMSA MRV, will remain paramount. It is the foundation upon which any claims of progress are built, allowing platforms like DirtiestShips.com to hold the industry accountable and monitor global efforts against the Net-Zero Framework. ### What this means for shipping emissions

The decisions made at MEPC 85 will be pivotal. They will either set a clear, ambitious course for global shipping's decarbonization, backed by strong economic incentives and a stable carbon price, or they risk diluting the framework, fostering uncertainty, and potentially delaying the transition to net-zero. The choice will directly determine how quickly global CO2 emissions from shipping will decline and the economic burden associated with this transformation.
