As the global shipping industry navigates the increasingly complex and costly landscape of decarbonization, with the EU Emissions Trading System (ETS) now a significant financial burden, attention is turning to innovative solutions. A recent call from maritime emissions specialist EmissionLink advocates for a dedicated portion of EU ETS revenues to be reinvested directly into accelerating the development and commercial viability of onboard carbon capture technology. This proposal, highlighted in Hellenic Shipping News, underscores the critical intersection of regulatory pressure, technological innovation, and financial incentives in the race to cut shipping's CO2 footprint. Without targeted support, EmissionLink warns, this promising technology risks remaining just that – a promise, rather than a practical tool for compliance under frameworks like the EU MRV and the IMO's CII ratings.

## Fueling Innovation with ETS Revenues

EmissionLink's central proposition is straightforward: Europe has a unique opportunity to catalyze maritime carbon capture by directing EU ETS revenues towards full-scale demonstration projects. Philippos Ioulianou, Managing Director of EmissionLink, argues that such investment is crucial to establish whether the technology can deliver verifiable emissions reductions at an economically sustainable cost. While the International Maritime Organisation (IMO) is making progress on interim safety guidelines for these systems, Ioulianou stresses that "regulation alone will not persuade shipowners to invest in carbon capture." Shipowners need a robust business case, complete with verified savings, practical carbon disposal arrangements, and a clear understanding of the financial benefits, particularly as they contend with rising carbon costs tracked on platforms like DirtiestShips.com via our [emissions data](/charts.html).

The proposed demonstration projects would encompass every stage of the carbon capture process, from onboard collection and storage to offloading, transportation, and eventual permanent storage or approved use. These trials are intended to generate independently verifiable emissions data, essential for both regulatory development and future investment decisions.

## Navigating Commercial and Operational Hurdles

Despite the potential, EmissionLink cautions that onboard carbon capture is not a universal panacea for every vessel. Its practical application is likely limited by several factors. Ships operating on fixed and predictable routes present the most promising early opportunities, allowing operators to plan carbon storage capacity and establish reliable offloading arrangements at designated ports. For vessels engaged in irregular routes or spot charters, the logistical challenges escalate significantly due to the uncertainty of carbon reception facilities.

Beyond route predictability, technical suitability is also a major consideration. Factors such as vessel age, available space onboard for equipment, additional power demand, and remaining commercial life will all play a role in determining feasibility. Furthermore, installing carbon capture equipment introduces complex contractual questions for shipowners and charterers, including how to allocate costs for additional fuel consumption, potential offloading delays, and the precise distribution of regulatory compliance benefits. These operational and financial complexities are significant hurdles that must be addressed before widespread adoption can occur, directly impacting the operational profiles and, subsequently, the [CII ratings](/cii.html) of fleets.

## Emissions Accounting and Regulatory Recognition

A critical concern raised by EmissionLink is the urgent need for clarity surrounding emissions accounting and regulatory recognition. While the EU ETS framework acknowledges qualifying carbon capture coupled with permanent geological storage or chemical binding, the practical monitoring, reporting, and verification (MRV) arrangements for maritime carbon capture remain underdeveloped. This lack of clear methodology poses a substantial risk for shipowners seeking to justify significant capital expenditure.

More crucially, Mr. Ioulianou warns against assuming that carbon capture benefits recognized under the EU ETS will automatically provide equivalent compliance advantages under FuelEU Maritime. FuelEU Maritime regulates the greenhouse gas intensity of energy used onboard, rather than absolute emissions, meaning that "capturing carbon is not necessarily the same as delivering a verified reduction in emissions" in the eyes of all regulations. This distinction highlights a significant regulatory gap that needs bridging to provide shipowners with the certainty required to invest. For companies closely monitoring their [company rankings](/companies.html) based on reported emissions, the lack of clear accounting could lead to confusion and misrepresentation.

## What this means for shipping emissions

EmissionLink's call underscores a critical juncture for the shipping industry. Onboard carbon capture holds genuine promise as a significant decarbonization tool, offering a pathway to reduce absolute CO2 emissions. However, its widespread adoption hinges on robust financial incentives, clear operational pathways, and unequivocal regulatory recognition across schemes like EU ETS, FuelEU Maritime, and IMO's CII. Until dedicated investment from sources like ETS revenues materializes, coupled with defined MRV protocols and an unambiguous business case, the technology risks remaining on the drawing board. For now, the industry continues to grapple with existing regulations, relying on operational efficiencies and alternative fuels while the potential of carbon capture awaits its moment of validated impact on global shipping emissions.
