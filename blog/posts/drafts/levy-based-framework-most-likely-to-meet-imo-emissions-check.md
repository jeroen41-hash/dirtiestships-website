The global shipping industry stands at a critical juncture, with the International Maritime Organization (IMO) racing against its own 2023 GHG Strategy targets to forge a framework that can genuinely deliver deep emissions cuts. A new comparative analysis from the Institute of Marine Engineering, Science and Technology (IMarEST) provides crucial insights, suggesting that a levy-based mechanism is the most probable path to meeting the IMO’s ambitious 2030 and 2040 emissions reduction checkpoints. This finding underscores the intensifying regulatory pressure on the shipping sector, with significant implications for operational costs, fuel choices, and the overall trajectory of decarbonization.

## IMO's Net-Zero Framework: Weighing the Options

As negotiations intensify ahead of the 22nd Intersessional Working Group on GHG Emissions from Ships (ISWG-GHG 22), the IMO is evaluating four proposed MARPOL amendment options for its Net-Zero Framework (NZF). The IMarEST's submission, drawing on modeling from University College London's Energy Institute Shipping and Oceans Research Group and the Rocky Mountain Institute, marks the first time all four proposals have been modeled side-by-side. This comprehensive analysis, which builds on the IMO's own impact assessments, aims to provide member states with a clearer understanding of each option's potential effectiveness in achieving the IMO's aggressive emissions targets.

## The Case for a Levy-Based System

The IMarEST's analysis strongly indicates that a levy-based framework offers the highest probability of delivering the IMO's 2030 and 2040 emissions reduction checkpoints. This option proposes a system of fixed financial penalties for vessels that fail to comply with established emissions standards. Crucially, it also includes provisions for subsidies, which would be directed towards operators actively adopting zero or near-zero emission fuels and technologies. Such a mechanism creates a powerful two-pronged incentive: penalizing high-emitting vessels while rewarding those investing in greener alternatives. This direct financial lever could accelerate the uptake of cleaner fuels and drive operational efficiencies far more effectively than less stringent measures.

## Carbon Abatement Costs and the Biofuel Conundrum

While the levy-based system emerges as the frontrunner for meeting emissions targets, the IMarEST study also offers an interesting counterpoint regarding abatement costs. It suggests that the NZF, if retained in its current form, *could* offer the lowest average carbon abatement cost among the pricing-based options. However, this is contingent on a critical condition: that significant increases in biofuel prices are avoided before 2038. This highlights a major challenge for the industry: the volatility and long-term price stability of alternative fuels. Such uncertainties directly impact strategic investment decisions and the economic viability of adopting certain low-carbon pathways. For companies already grappling with rising costs, particularly those impacting their [CII ratings](/cii.html), accurately forecasting future fuel prices is paramount.

## Global Carbon Costs vs. Regional Initiatives

The potential adoption of a global levy-based system by the IMO draws strong parallels with existing regional carbon pricing mechanisms, such as the EU Emissions Trading System (ETS). The EU ETS has already begun to impose significant [EU ETS carbon costs](/charts.html) on voyages within and to/from European ports, driving a re-evaluation of operational strategies and fleet efficiency. A global IMO levy would amplify this financial pressure exponentially, extending its reach to *all* international shipping, not just those trading in EU waters.

Such a global carbon price would fundamentally alter the economic landscape for shipping companies. Less efficient vessels, as evidenced by their [CII ratings](/cii.html) and their reported emissions via EMSA MRV data, would face considerably higher operating costs. This escalating financial burden will undoubtedly drive further scrutiny of [company rankings](/companies.html) as operators are forced to prioritize investments in fuel efficiency, retrofits, or newbuilds capable of utilizing zero-emission fuels. The data we track at DirtiestShips.com, derived from EU MRV and other sources, becomes even more vital in this context, providing transparency on actual emissions performance against a backdrop of increasing carbon costs.

## What this means for shipping emissions

The IMarEST's analysis provides clear evidence that aggressive, financially-driven mechanisms are likely essential for the IMO to meet its critical emissions reduction goals for 2030 and 2040. A global levy-based system, while imposing new costs, would create an unavoidable economic imperative for decarbonization across the entire industry. This means accelerated adoption of operational efficiencies, faster uptake of alternative fuels, and significant investment in new, cleaner propulsion technologies. The era of cheap, carbon-intensive shipping is rapidly drawing to a close, replaced by a future where emissions performance directly impacts profitability and competitiveness.
