The International Maritime Organisation's (IMO) 22nd Intersessional Working Group on Reduction of GHG Emissions from Ships (ISWG-GHG 22), which concluded on September 4, provided a crucial albeit slow-moving update on the global shipping industry's decarbonisation journey. While no final legal text was produced, negotiators signalled "renewed momentum" towards a Net-Zero Framework (NZF), a development that holds significant implications for future regulatory compliance, carbon costs under schemes like the EU ETS, and ultimately, the CO2 emissions profiled daily on DirtiestShips.com. The persistent disagreements, particularly on carbon pricing and revenue distribution, underscore the complexity of transforming ambitious targets into enforceable global standards.

## Carbon Pricing: The Unresolved Core of IMO's Net-Zero Framework

At the heart of the IMO's Net-Zero Framework (NZF) lies the contentious issue of a global carbon pricing mechanism and how its revenues would be distributed. According to an analysis by the UCL Energy Institute, discussions at ISWG-GHG 22 reaffirmed "substantial, resilient support" for establishing a centralised structure for receiving and managing these revenues. The report highlights that 38 member states intervened in favour of some form of revenue collection and distribution mechanism, significantly outweighing the 17 states that advocated for a technical-only redesign without a central financial component.

This divergence mirrors the global debate on carbon taxation, a mechanism already implemented regionally via the EU Emissions Trading System (ETS) which now covers shipping. While the IMO aims for a unified global approach, the struggle to agree on how to internalize the cost of emissions – and crucially, who benefits from the generated funds – remains a primary hurdle. Proposals from several nations, including Japan's idea for direct contributions to approved projects instead of greenhouse gas pricing, failed to gain significant traction, suggesting the foundational architecture of a pricing mechanism, however debated, is likely to endure. This ongoing negotiation directly impacts future carbon costs for shipowners, potentially extending financial obligations similar to those now seen under the EU ETS to a global scale.

## Emissions Trajectory and Fuel Incentives: A Glimmer of Flexibility

While the big-picture financial mechanisms remain elusive, ISWG-GHG 22 did show progress on the operational aspects of emissions reduction. Delegates discussed the Global Fuel Intensity (GFI) reduction pathway, with the UCL report indicating a likely softening of initial stringency during the 2029-2035 period. This flexibility would be offset by steeper reductions later, ensuring that ambitious long-term goals remain in place, particularly for a defined 2040 target, which industry observers deem crucial for investment certainty in future fuels and infrastructure.

Crucially, there was continued strong support for mechanisms that actively encourage the uptake of zero- and near-zero emission (ZNZ) fuels. A large majority of countries backed dedicated reward mechanisms for ZNZ fuels, with growing interest in multipliers linked to surplus-unit values. This signals the IMO's intent to not just penalize high emitters but to proactively shape fuel markets and incentivize cleaner alternatives. For vessels tracked via their [CII ratings](/cii.html) on DirtiestShips.com, this indicates that future operational profiles will increasingly be judged not just on current efficiency but on their transition pathway to low-carbon fuels. Companies that strategically invest in ZNZ fuel capabilities early will likely gain a competitive advantage and improve their standing in future [company rankings](/companies.html).

## The Road Ahead: Deferred Details and Decisive Meetings

Despite the political signals of momentum, the technical details crucial for implementation continue to be a bottleneck. Discussions on vital implementation guidelines and the Life Cycle Assessment (LCA) framework for alternative fuels were largely postponed due to time constraints, deferred to ISWG-GHG 23, scheduled for November 2026. The LCA framework, in particular, is essential for accurately assessing the full emissions footprint of new fuels, preventing unintended consequences or "carbon leakage" upstream. Without clear guidelines and robust LCA methodologies, the practical enforcement and verification of the NZF will remain challenging.

The UCL team stresses that the upcoming ISWG-GHG 23 meeting will be "decisive," with a continuing commitment among delegates to reach a conclusion before the end of the year, targeting MEPC 85 for the final text. This urgency highlights the pressure on the IMO to deliver a credible global framework amidst growing regional regulatory pressures and the industry's need for long-term predictability to guide massive investment decisions in new vessels, fuels, and infrastructure.

## What this means for shipping emissions

The ISWG-GHG 22 meeting offered a mixed bag of persistent challenges and renewed political will. While a global carbon pricing mechanism remains contentious, the broad support for its underlying architecture persists, signaling that a global cost for emissions is increasingly inevitable. Simultaneously, the focus on softening near-term intensity targets while incentivizing zero- and near-zero emission fuels indicates a pragmatic approach to facilitate transition without compromising long-term goals. For DirtiestShips.com, these developments mean that the regulatory landscape is rapidly shifting, promising stricter emission standards and higher operational costs for high-emitting vessels. The IMO's journey towards net-zero is slow, but the direction of travel is clear: cleaner, more accountable shipping is on the horizon.
