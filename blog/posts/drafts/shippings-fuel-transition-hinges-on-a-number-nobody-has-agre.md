The global shipping industry is caught in a profound paradox: mandated to decarbonize, yet operating without a clear understanding of the future fuels it will burn or the precise carbon price it will face. As vessels capable of running on alternative fuels like methanol and ammonia begin to enter the fleet, the latest analysis from the Global Centre for Maritime Decarbonization (GCMD) and Boston Consulting Group (BCG) makes it starkly clear: conventional bunkers remain the cheapest option at scale, and the regulatory framework intended to bridge this economic gap is still hotly debated. For platforms like DirtiestShips.com, which track actual CO2 emissions using EU MRV data, this regulatory inertia underscores the critical importance of transparent emissions reporting to hold the industry accountable.

### The Carbon Price Conundrum: A Global Standoff

The core of shipping's decarbonization challenge, according to the GCMD-BCG study, hinges on a single, yet elusive, number: the global carbon price. The International Maritime Organization's (IMO) Net-Zero Framework, initially slated for formal adoption in October 2025, has been delayed to December 2026 due to member state opposition, notably from Washington. This postponement leaves shipowners committing significant capital to newbuilds without certainty on the carbon costs they will incur.

The study's models reveal the dramatic impact of this price. If the carbon penalty remains at the currently discussed level of USD 380 per tonne of CO2 equivalent, conventional fuel oil paired with onboard carbon capture remains the most cost-effective compliant option through mid-century. This scenario predicts a fleet still dominated by fossil fuels, raising significant concerns for global emissions trajectories and potentially impacting [company emissions rankings](/companies.html) for years to come. Only if the carbon price were to rise substantially, nearing USD 700 per tonne, does the economic calculus decisively shift towards greener alternatives like methanol, ammonia, and ethanol. This highlights the carbon price as the single most powerful lever available to policymakers.

### Regional Efforts vs. Global Gridlock: The EU ETS Case Study

While the global debate rages, regional regulations like the EU's Emissions Trading System (ETS) and FuelEU Maritime are attempting to drive change, albeit with limited scope. These initiatives, crucial for informing our EU MRV data, collectively cover only about a fifth of international shipping's total energy use, applying solely to voyages touching European ports. The GCMD-BCG modeling suggests that outside this regulated pool, the fleet largely continues to burn the cheapest available fuel.

Brussels appears to acknowledge this limitation. In July 2026, the European Commission proposed a revision to its ETS, reserving up to 110 million emissions allowances between 2028 and 2040 specifically to subsidize the higher costs of low- and zero-emission marine fuels. This move is an implicit admission that existing regulatory pressure alone has not been sufficient to rapidly shift market economics. Meanwhile, the UK extended its own ETS to domestic shipping from July 2026, adding another layer of compliance complexity and cost for operators. These regional developments, while positive, underscore the fragmentation of regulatory efforts and the ongoing need for comprehensive [emissions data](/charts.html) to track their true impact.

### Future Fuels: Ammonia, Methanol, and the Green Hydrogen Hurdle

The GCMD-BCG analysis identifies e-ammonia and e-methanol as the leading long-term candidates for decarbonized shipping, neither with a decisive advantage. E-ammonia is cheaper to produce but comes with significant handling costs due to its toxicity and need for refrigeration, demanding specialist storage, larger exclusion zones, and extensive crew training. E-methanol is simpler to handle but requires a sustainable carbon source, making the price of biogenic CO2 a critical swing factor. Below roughly USD 150 per tonne of biogenic CO2, methanol tends to be more cost-effective; above it, ammonia often wins out. For both fuels, however, the price of green hydrogen remains the dominant input, accounting for over half of their total cost out to 2050.

Interestingly, a live data point offers a glimpse into a potentially accelerating cost curve. A March 2026 study by WinGD and Envision Energy on ammonia bunkering economics for the China-to-Australia route, using a real bunker price of USD 710 per tonne, calculated that ammonia-fueled bulk carriers and container ships could already undercut equivalent VLSFO-powered vessels. While specific to one route and supplier, this suggests that the cost parity anticipated for the 2030s by the GCMD-BCG model might be achievable at the margins today, offering a potential boost to the [CII ratings](/cii.html) of early adopters.

### The Ethanol Dilemma: A Regulatory Class Divide

Ethanol's future in shipping illustrates how regulatory classification, rather than chemistry, can dictate a fuel's fate. First-generation, food-crop-based ethanol is currently cost-competitive and available at scale, with the study projecting it could supply nearly a tenth of the fleet's energy needs by 2050 under neutral regulatory treatment. However, the EU’s FuelEU Maritime Regulation already excludes food-crop biofuels from favourable treatment, assigning them the emissions factor of the dirtiest available fossil fuel pathway. If this stance were applied globally, ethanol's contribution to shipping's energy mix would collapse to near zero, with the lost volume largely absorbed by conventional fuel oil, not other green alternatives. This scenario highlights how fragmented or punitive regulatory approaches can inadvertently hinder decarbonization efforts by pushing the industry back to fossil fuels.

### What This Means for Shipping Emissions

The GCMD-BCG study powerfully reinforces that without a robust, globally agreed-upon carbon price mechanism from the IMO, the shipping industry’s transition away from fossil fuels will remain economically challenging and slow. Regional initiatives, while valuable, cannot compensate for the lack of a strong global signal. The delay in the IMO’s Net-Zero Framework adoption continues to create uncertainty, hindering investment in cleaner technologies and alternative fuels. For DirtiestShips.com, this translates into a continued reliance on EU MRV data and other emissions reporting to track the actual environmental footprint of the global fleet, irrespective of policy debates. Until a clear, impactful carbon price is set, the drive for decarbonization will largely depend on the few pioneers and regional mandates, making the industry's path to net-zero unnecessarily long and complex.
