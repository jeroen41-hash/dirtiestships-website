The latest EU MRV data reveals a concerning rise in greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions from shipping, with total emissions on EU-linked voyages climbing by 2.1% to 156 million mtCO2eq in 2025. This headline figure, while alarming at first glance, demands a nuanced understanding. Analysis of the verified data indicates that the increase is largely attributable to the expanded scope of the EU's reporting requirements, bringing thousands of smaller vessels and new categories into the regulatory spotlight. This development underscores the growing reach of EU regulations like the EU Emissions Trading System (ETS) and the Carbon Intensity Indicator (CII), critical tools for driving decarbonization in an industry facing immense pressure.

## The Expanding Net of EU MRV: What the Numbers Show

The 2.1% increase in total GHG emissions, translating to 156 million mtCO2eq in 2025, marks a significant shift from the 153 million mtCO2eq reported in 2024. CO2 emissions specifically rose by 1.8% from 148 million mt to 151 million mt. More strikingly, methane emissions surged by 30% from 66,000 mt to 85,000 mt (accounting for approximately 2.4 million mtCO2eq), and nitrous oxide emissions increased by 1.2% to 8,000 mt (around 2.2 million mtCO2eq). It's crucial to note that 2024 was the first year these potent non-CO2 GHGs were mandated for reporting, providing an initial baseline for monitoring.

The primary driver behind this overall increase is the expansion of the EU MRV scope. From January 1, 2025, general cargo ships of 400-5,000 gross tonnage (GT) and offshore vessels of 400 GT and above were required to report emissions for the first time. This brought roughly 3,000 additional ships into the reporting framework, swelling the total number of vessels from 14,000 to 17,000. Specifically, the number of general cargo vessels in scope more than doubled from 1,400 to nearly 3,000, and offshore vessels rose from a mere six to 489.

Emissions from these newly regulated vessels are estimated to be between 4 million and 6 million mtCO2eq, effectively accounting for the majority of the headline increase. Crucially, ENGINE's analysis of the EMSA MRV data suggests that *excluding* these newly mandated vessels, total GHG emissions would have actually seen a reduction of up to 1.8%, or 3 million mtCO2eq, to 150 million mtCO2eq in 2025. This nuanced perspective is vital for understanding the true progress (or lack thereof) within the previously established fleet.

## Sectoral Shifts: Container Dominance and Geopolitical Impacts

Within the broader emissions landscape, container ships continue to dominate, reporting 53 million mtCO2eq in 2025. This figure, largely comprising 52 million mtCO2, surpasses the combined emissions of oil tankers, bulk carriers, and ro-pax vessels. Notably, CO2 emissions from container ships actually fell by 3.6% from 54 million mtCO2 in 2024.

However, this recent dip must be viewed in the context of persistent geopolitical disruptions. Container ship CO2 emissions in 2025 remain significantly higher than the 36 million mtCO2 reported in 2023, prior to the widespread rerouting away from the Suez Canal due to Houthi attacks in the Red Sea. This highlights how operational changes, driven by external factors rather than efficiency improvements, continue to impact emissions trajectories. Beyond container ships, oil tankers contributed 17 million mtCO2eq in 2025, representing approximately 11% of all emissions. Bulk carriers followed closely at 10%, and ro-pax ships at 9%. These figures underscore the diverse contributions of different vessel types to the overall shipping emissions footprint, each facing unique challenges in decarbonization.

## Implications for CII Ratings and EU ETS Costs

The expansion of EU MRV reporting has significant ramifications for both the Carbon Intensity Indicator (CII) ratings and the financial burden of the EU Emissions Trading System (ETS). Vessels entering the MRV scope for the first time will now have their emissions tracked, forming the baseline for future CII ratings. Owners of these newly included general cargo and offshore vessels will need to rapidly assess their operational profiles and consider measures to avoid poor ratings, which can have commercial repercussions. Our [CII ratings](/cii.html) tool provides a comprehensive overview of how these regulations impact vessel performance.

Furthermore, the aggregation of emissions from these thousands of additional vessels will directly impact the EU ETS. Even if individual smaller vessels contribute less, their collective emissions of 4-6 million mtCO2eq represent new demand for EU Allowances (EUAs). This increased demand, without a proportional increase in the ETS cap, could exert upward pressure on carbon prices, affecting all participants in the scheme. Owners must integrate these potential carbon costs into their operational planning and investment decisions, considering both current and future regulatory tightening.

## What this means for shipping emissions

The latest EU MRV data presents a complex picture. While the headline increase in shipping GHG emissions is concerning, a deeper analysis reveals it's primarily a consequence of regulatory expansion, bringing more of the global fleet under scrutiny. This transparency is vital. The fact that emissions from the *existing* fleet might have slightly decreased is a cautiously optimistic signal, though dwarfed by the lingering impact of geopolitical events on sectors like container shipping. The significant rise in methane emissions also highlights the critical need to monitor all GHGs comprehensively.

Ultimately, the expanded MRV scope, coupled with the ongoing implementation of CII and ETS, provides an increasingly robust framework for accountability. However, the data underscores that while the regulatory net is widening, the underlying challenge of truly reducing emissions across the entire global shipping industry remains immense. Continued scrutiny, data-driven analysis, and concerted action across all vessel types are essential to steer shipping towards its decarbonization targets.
