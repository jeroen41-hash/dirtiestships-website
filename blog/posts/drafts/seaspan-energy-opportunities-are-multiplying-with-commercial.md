As the global shipping industry grapples with increasingly stringent emissions regulations and the financial weight of carbon pricing, the push for lower-carbon fuel alternatives intensifies. Recent activity from Seaspan Energy (SE), a member of the Seaspan Marine group, highlights the current reliance on liquefied natural gas (LNG) as a transitional fuel, but also underscores the broader challenges in decarbonizing a notoriously difficult industry. Their rapid expansion in LNG bunkering services signals a market response to regulatory pressure, yet wider trends indicate a complex and uncertain path for shipowners navigating the transition.

## Seaspan's LNG Push: A Bridge to Somewhere?

Seaspan Energy has rapidly emerged as a significant player in the LNG bunkering market. According to a recent article by resourceworks.com, SE completed its 150th ship-to-ship LNG bunkering in under two years since launching operations, securing seventh place globally in Lansdowne Moritz's LNG bunkering volume rankings. This includes Canada's first ship-to-ship LNG bunkering in the Port of Vancouver in January 2025. This aggressive expansion by Seaspan, whose parent company Seaspan Corp. operates a fleet of 227 container ships with a total capacity of approximately 2.4 million TEU, demonstrates a clear commitment to supporting LNG as a "bridge fuel." The company's strategy anticipates that demand for dual-fuel ships will grow as "maritime shipping’s pollution restrictions tighten and carbon emissions penalties erode earnings," a direct reference to the financial implications of regulations like the EU Emissions Trading System (ETS).

## The Dual-Fuel Dilemma: Market Trends vs. Investment

Despite Seaspan's optimistic investment in LNG bunkering infrastructure, the broader market for alternative-fuel ships presents a more mixed picture. The resourceworks.com article notes that orders for alternative-fuel ships in 2026’s first half are projected to be 137, a drop from 155 in 2025’s first six months, with a substantial 45% decrease in overall 2025 orders compared to 2024. This slowdown suggests that concerns over "fuel availability, regulatory uncertainty, and comparative cost" are dampening demand. Shipowners face considerable risk when committing to newbuilds costing around US$100 million for a 10,000 TEU container ship with a 25-year lifespan, especially when bunker fuel can represent 40% to 60% of operating budgets. This uncertainty impacts [CII ratings](/cii.html) projections, as a vessel's future operational carbon intensity is heavily dependent on the viability and uptake of its intended fuel.

## LNG's Dominance Amidst Future Fuel Uncertainties

Despite the recent dip in overall alternative-fuel ship orders, LNG maintains a dominant position among current lower-carbon alternatives. The World Bank Group's "Ports, Ships, and Fuels" report highlights that approximately 470 LNG-fueled ships are currently in service globally, with over 400 more on order. This significantly outpaces other options, with a recent analysis by Xinde Marine News noting 173 LNG-capable ships ordered in 2026's first half compared to only four methanol ships. While major carriers like A.P. Moller-Maersk remain committed to decarbonization, the multi-billion dollar gamble on bridge fuels means LNG, for now, holds the "inside track." This reliance on LNG, while a step away from heavy fuel oil, still means significant methane slip concerns and higher lifecycle emissions compared to truly zero-emission fuels. ## Infrastructure and Global Ambitions

The commercial viability of any alternative fuel hinges on robust bunkering infrastructure. The World Bank Group estimates that by 2040, supporting global maritime fuel supply could require about US$310 billion for green ammonia, US$81 billion for green methanol, and US$42 billion for renewable LNG. These figures underscore the massive investment required for a true energy transition. China is actively pursuing dominance in green shipping, with its Ministry of Industry and Information Technology targeting over 50% international market share for green-powered vessels. This includes large-scale LNG bunkering infrastructure at key ports, demonstrating a strategic national commitment. While British Columbia, as explored in the resourceworks.com article, has geographic advantages to become a transpacific fueling hub, its success will depend on leveraging private-sector "mettle" like Seaspan's alongside government commitment. As we track [company rankings](/companies.html), it becomes clear that investment in infrastructure is as critical as individual vessel efficiency.

## What this means for shipping emissions

Seaspan Energy's significant push into LNG bunkering reflects an industry in transition, leveraging a readily available "bridge fuel" to mitigate immediate regulatory and carbon cost pressures. However, the broader slowdown in alternative-fuel ship orders, coupled with the long-term investment needed for truly green fuels, highlights the complex path to decarbonization. While LNG offers a marginal improvement over conventional heavy fuel oil, it is not a zero-emission solution. The market's current preference for LNG, driven by existing infrastructure and lower costs, could delay the necessary shift to fuels like green ammonia or methanol, impacting future [CII ratings](/cii.html) and increasing cumulative emissions. The shipping industry must accelerate investment in genuinely sustainable fuel pathways and supportive infrastructure to meet global climate targets, rather than relying indefinitely on transitional solutions.
