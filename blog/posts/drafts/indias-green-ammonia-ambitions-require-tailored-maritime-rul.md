The global shipping industry, responsible for 3% of global CO2 emissions, faces an existential challenge in meeting the International Maritime Organization (IMO)'s net-zero target by around 2050. Amidst this imperative, green ammonia is rapidly emerging as a leading contender for future marine fuel, projected to capture an average 35% of the shipping fuels market by 2050. However, as India rapidly positions itself as a major producer and exporter, securing long-term agreements with global buyers, the path to a genuinely sustainable future demands a robust examination of green ammonia's full emissions profile and the comprehensive regulatory frameworks required to govern its adoption. DirtiestShips.com continues to track these developments, analyzing their profound implications for vessel emissions, [CII ratings](/cii.html), and [EU ETS carbon costs](/news.html) for the sector.

## India's Ambitious Green Ammonia Drive

India is making significant strides in establishing itself as a global hub for green ammonia production and export. The nation's Ministry of New and Renewable Energy (MNRE) has set a stringent standard, limiting total lifecycle GHG emissions to 0.38 kg of CO2 equivalent per kg of ammonia. Furthermore, India's Harit Sagar Green Port Guidelines (2023) mandate all major ports to establish green ammonia bunkering and refuelling facilities by 2035, identifying key "Green Hydrogen Hubs" like Paradip Port (developing a 4 mtpa green hydrogen/ammonia jetty), V.O. Chidambaranar Port, and Deendayal Port Authority (commissioning a 1 MW green hydrogen plant and 3.5 mtpa compatible jetty).

Private players are also scaling up, cementing India's role in the emerging green ammonia value chain. AM Green has signed a long-term offtake agreement with Germany’s Uniper to supply 500 kilotons per annum (ktpa) of green ammonia, while Reliance Industries Limited is set to supply green ammonia to South Korea’s Samsung C&T Corporation over 15 years, starting in 2029. Most recently, Larsen and Toubro agreed to supply 300 ktpa to ITOCHU for green bunkering applications, as detailed in the orfonline.org article. These substantial investments underscore a clear commitment to integrating green fuels into a maritime sector that carries 95% of India's trade by volume.

## The Full Emissions Picture: CO2, N2O, and NOx

While green ammonia offers the promise of CO2-free combustion, a closer look reveals a more complex emissions profile that requires careful consideration. Ammonia combustion typically requires a pilot fuel, often diesel, which can account for 5–10 percent of the total fuel mix. This directly translates to residual CO2 emissions from what is marketed as a "zero-carbon" fuel.

More critically, ammonia combustion generates nitrous oxide (N2O), a potent greenhouse gas with a Global Warming Potential (GWP) approximately 273 times greater than CO2 over 100 years. Additionally, it produces nitrogen oxides (NOx), which contribute to air pollution, fine particulate matter, acid rain, and smog, posing significant risks to human health and the environment. This means that vessels adopting green ammonia will still have a notable carbon footprint, not just from pilot fuels, but also from direct N2O emissions. For platforms like DirtiestShips.com, accurately tracking these multi-faceted emissions will be paramount to assessing true environmental performance.

## Navigating Regulatory Waters: From India to IMO

The potential of green ammonia as a sustainable marine fuel hinges not only on production capacity but also on robust regulatory frameworks. As the article highlights, India is developing domestic regulations like the Harit Sagar Green Port Guidelines and the MNRE's lifecycle GHG emissions standard. However, the international maritime community, led by the IMO, must establish comprehensive global rules addressing both safety and emissions challenges.

Green ammonia's highly toxic and corrosive properties pose significant hazards to maritime workers during handling and potential leakage, as well as to marine life. The IMO's 2023 Strategy on Reduction of GHG Emissions identifies the uptake of zero- or near-zero-GHG-emission fuels as key, but detailed safety protocols, monitoring, and certification for ammonia as a marine fuel are still evolving. Without harmonized and stringent international standards, the risks associated with widespread adoption could undermine its environmental benefits and operational safety.

## Implications for Shipping's Carbon Footprint and Compliance

The emergence of green ammonia, with its nuanced emissions profile, carries significant implications for shipping companies and regulatory compliance:

*   **CII Ratings:** While CII primarily focuses on operational CO2 emissions per transport work, the CO2 generated by the pilot fuel will directly impact a vessel's [CII ratings](/cii.html). As regulatory bodies increasingly scrutinize lifecycle emissions, the broader GHG impact, including N2O, could eventually be integrated into such rating systems, demanding a holistic approach to fuel choice.
*   **EU ETS Carbon Costs:** This is a critical area. The EU Emissions Trading System (ETS) for shipping already covers CO2 and will expand to include methane (CH4) from 2024 and nitrous oxide (N2O) from 2026. Given N2O's extremely high GWP, ammonia-powered vessels operating within EU waters will face substantial carbon costs for their N2O emissions, in addition to any CO2 from pilot fuels. This necessitates a thorough analysis of overall carbon costs, not just CO2, for route planning and bunkering strategies. Companies can track aggregate ETS costs on our [emissions data](/charts.html) page.
*   **Company Emissions Rankings:** Companies like AM Green, Reliance, Uniper, Samsung C&T, and ITOCHU, which are making early investments in the green ammonia value chain, will see their future [company rankings](/companies.html) profoundly influenced by how effectively they mitigate *all* emissions, including N2O and NOx, not just CO2. Accurate measurement and reporting, via systems like EMSA MRV, will be crucial.

### What this means for shipping emissions

Green ammonia represents a significant stride towards decarbonizing the global shipping industry, particularly with nations like India championing its production. However, its adoption is not a simple swap. The true environmental benefit will depend entirely on how effectively the industry and regulators address the full emissions spectrum—accounting for pilot fuel CO2, highly potent N2O, and harmful NOx—alongside critical safety concerns. Without robust, internationally harmonized regulations and comprehensive emissions accounting, green ammonia's promise risks being diluted. The sector must ensure that the transition to new fuels genuinely reduces the overall climate impact, moving beyond a single-minded focus on CO2 to encompass all greenhouse gases and pollutants for a truly sustainable future.
