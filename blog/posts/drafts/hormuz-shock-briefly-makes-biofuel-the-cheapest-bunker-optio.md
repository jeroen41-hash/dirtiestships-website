The recent turmoil in global energy markets, particularly the fallout from conflict in the Middle East impacting the Strait of Hormuz, has unveiled a critical, albeit temporary, inflection point for marine fuels. For a brief period, the combination of soaring fossil fuel prices and the escalating costs of carbon under the EU Emissions Trading System (ETS) transformed biofuel into the more economical bunkering choice in Rotterdam. This development underscores the growing, tangible impact of carbon pricing mechanisms on operational decisions for the global shipping fleet, directly affecting reported emissions and the financial calculus for carriers navigating increasingly stringent environmental regulations.

## The Hormuz Effect: Carbon Costs Shift the Balance

A new white paper from energy pricing specialist General Index and the London Stock Exchange Group (LSEG) highlights an unprecedented market dynamic in April. B30, a blend of 30% used cooking oil methyl ester (UCOME) and 70% very low sulphur fuel oil (VLSFO), became $74.61 per tonne cheaper than conventional VLSFO in Rotterdam. This calculation critically factors in EU ETS allowances and potential FuelEU Maritime penalties. For context, in the three months prior, B30 had been consistently $11 to $27 per tonne more expensive on the same basis. Furthermore, the report, "Lessons From Hormuz: Disruptions For Renewable Fuel Cost Competitiveness," found that B100 UCOME traded at a discount to marine gasoil for eight weeks between March and May when carbon costs were included. This abrupt reversal demonstrates how external geopolitical events, when combined with regulatory frameworks, can rapidly alter the economic viability of cleaner fuels, influencing shipping's overall [emissions data](/charts.html).

## Unpacking the Price Dynamics

The primary driver behind this shift was the dramatic increase in fossil fuel prices following the closure of the Strait of Hormuz. Between February and March, average European gasoil prices surged by $476 per tonne. In contrast, UCOME, a key component of marine biodiesels, saw a comparatively modest increase of just $65 per tonne over the same period. This differential in price sensitivity insulated biodiesels to some extent from the broader energy market shock.

While traditional biodiesels proved relatively resilient, the study notes that other renewable fuels like hydrotreated vegetable oil (HVO) and sustainable aviation fuel (SAF) experienced sharp price increases, mirroring their fossil equivalents. For instance, SAF prices jumped $636 per tonne between February and March, despite largely independent supply chains. It is important to note the authors' caution: these figures reflect a temporary market anomaly, not a permanent shift, and the Rotterdam comparisons incorporate *potential* regulatory penalties, which may not yet be universally incurred by every vessel. Biofuels generally remain more expensive than conventional bunkers *before* carbon costs are applied.

## Carbon Pricing: A Growing Lever for Decarbonization

The General Index and LSEG report strongly argues that carbon pricing mechanisms are beginning to prove their effectiveness in making renewable marine fuels commercially competitive. This specific instance illustrates how the financial burden of carbon emissions, imposed by regulations like the EU ETS, can create a powerful incentive for fuel switching. For shipping companies, integrating carbon costs into bunkering decisions is no longer theoretical; it's an immediate financial reality that directly impacts profitability and competitive standing.

For vessels operating within the EU MRV scope, the choice of fuel directly influences their reported CO2 emissions and, consequently, their compliance costs and [CII ratings](/cii.html). Vessels using lower-carbon fuels like B30 or B100 would see an improvement in their operational carbon intensity, potentially achieving better CII grades and avoiding penalties. This market shift, even if brief, highlights the strategic importance for companies to not only track their emissions but also actively explore cleaner fuel options to manage their ETS liabilities and improve their standing in [company rankings](/companies.html) for environmental performance.

## Shipping's Unique Position vs. Aviation

The study also draws a compelling distinction between the shipping and aviation sectors regarding renewable fuel competitiveness. While EU carbon regulations briefly made marine biofuels the cheaper compliance option, the researchers calculated that aviation would require carbon prices of approximately $445 per tonne of CO2 equivalent—roughly five times prevailing levels—to close the price gap between SAF and conventional jet fuel. This stark difference underscores the varying maturities of renewable fuel markets, the differing regulatory pressures, and the relative effectiveness of current carbon pricing mechanisms across different transport modalities. It suggests that while shipping is feeling the financial pinch of decarbonization, the policy levers currently in place may be more immediately impactful for the maritime sector in driving fuel transitions.

### What this means for shipping emissions

The brief period where biofuel became the cheapest option for bunkering in Rotterdam, once carbon costs were included, is a stark indicator of the evolving landscape for shipping emissions. While triggered by an external geopolitical shock, this event powerfully demonstrates the growing influence of regulations like the EU ETS and FuelEU Maritime in shaping fuel choices and operational strategies. It validates the argument that robust carbon pricing can, under certain conditions, align economic incentives with environmental goals. Though not a permanent market shift, it highlights the increasing financial risk associated with high-emission fuels and the potential for a more volatile, yet potentially greener, future for the global fleet. Companies must prepare for a future where carbon costs are a fundamental component of fuel procurement, driving strategic decisions towards sustainable alternatives to effectively manage their carbon footprint and ensure long-term viability.
