The global drive for maritime decarbonization extends far beyond the vessels themselves, with port operations representing a crucial, often overlooked, frontier. The recent commissioning of electric prime movers (e-PMs) by Malaysia's Port of Tanjung Pelepas (PTP) marks a significant step, signaling a tangible commitment to reducing Scope 1 and Scope 2 emissions from landside activities. This move, particularly given PTP's status as a major transshipment hub and its partnership with global terminal operator APM Terminals, sets a new benchmark for operational sustainability and reinforces the interconnected nature of the maritime supply chain's decarbonization journey.

## Setting the Stage: PTP's Bold Move

The Port of Tanjung Pelepas (PTP), a joint venture between Malaysia's MMC Group and the Netherlands-based APM Terminals (a part of A.P. Moller-Maersk), has become the first port in Malaysia to deploy an electric prime mover fleet. On August 11, 2026, the initial 21 e-PMs, manufactured locally by Terberg Tractors Malaysia (TTM), officially entered service. This follows a procurement agreement signed in December 2025 for 52 units, with the remaining e-PMs scheduled for delivery by the end of September 2026. This phased rollout underscores a strategic shift towards electrifying terminal equipment, a critical component of PTP's long-term sustainability and expansion goals, which aim to boost the port’s handling capacity to 16.3 million TEUs annually by 2029.

## The Numbers Behind the Electrification

The decision to electrify is underpinned by compelling data from a Proof of Concept conducted between 2023 and 2024. PTP reported that each e-PM generates 4,000 kilograms of CO2 equivalent (kgCO2eq) per month, a substantial 48 percent reduction compared to the 7,600 kgCO2eq/month produced by conventional units. Beyond the environmental benefits, the switch also delivers significant economic advantages, with a reported 57 percent reduction in monthly operational costs per unit. This twin benefit – reduced emissions and lower costs – provides a strong incentive for broader adoption across the industry. PTP has established an ambitious decarbonization pathway, targeting a 45 percent reduction in carbon emissions by 2030 and achieving net-zero by 2050, building on its prior success in electrifying its rubber-tyred gantry crane (RTG) fleet, with 245 e-RTGs now in operation.

## Beyond the Terminal: Implications for Global Shipping

While e-PMs operate on land, their deployment by a port like PTP, a key player in the global transshipment network, has broader implications for shipping companies. The involvement of APM Terminals means that a major player in the global shipping ecosystem, Maersk, is directly connected to this decarbonization effort. As companies like Maersk face increasing pressure to improve their overall sustainability profiles and meet stringent regulatory requirements, their terminal operations contribute significantly to their Scope 2 emissions footprint. Such initiatives influence [company rankings](/companies.html) and demonstrate a commitment to greening the entire logistics chain. Moreover, the local manufacturing of these e-PMs by TTM highlights the potential for regional supply chain development in sustainable maritime technology, fostering a more self-reliant approach to decarbonization.

## A Wider Lens: Linking Port Decarbonization to Ship Compliance

The electrification of port equipment, while not directly impacting individual vessel emissions tracked by [EMSA MRV data](/emissionsdata.html) or influencing a ship's [CII ratings](/cii.html) in transit, is a vital part of the holistic approach required for maritime decarbonization. Green ports create an environment that supports lower-emission vessel operations. Ports that invest in electrification of landside equipment are more likely to also invest in shore power (cold ironing) capabilities. Shore power directly impacts a vessel's emissions at berth, reducing reliance on auxiliary engines and thereby influencing a ship's overall carbon footprint, which is increasingly scrutinized under frameworks like the EU ETS. As the EU ETS extends to cover intra-EU voyages and a percentage of extra-EU voyages, port-side emissions reductions contribute to the broader ecosystem of compliance, potentially lowering the carbon "cost" associated with port calls for vessels subject to these regulations. This progress, detailed in our [news feed](/news.html), showcases the global push for cleaner shipping infrastructure.

## What this means for shipping emissions

PTP's commissioning of electric prime movers signals a clear trend: comprehensive decarbonization in shipping requires tackling emissions across the entire supply chain, from vessel to port. The significant emissions and operational cost reductions achieved demonstrate the viability and economic sense of such investments. For global shipping, this means increasing pressure on ports worldwide to follow suit, offering greener infrastructure and services that support vessels in meeting their own emissions reduction targets and improving their [CII ratings](/cii.html). As regulatory landscapes evolve and carbon pricing mechanisms like the EU ETS expand, ports that proactively decarbonize their operations will become more attractive to shipping lines seeking to reduce their overall carbon footprint and associated costs. This move by PTP is not just about local efficiency; it's a blueprint for integrated maritime decarbonization, setting a precedent for what a truly sustainable shipping future will look like.
