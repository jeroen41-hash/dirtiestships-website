The ongoing debate between British ports and the European Union over alleged carbon charge evasion highlights a critical challenge for global shipping's decarbonisation efforts: the potential for regulatory arbitrage to undermine emissions reduction targets. As the EU Emissions Trading System (ETS) expands its reach to maritime transport, accusations of container traffic diversion to non-EU ports, particularly in the UK, threaten the integrity of carbon pricing mechanisms and necessitate closer scrutiny of shipping routes, capacity changes, and their true environmental impact.

## The Core of the Dispute: ETS Evasion vs. Commercial Growth

At the heart of the contention are findings from Spain’s EU ETS Observatory and Puertos del Estado, which suggest a significant shift in northern European deepsea shipping activity towards the UK. Their research, measured in teu-miles (a metric combining vessel capacity with voyage distance), indicated the UK's share rose from 17.5% in 2023 to 32.1% by July 2025, reaching approximately 36% in Q1 2026. This analysis further estimated that 76% of observed route changes were attributable to the EU ETS, raising concerns that containerlines are restructuring networks to reduce carbon costs.

The British Ports Association (BPA), however, strongly rejects these conclusions, arguing that the increased activity is driven by genuine commercial factors rather than ETS avoidance. Their report attributes around 85% of the UK’s container traffic growth in 2025 to new capacity, particularly the opening of DP World’s £350m fourth berth at London Gateway in late 2024. Government figures show UK ports handled 11% more containers last year, an additional 600,000 units, with London accounting for approximately 500,000 of those. London Gateway itself saw a 52% increase, handling over 3m teu in 2025, following the arrival of Asia-Europe services operated by major players like Maersk and Hapag-Lloyd’s Gemini Cooperation. Such shifts, as detailed in our [company rankings](/companies.html), can significantly reshape market dynamics and emissions profiles.

## Measuring the Impact: Data Discrepancies and Methodologies

The divergence in findings underscores the complexity of accurately attributing changes in shipping patterns to specific regulatory drivers. The BPA critiques the use of "teu-miles" as a measure of diversion, arguing it reflects shipping network activity rather than actual containers handled and can be distorted by longer voyages (e.g., around the Cape of Good Hope) or changes in port call sequences. This highlights a crucial point for platforms like DirtiestShips.com, which relies on robust [emissions data](/charts.html) derived from sources like EMSA MRV: the methodology behind data interpretation is as critical as the data itself. Misinterpretations can lead to misdirected policy interventions.

While the UK's share reportedly surged, continental European ports have not uniformly experienced declines. Rotterdam's container throughput increased 3.1% to 14.2m teu in 2025, Hamburg recorded 7.3% growth to 8.3m teu, and Antwerp-Bruges posted a 0.7% increase, suggesting a more nuanced picture than a wholesale shift away from the EU.

## Brussels' Response and Potential Carbon Cost Implications

In response to perceived evasion, the European Commission has proposed tightening rules. These include potentially excluding certain non-EU container ports within 150 nautical miles of an EU port from the definition of a port of call for emissions accounting. Additionally, the threshold for transhipment activity at other qualifying non-EU ports within 300 nautical miles could be lowered from 65% to 50%.

If adopted, these proposals could significantly impact shipping lines' EU ETS carbon costs, potentially bringing more British terminals within the scope of EU emissions charges. This would directly increase the operational expenses for vessels calling at these ports, influencing strategic decisions about route optimization and vessel efficiency. The UK, for its part, has already introduced its own emissions trading scheme for domestic shipping from July this year, with plans to extend carbon pricing to international voyages from 2028, further complicating the regulatory landscape.

## What this means for shipping emissions

This dispute underscores the critical need for transparent, verifiable data and consistent methodologies in tracking and attributing shipping emissions. While genuine commercial competition and infrastructure investment are natural drivers of port activity, regulatory evasion could compromise the effectiveness of carbon pricing mechanisms like the EU ETS in driving actual emissions reductions. For shipping lines, the evolving regulatory environment necessitates continuous monitoring of operational choices, ensuring compliance, and optimizing vessel performance to manage [CII ratings](/cii.html) and mitigate escalating carbon costs. Ultimately, the goal must be to foster a regulatory framework that encourages deep decarbonisation across the industry, rather than merely shifting emissions or economic activity to avoid charges.
