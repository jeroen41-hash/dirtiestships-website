The global shipping industry faces an increasingly urgent mandate to decarbonize, yet the transition to near-zero emission fuels remains painfully slow. With only an estimated 4% of necessary near-zero fuel production capacity reaching a final investment decision by 2030, and orders for alternative-fuel-capable vessels declining from 45% in 2024 to 37% in 2025, the vast majority of the global fleet will continue its reliance on fossil fuels for years to come. This stark reality intensifies the search for immediate solutions, positioning onboard carbon capture and storage (oCCS) as a potentially critical, albeit complex, pathway to drastically reduce emissions from existing vessels and manage rising carbon costs under regimes like the EU ETS.

### The Decarbonization Gap and oCCS Emergence

The current pace of shipping's decarbonization is clearly insufficient to meet ambitious climate targets. While energy efficiency measures offer some reprieve by reducing fuel consumption, they do not eliminate carbon emissions entirely. This persistent dependence on fossil fuels, as highlighted in the Hellenic Shipping News article, creates a significant "decarbonization gap" that oCCS aims to bridge. By capturing CO₂ from engine exhausts before it enters the atmosphere, oCCS offers a tangible way for vessels that cannot immediately switch to new fuels to improve their environmental performance. This is particularly relevant for ships with considerable remaining trading life, which would otherwise continue to be significant emitters.

### Onboard Carbon Capture: Reality and Performance Metrics

oCCS is rapidly moving beyond pilot projects towards commercial application. Amine-based post-combustion capture has emerged as the most mature marine technology, with initial commercial installations already in operation. Eastern Pacific Shipping's *Pacific Cobalt* notably became the first classed commercial oCCS installation in 2024, achieving LR’s Emission Abatement Carbon Capture & Storage (EACCS) notation. Similarly, Solvang’s *Clipper Eris* is testing a full-scale system targeting a substantial 70% CO₂ capture rate from its main engine.

However, the real-world impact hinges on net emissions reductions. Current full-scale systems capture between 30% and 40% of CO₂, with larger pilots aiming for 70%. Crucially, operating oCCS systems demands energy, increasing fuel consumption by an estimated 15% to 30%. The article details that a 70% gross capture, when offset by a 15% fuel penalty, delivers an estimated tank-to-wake CO₂ reduction of approximately 44%. While waste-heat recovery can mitigate this penalty, operators must scrutinize these figures. For data-driven platforms like DirtiestShips.com, the net reduction, as evidenced by EMSA MRV data, will be the true measure of a vessel's improved [CII ratings](/cii.html) and its actual contribution to decarbonization.

### Strategic Deployment: Which Ships Benefit Most?

The business case for oCCS is not universal but highly dependent on "vessel suitability and operational patterns," as stated by Panos Mitrou of LR. Strongest opportunities exist for vessels with sufficient remaining trading life and space for the capture equipment. Critically, the commercial viability is amplified by operating conditions that align with two key factors:

1.  **Carbon Price Exposure:** Vessels frequently trading on EEA (European Economic Area) routes, such as medium range and chemical tankers, face increasing EU ETS carbon costs. For these ships, reducing emissions directly translates to tangible financial savings by lowering their carbon allowance obligations. This enhances the value proposition of oCCS, making it a viable strategy to manage carbon expenditure.
2.  **Offloading Opportunities:** The limited onboard storage capacity for captured CO₂ means frequent port calls with reception infrastructure are essential. The article highlights that LNG-fuelled vessels and LNG carriers can leverage cold energy from LNG vaporisation to aid CO₂ liquefaction, reducing power requirements. Short-sea and feeder vessels, with their predictable routes and regular port access, also present strong candidates. Without consistent access to offloading facilities, the practical capture rate over a voyage diminishes, undermining the system's effectiveness. This operational detail will be critical for assessing a company's true emissions profile and potential standing in [company rankings](/companies.html).

### Regulatory Hurdles and Infrastructure Bottlenecks

Despite the technological advancements, the widespread success of oCCS faces two decisive external challenges: port reception infrastructure and regulatory recognition. Currently, very few ports are equipped to receive ship-captured liquefied CO₂, and existing facilities often cater to food-grade CO₂, which may not be suitable for exhaust-derived streams. While north-west Europe is making progress with North Sea transport and storage networks, access remains severely limited globally.

On the regulatory front, the EU ETS can recognize captured CO₂ if it reaches qualifying permanent storage or utilization, offering a potential pathway for compliance and cost savings. However, FuelEU Maritime, another key EU regulation, does not yet include oCCS in its greenhouse gas intensity calculations, creating uncertainty for operators. Globally, the IMO is still developing a comprehensive framework covering safety, testing, verification, and carbon accounting. Without clear and consistent regulatory frameworks that fully acknowledge and credit emissions reductions from oCCS, the investment case for shipowners remains challenging. This regulatory ambiguity will affect how oCCS installations ultimately impact vessels' reported emissions data and their standing on platforms like ours.

### What this means for shipping emissions

Onboard carbon capture and storage offers a practical, near-term emissions reduction pathway for suitable vessels operating on conventional or bio/e-fossil fuels, acting as a crucial bridge while the transition to truly zero-emission fuels matures. However, its effectiveness in meaningfully bending the global shipping emissions curve hinges on more than just technological capability. The commercial viability, and thus widespread adoption, is deeply intertwined with the development of comprehensive shore-side infrastructure for CO₂ offloading and permanent storage, alongside robust, globally harmonized regulatory frameworks that unequivocally recognize and credit the emissions reductions achieved. For DirtiestShips.com, tracking the actual, verified emissions data – from both EU MRV and upcoming global schemes – will be paramount to discern which vessels and companies are genuinely leveraging oCCS to reduce their environmental footprint and improve their [CII ratings](/cii.html).
