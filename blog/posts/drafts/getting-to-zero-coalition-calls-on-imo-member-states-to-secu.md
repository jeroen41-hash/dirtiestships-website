The global shipping industry stands at a critical juncture, with discussions at the IMO's ISWG-GHG 22 session underscoring the urgent need for a cohesive, net-zero framework. As the world grapples with escalating CO2 emissions, the absence of an overarching international strategy risks further fragmentation of regulations, potentially hindering the very transition it seeks to accelerate. This latest call from the Getting to Zero Coalition highlights the imperative for the IMO to deliver a robust and investable framework that can steer the maritime sector towards its decarbonization targets effectively.

## The Imperative for a Globally Investable Framework

The Getting to Zero Coalition, following the 22nd session of the International Maritime Organization’s (IMO) Intersessional Working Group on the Reduction of GHG Emissions from Ships (ISWG-GHG 22), has stressed the critical need for an "investable Net-Zero Framework." The emphasis here is on stability and predictability, elements crucial for companies making significant capital commitments in zero-emission fuels and technologies. The Coalition argues that thousands of new vessels will be ordered in the coming years, locking in investment decisions that will determine the emissions trajectory for decades. Without strong incentives and a stable credit market, early movers face undue risk, potentially slowing the transition. This focus on long-term investment decisions is paramount, as the vessels ordered today, running on conventional fuels, will be operating well into the 2040s, impacting our long-term [emissions data](/charts.html).

## Minimizing Fragmentation: Learning from the EU ETS

A key concern raised by the Getting to Zero Coalition is the risk of "regional fragmentation." This directly references the current regulatory landscape, where the European Union's Emissions Trading System (EU ETS) has begun to impose carbon costs on shipping, creating a regional precedent in the absence of a global IMO levy. While the EU ETS aims to drive decarbonization, a patchwork of regional regulations could complicate compliance, distort market competition, and impede the global adoption of zero-emission solutions. The call for a globally inclusive framework seeks to avoid this scenario, advocating for a single, harmonized approach that provides clarity and equal footing for all stakeholders, rather than a system where different regulatory zones lead to varied carbon costs and operational strategies for international fleets.

## Driving Compliance and Rewarding Early Movers

The proposed framework aims for "ambitious and fixed targets for the near and long term," along with "predictable reward mechanisms." This directly impacts how shipping companies will need to adapt and innovate. Under such a system, vessels' emissions performance, as currently tracked by our platform using [EMSA MRV data](/charts.html), would become even more critical. Companies making early investments in zero-emission technologies would likely see significant advantages in their [CII ratings](/cii.html) and overall emissions profiles. A global credit market and reward structure would incentivize these "first movers," providing a financial return on their environmental investments and potentially improving their standing in future [company rankings](/companies.html). Conversely, those delaying action would face increasing pressure and potential penalties, aligning with the "polluter pays" principle seen in emerging carbon markets.

## Equity and the Cost of Transition

The Coalition also highlights the need to address the "concerns of countries that may be disproportionately affected," calling for "targeted support for lower-income countries." Decarbonization requires significant capital investment in new fuels, bunkering infrastructure, and vessel technologies. For developing nations or smaller shipping economies, these costs can be prohibitive without external support. A truly investable framework must incorporate mechanisms to ensure a just and equitable transition, preventing a two-tier system where wealthier nations or larger companies can decarbonize faster due to financial capacity. This support could manifest through dedicated funds, technology transfer programs, or preferential access to financial instruments, all requiring transparent reporting and accountability.

## What this means for shipping emissions

The outcome of IMO discussions on a net-zero framework will fundamentally shape the global shipping industry's decarbonization trajectory. A decisive, high-ambition, and predictable framework is crucial to unlock the necessary investments in zero-emission fuels and technologies, providing confidence to first movers and preventing regulatory fragmentation. Failure to secure such a framework risks slower adoption of green technologies, continued reliance on fossil fuels, and a more challenging path to meet the sector's emissions reduction targets, ultimately delaying the industry's much-needed shift towards a truly sustainable future.
