The global shipping industry is on the cusp of a significant regulatory convergence, with the UK and EU agreeing to link their respective Emissions Trading Schemes (ETS). This landmark agreement, set to become operational from January 1, 2028, marks a critical expansion of carbon pricing for maritime transport, impacting a vast swathe of international voyages and fundamentally reshaping compliance obligations for operators. For a sector under increasing pressure to decarbonize, this move signifies not just a unified approach to carbon markets but a direct and substantial increase in operational costs tied to CO2 emissions, demanding urgent strategic responses from shipping companies across the board.

## A Unified Carbon Market for Shipping's International Emissions

From January 1, 2028, the UK and EU ETS will be linked, allowing allowances issued under either scheme (UKAs or EUAs) to be used for compliance in the other. This integration aims to simplify the compliance burden for shipping companies engaged in international voyages, as they will no longer need to acquire allowances for each jurisdiction separately. Critically, the UK Emissions Trading Scheme will expand its scope to cover 50% of emissions from international voyages by ships of 5,000 GT and above from the same date, mirroring the existing coverage of the EU ETS for voyages between an EU port and a port outside the bloc.

This expansion means a larger proportion of shipping's global emissions will now fall under a carbon pricing mechanism. While the UK ETS has covered 100% of domestic and in-port emissions since July 1, 2026, the inclusion of international voyages signifies a broad step towards comprehensive carbon pricing. Operators should note that the first joint surrender deadline for 2027 emissions will be September 30, 2028. This move leverages data primarily derived from EU MRV (Monitoring, Reporting, Verification) regulations, which forms the bedrock of emissions tracking and compliance for both schemes. As we track [emissions data](/charts.html) at DirtiestShips.com, this expanded regulatory reach means a clearer financial consequence for the carbon footprint of individual vessels and fleets.

## Navigating Carbon Costs: Price Convergence and Compliance

The linkage agreement also signals significant changes to carbon allowance pricing. The UK will remove its auction reserve price when the schemes go live, a logical step as maintaining a price floor in only one part of a linked market would be impractical. This decision has direct implications for shipping companies, as UK allowances have historically traded at a discount compared to EUAs, averaging 21.8% in the first half of 2026, according to Ship & Bunker. The expectation is that prices for UKAs and EUAs will converge, likely upwards, increasing the overall cost of carbon for many operators.

This price convergence and the expanded coverage translate directly into higher operating costs for many shipping companies. While simplified compliance is a benefit, the financial burden of purchasing allowances will increase. This pressure will undoubtedly influence operational decisions, such as route optimization, speed reductions, and investments in energy efficiency, all of which directly impact a vessel's [CII ratings](/cii.html). Companies that have lagged in emissions reduction will see their financial liabilities grow, potentially affecting their standing in [company rankings](/companies.html) not just on emissions but also on profitability.

## Derogations and Decarbonization Incentives

The agreement includes specific derogations designed to address unique operational circumstances. Notably, the temporary 50% surrender derogation for voyages between Northern Ireland and Great Britain will be removed, ensuring all Irish Sea voyages face a 100% obligation. However, the UK will align with the EU's temporary derogation for passenger and ro-pax voyages to islands with fewer than 200,000 people and no road or rail link to the mainland until at least December 31, 2030. Similarly, ice-class ships will receive a 5% surrender derogation until the same date.

Crucially, the agreement provides a clear incentive for the adoption of cleaner fuels: shipping companies will be able to apply a zero-emission factor to biofuels, Renewable Fuels of Non-Biological Origin (RFNBOs), and recycled carbon fuels that meet the Renewable Energy Directive's criteria. This move is a critical signal for investment in these alternative fuels, offering a pathway to avoid carbon costs entirely for compliant fuel use. While the UK pledges to review its policies should a global IMO measure be adopted to avoid double charging, the sentiment from industry figures, as heard at the IBIA Annual Convention, is that the EU is unlikely to abandon its ETS, regardless of IMO outcomes.

## The Industry's Call: Revenue Allocation and Decarbonization Funding

The UK Chamber of Shipping has been vocal, calling for ETS revenues to be reinvested into shipping's decarbonization efforts and for a full economic impact assessment before the UK ETS was extended internationally. Their concern is that these schemes, while increasing compliance costs, might not deliver additional emissions reductions if the revenues are not strategically directed. The Department for Energy Security and Net Zero's explainer, however, makes no mention of specific plans for revenue allocation towards maritime decarbonization.

This lack of clarity on revenue reinvestment is a point of contention and a critical missed opportunity. While carbon pricing provides a financial incentive to reduce emissions, direct support for technological innovation and infrastructure development for alternative fuels is equally vital. Without such support, the risk remains that the ETS becomes primarily a tax burden rather than a transformative mechanism, potentially hindering the industry's ability to achieve ambitious emissions targets and improve [CII ratings](/cii.html) across the fleet.

## What this means for shipping emissions

The linkage of the UK and EU ETS represents a powerful, unified expansion of carbon pricing on international shipping, directly increasing the financial impetus for emissions reduction. For operators, this means higher compliance costs, simplified but more stringent regulatory oversight, and a clear incentive to adopt zero-emission fuels. While the regulatory framework is solidifying, the critical question of how the substantial revenues generated will be utilized remains largely unanswered. For genuine progress towards net-zero, these funds must actively support decarbonization, ensuring that this significant regulatory step translates into measurable and sustained reductions in shipping's environmental footprint, which we will continue to track closely on our [news feed](/news.html).
