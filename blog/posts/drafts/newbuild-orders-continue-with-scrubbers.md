The global shipping industry's decarbonization journey faces a revealing paradox: despite escalating pressure for carbon neutrality, newbuild orders continue to heavily favour scrubber technology, enabling ships to burn cheaper, higher-carbon heavy fuel oil (HFO) for decades to come. This trend, highlighted by recent industry analysis, signals a strategic pivot by owners and charterers towards fuel flexibility rather than outright decarbonization in the short to medium term, complicating the trajectory of global shipping emissions and the efficacy of regulatory frameworks like the EU ETS and IMO's CII.

## The Scrubber Surge in Newbuild Orders

The data reveals a stark commitment to Exhaust Gas Cleaning Systems (EGCS), commonly known as scrubbers, for vessels slated for delivery in the latter half of this decade. Greek owners are leading this charge, with prominent examples including:
*   **Navios Maritime Partners** investing $482 million in four scrubber-fitted VLCC newbuildings, with options for four more, due in 2028.
*   **TOP Ships** acquiring three scrubber-fitted, eco-design MR product tanker newbuildings for 2029 delivery.
*   **Aegean Shipping Management** committing to two scrubber-fitted VLCCs and two LR2/Aframax tankers.
*   In dry bulk, **Seanergy Maritime Holdings** expanded its renewal programme to include seven scrubber-fitted capesize newbuildings for 2029.
*   **Thenamaris** is linked to an order for four scrubber-fitted MR2 product tankers.

This isn't isolated to a few players. Lloyd's List analysis of Clarksons Research data shows scrubbers fitted or planned on 92% of VLCCs on order, significantly up from 66% of the existing fleet. Similar patterns are evident for other ship types: 66% of suezmax newbuilds (vs. 40% existing), 74% of capesize newbuilds (vs. 57% existing), and 46% of panamax newbuilds (vs. 19% existing). These vessels are designed to trade through the 2030s and beyond, locking in a preference for HFO.

## Fuel Flexibility Over Decarbonization?

The primary driver behind this scrubber investment is "fuel flexibility." Owners and charterers are hedging against the uncertainty of future compliant marine fuel availability and pricing in 2035. By installing scrubbers, newbuilds retain the option to burn HFO, which is generally cheaper than very low sulphur fuel oil (VLSFO) or marine gas oil (MGO). This allows vessels to comply with MARPOL Annex VI sulphur limits while adjusting to market and fuel availability fluctuations.

While some newbuilds are also incorporating basic infrastructure for alternative fuels like methanol or ammonia, the simultaneous commitment to scrubbers suggests a strategic preference for bridging the transition with conventional, albeit high-carbon, fuels. The article notes that charterers are actively seeking these scrubber-fitted vessels years in advance, indicating strong commercial demand and a calculated bet on the economics of HFO. This strategy directly impacts our tracking of [company emissions rankings](/companies.html), as these fleets will continue to emit significant CO2.

## Scrubber Emissions and Lifecycle Claims

The environmental debate surrounding scrubbers traditionally focuses on discharge water. The article highlights a body of research, including over 30 studies collated by the Clean Shipping Alliance, suggesting "low or negligible environmental risk" from EGCS discharge water, finding it within relevant water-quality criteria.

Crucially for DirtiestShips.com, the article also references lifecycle research challenging the assumption that low-sulphur fuel is automatically the lower-impact option. These studies suggest that "HFO with EGCS can match or outperform low-sulphur alternatives across a range of environmental impact categories, including climate impact (CO2)," once fuel production, refining, transport, and onboard use are considered. This claim warrants rigorous scrutiny through EU MRV data, as scrubbers do not directly reduce a ship's CO2 emissions but enable the use of a fuel that typically has a higher carbon content than alternatives on a per-tonne basis. The ultimate impact on a vessel's [CII ratings](/cii.html) and overall contribution to global warming remains a critical area of analysis for us.

## Fragmented Regulatory Landscape and ETS Implications

Despite claims of environmental benignity, the regulatory landscape for scrubber discharge remains fragmented. The IMO's MEPC.1/Circ.899 Guidelines provide a framework for risk assessment, yet regional restrictions are proliferating. The OSPAR Decision 2025/01, for instance, mandates national prohibitions on open-loop EGCS discharge in internal waters and port areas by July 2027, extending to closed-loop discharge by July 2029, though implementation delays are expected. This patchwork of rules adds operational complexity and potentially limits the flexibility scrubbers are intended to provide.

For operators under the EU Emissions Trading System (ETS), the choice of HFO via scrubbers does not alleviate carbon costs. Vessels burning HFO will still incur [EU ETS carbon costs](/ets.html) based on their CO2 emissions. While the lower cost of HFO might offset some of these ETS expenses, it underscores that scrubbers are a sulphur compliance solution, not a decarbonization tool. This continued reliance on fossil fuels, albeit with sulphur abatement, means our [emissions data](/charts.html) will reflect persistent CO2 output from these new fleets well into the next decade.

## What this means for shipping emissions

The continued, aggressive investment in scrubber technology for newbuilds is a clear signal that the shipping industry is prioritizing conventional fuel flexibility and cost efficiency for the foreseeable future. While it addresses sulphur compliance, it poses a significant challenge to global decarbonization efforts. DirtiestShips.com will continue to monitor these trends through rigorous analysis of EU MRV data, assessing the actual CO2 footprint of these fleets. This strategic choice by owners and charterers means a protracted reliance on fossil fuels, complicating the pathway to zero emissions and placing increased pressure on the effectiveness of carbon pricing mechanisms like the EU ETS to drive genuine decarbonization, rather than simply offsetting operational costs.
