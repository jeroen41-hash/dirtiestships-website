The global shipping industry faces unprecedented pressure to decarbonize, driven by escalating climate concerns and a tightening regulatory landscape from both the IMO and the European Union. Amidst this imperative, a new contender for marine fuel is gaining significant traction: ethanol. Backed by major players like Shandong Shipping and Vale, ethanol is emerging as a technically viable and potentially cost-effective solution capable of delivering immediate, substantial reductions in greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, marking a critical step for shipowners grappling with their [CII ratings](/cii.html) and burgeoning EU ETS costs.

## Ethanol's Promising Emissions Profile and IMO Endorsement

The appeal of ethanol as a marine fuel hinges significantly on its favorable emissions profile. According to the analysis by WinGD's Andrea Lazzaro in *splash247.com*, specific forms of ethanol, such as Brazilian 'second growth' corn ethanol, boast a remarkably low emissions factor of 20.8gCO2eq/MJ. This figure is more than four times lower than conventional VLSFO, representing a monumental leap towards the industry's net-zero aspirations. A crucial milestone in ethanol's marine fuel journey was its recent approval by the IMO’s Marine Environmental Protection Committee (MEPC) for inclusion in its Lifecycle Analysis (LCA) Guidelines for Marine Fuels. This is the first biofuel to achieve such recognition, providing a clear pathway for vessels utilizing it to claim significant emissions reductions under future IMO regimes. The article highlights that global mining giant Vale, known for its extensive dry bulk chartering, is already backing the first ethanol-fuelled two-stroke engines for Newcastlemax vessels, indicating a strong commercial belief in its potential for specific trade routes, particularly those between Brazil and China.

## The EU Regulatory Divide: ETS Gains, FuelEU Maritime Hurdles

While the emissions benefits of ethanol are clear, its treatment under European Union regulations presents a nuanced picture. For vessels exposed to the EU Emissions Trading System (ETS), the adoption of ethanol offers a straightforward advantage. By significantly reducing reported CO2 emissions, ships using ethanol will directly lower their ETS carbon costs, improving their operational economics and potentially enhancing their standing in future [company rankings](/companies.html) based on emissions intensity. This immediate benefit is a powerful incentive for operators.

However, the situation is more complex under FuelEU Maritime. The regulation currently does not credit emissions reductions from biofuels derived from food crops or their byproducts. This means that while a vessel might see its ETS costs drop, it would not receive similar recognition for FuelEU Maritime compliance, which aims to drive the adoption of truly sustainable, non-food-based alternative fuels. This regulatory disconnect, affecting approximately 20% of the global merchant fleet calling at European Economic Area ports, creates a significant challenge. Suppliers are actively petitioning against this exclusion, arguing that modern ethanol production does not compromise food security, but until resolution, ship operators must navigate this regulatory duality.

## Commercial Viability and Strategic Blending for Decarbonization

Beyond its emissions benefits, ethanol also offers practical advantages in terms of availability and cost. Large-scale production, particularly in regions like Brazil and the US, means it is already widely available. Crucially, its price is reported to be nearly competitive with conventional marine fuels, a position that strengthens considerably once carbon pricing mechanisms take full effect. This cost-effectiveness makes ethanol an attractive, immediately deployable option for operators whose routes intersect these production hubs.

Furthermore, ethanol can play a strategic role in broader decarbonization plans, particularly in conjunction with green methanol. As green methanol, a near-zero emissions fuel, remains scarce and expensive, ethanol offers a flexible interim solution. WinGD's engines are compatible with both alcohol fuels, allowing for blends or a phased transition. Operators aiming for green methanol but facing supply or cost constraints can utilize ethanol to achieve steep emissions reductions now, with the ability to ramp up green methanol usage as it becomes more available and affordable. This flexible technology platform is exemplified by Maersk's Terma Maersk, whose methanol-fuelled engine is inherently adaptable to ethanol with minimal modifications, providing a bridge to future zero-emission targets.

## What this means for shipping emissions

The emergence of ethanol as a viable marine fuel represents a significant development in the shipping industry's decarbonization journey. Its low lifecycle emissions, coupled with existing production infrastructure and competitive pricing, position it as a potent tool for immediate GHG reductions. While its pathway is smoothed by IMO LCA approval and EU ETS benefits, the current exclusion under FuelEU Maritime highlights ongoing regulatory complexities that require resolution for wider adoption in European trades. Ultimately, ethanol offers a tangible, technologically ready solution for shipowners seeking to improve their environmental performance, manage carbon costs, and progress towards net-zero goals, demanding continuous monitoring through accurate [emissions data](/charts.html) reported via frameworks like EMSA MRV. The strategic integration of ethanol, potentially as a blend with green methanol, will be a key component in how companies manage their transition to cleaner operations, influencing their standing in future [company rankings](/companies.html) and shaping the overall emissions trajectory of the global fleet.
