The global shipping industry faces a pivotal moment in its decarbonization journey, with the International Maritime Organization (IMO) grappling to finalize a global net-zero framework. A new proposal from Brazil, submitted ahead of the September 2026 intersessional working group meeting (ISWG-GHG 22), injects a critical new perspective into these negotiations. This submission, detailed by hellenicshippingnews.com, aims to bridge disagreements while preserving the core compliance architecture, potentially reshaping how shipping companies manage their carbon footprint, navigate future regulations, and incur associated costs, directly impacting the trajectory of global maritime emissions.

## Brazil's Flexible Compliance Vision: Learning from FuelEU

At its core, Brazil's proposal retains the global GHG Fuel Intensity standard, measured on a well-to-wake basis and expressed in gCO₂eq/MJ, benchmarked against a 2008 average of 93.3 gCO₂eq/MJ. The framework features two distinct GHG Intensity thresholds: a less stringent Base Target and a more demanding Direct Compliance Target. Ships can achieve compliance either directly through low-carbon fuels or advanced technologies, or by balancing any deficit with transferable "Surplus Units" generated by over-complying vessels, or "Remedial Units" obtained via contributions to an IMO mechanism.

This flexible compliance system bears striking resemblance to market-based mechanisms already taking root, such as FuelEU Maritime. The article highlights that FuelEU has demonstrated the effectiveness of surplus markets in facilitating compliance but also exposed a challenge: the cheapest compliance pathway isn't always the one that aligns with long-term decarbonization goals. For operators tracked on DirtiestShips.com, understanding these market dynamics will be crucial. The decisions on fuel pathways not only affect a vessel's [CII ratings](/cii.html) but also its overall operational costs and its standing in [company rankings](/companies.html) for emissions efficiency.

## A Phased Introduction to Stricter Targets

A key aspect of Brazil's submission is its proposed adjustment to the reduction trajectory. Recognizing concerns over fuel availability and supply chain maturity, the proposal shifts implementation dates by one year. Notably, it introduces a transitional arrangement for 2029 and 2030, during which the Base Target and Direct Compliance Target would be identical. This "softer introduction" aims to reduce early revenue collection and allow time for institutional systems to mature.

However, this initial leniency is followed by a sharply accelerating trajectory. From 2031 onward, the two targets diverge significantly, re-establishing the full two-tier architecture. Furthermore, Brazil's proposal doesn't dilute long-term ambition; it suggests replacing the previously envisaged 65% Base Target reduction in 2040 with an even more ambitious 70% reduction in 2041. This nuanced approach signals a political willingness to accommodate short-term challenges without compromising long-term climate objectives, demanding significant strategic planning from shipping companies regarding fleet upgrades and fuel transitions. The phased introduction echoes the gradual increase in stringency seen in the EU ETS over time, providing valuable insights into how shipping might adapt.

## Carbon Costs and the Evolution of Compliance

Politically, Brazil's proposal also seeks to reframe the "economic element." By removing explicit references to "GHG emissions pricing," the system is presented not as a general levy but as an alternative compliance route. Companies would weigh the cost of adopting new fuels or technologies against the cost of acquiring the necessary Surplus or Remedial Units to cover their deficit. This distinction, while subtle, is significant, moving away from a punitive tax model towards a more incentive-driven, market-based approach.

The creation of transferable Surplus Units effectively turns over-compliance into a tradable asset. This parallels the functioning of carbon markets like the EU ETS, where emission allowances are bought and sold. As companies navigate these new compliance markets, the price of these units will become a direct carbon cost, influencing investment decisions in new vessels, retrofits, and alternative fuels. Operators will need to carefully track their performance, relying on robust data similar to the EMSA MRV data used in the EU, to optimize their compliance strategy and minimize their financial exposure.

## What this means for shipping emissions

Brazil's IMO proposal represents a critical attempt to break the deadlock in global shipping decarbonization. By offering a flexible compliance system with a staged, yet ultimately ambitious, reduction trajectory, it provides a potential pathway forward for the IMO Net-Zero Framework. For the shipping industry, this proposal underscores an undeniable trend: carbon costs are becoming an integrated part of operational expenses, whether through direct compliance, tradable units, or regional schemes like the EU ETS. Companies must prepare for a future where robust emissions monitoring, strategic fuel choices, and active participation in compliance markets will dictate not only their regulatory standing but also their financial viability. The push for real-world emissions reductions, supported by transparent data, remains paramount.
