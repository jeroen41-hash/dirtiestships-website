A staggering US$200 billion has been committed by the shipping industry to a rapidly expanding fleet of dual-fuel container ships and vehicle carriers, signalling a major pivot towards lower-emission propulsion. However, this monumental investment, while promising for global decarbonization efforts, faces a critical hurdle: the lack of commercially competitive alternative fuels and a coherent global regulatory framework. Without these, the potential for emissions reduction, as tracked by platforms like DirtiestShips.com using EU MRV data and CII ratings, remains largely theoretical, risking increased carbon costs under schemes like the EU ETS.

## The Surge in Dual-Fuel Vessel Orders

According to recent World Shipping Council (WSC) data, the global dual-fuel fleet for container ships and vehicle carriers has reached impressive numbers. As of September 2026, 503 such vessels are operational, marking a significant 41% increase from 358 ships just a year prior. An additional 849 ships are currently on order, bringing the total delivered or committed to 1,352 vessels. This represents a colossal investment exceeding US$200 billion. These ships are designed with the flexibility to run on conventional marine fuels or alternative, lower-emission options, positioning them as key players in preparing the fleet for a cleaner future. The WSC data highlights the dominance of alternative-fuel-capable ships in these segments, accounting for 65% of container ship orders and a striking 97% of vehicle carrier orders by deadweight tonnage, a stark contrast to the 23% across the rest of the global shipping fleet. This commitment underscores a clear industry intention to improve future [CII ratings](/cii.html) and meet evolving environmental standards.

## The Critical Alternative Fuel Dilemma

Despite the impressive investment in dual-fuel technology, the core challenge, as reiterated by WSC president and CEO Joe Kramek, lies in the availability of "commercially competitive alternative fuels." These vessels are built with the *capability* to run on cleaner fuels, but their actual emissions performance hinges entirely on the fuel choices made by operators. If these ships are compelled to predominantly use conventional marine fuels due to the scarcity or high cost of alternatives, their decarbonization potential will not be realized. This directly impacts their operational environmental footprint, leading to higher actual CO2 emissions. For companies operating in or through European waters, this means a greater exposure to [EU ETS carbon costs](https://dirtiestships.com/ets.html), eroding the financial benefits of their advanced fleet. Our analysis of [emissions data](/charts.html) derived from EU MRV will be crucial in distinguishing between *potential* and *actual* emissions reductions from this new fleet.

## The Imperative for Global Regulatory Certainty

The WSC has intensified its call for a global decarbonization framework under the International Maritime Organization (IMO). The current uncertainty surrounding global greenhouse gas regulation is palpable; while dual-fuel orders surge, conventional-fuelled ship orders have also increased, a testament to the lack of clear, consistent rules. "Ships ordered today will be operating for decades," Kramek stated, emphasizing that long-term investment decisions require regulatory predictability. Without a global measure, the shipping industry faces a growing "patchwork of regional emissions regulations," adding complexity and cost to international trade. Such fragmentation, with the EU ETS as a prime example, can create uneven playing fields and impede the widespread adoption of cleaner fuels and technologies. A global framework is seen as essential to bridge the "cost gap" between conventional and renewable fuels, providing the confidence needed for both shipping companies and fuel producers to invest in necessary infrastructure and supply chains.

## What this means for shipping emissions

The $200 billion investment in dual-fuel ships marks a pivotal moment for the shipping industry's decarbonization journey, demonstrating a clear commitment from key sectors. However, the actual reduction in shipping emissions hinges entirely on the immediate availability of commercially viable alternative fuels and the establishment of a robust, global regulatory framework from the IMO. Without these critical components, this massive fleet risks operating predominantly on conventional fuels, thereby failing to deliver its promised environmental benefits and leaving operators exposed to escalating regional carbon costs.
