The global shipping industry stands at a critical juncture, facing increasing pressure to decarbonise while navigating complex regulatory landscapes. A recent report from Ship & Bunker highlights a stark paradox: pure biodiesel (B100) is now economically more attractive than conventional gasoil in key hubs like ARA, offering substantial savings even before factoring in carbon compliance costs. Yet, its uptake is significantly hampered, not by supply or price, but by a crucial technical hurdle – engine manufacturer approvals and warranty concerns. This impasse threatens to slow immediate emissions reductions and complicate the industry's path to meeting stringent environmental targets.

### The Economic Case for Biofuels: A Missed Opportunity

According to KPI OceanConnect's Jesper Sørensen, B100 in the ARA region has been trading around $150/mt below gasoil, thanks to subsidies, as of mid-October. This initial price advantage is just the beginning. When the financial implications of the EU Emissions Trading System (EU ETS) and FuelEU Maritime are considered, the economic argument for gasoil "literally disappears." Sørensen estimates that the EU ETS allowance saving alone, based on a conversion factor of approximately 3.2 tonnes of CO2 per tonne of fuel, is worth around $320/mt. This means switching to B100 could net total savings upwards of $470/mt in ARA, before accounting for FuelEU Maritime benefits.

This significant cost advantage should be a major driver for bunker buyers, especially with conventional fuel prices recently rising amidst geopolitical tensions while biofuel prices have remained more stable. The economics, globally, "have spoken in favour of using biofuels," Sørensen notes. However, despite this clear financial incentive, demand for these greener alternatives is not surging as one might expect.

### Engine Warranties: The Unseen Barrier to Adoption

The primary roadblock, as identified by KPI OceanConnect, is the reluctance of engine manufacturers to provide approvals that protect existing warranties for ships using B100. Shipowners, naturally, are hesitant to risk their valuable assets by using fuels not explicitly sanctioned by manufacturers. Sørensen expresses surprise that the industry is still at the "proof of concept" stage when it comes to biofuels, stressing that while feedstock discussions continue, the operational viability should be less contentious.

This situation creates a significant dilemma. Owners are caught between the economic and environmental imperative to adopt low-carbon fuels and the need to safeguard their investments. While biofuel availability is no longer a constraint, with over 220 ports now offering it, the lack of widespread manufacturer backing means that many vessels, potentially even those with high [CII ratings](/cii.html), are unable to capitalise on immediate opportunities to improve their environmental performance and reduce operational costs.

### Navigating FuelEU Maritime's Early Cycles

The article also sheds light on a recent dip in biofuel volumes, which Sørensen attributes not to a loss of interest, but to the mechanics of FuelEU Maritime's inaugural compliance cycle. Owners who used biofuel to cover their 2025 obligations had burned it by December, and attention then shifted to the complex paperwork for the first compliance year, running up to the April deadline. Some owners, initially over-complying, have also scaled back purchases.

However, as the "crunch time" for 2026 compliance approaches, an uptick in demand is anticipated. FuelEU Maritime sets increasingly stringent GHG intensity targets for ships, compelling owners to incorporate greener fuels. The interplay between this regulatory pressure and the economic attractiveness of biofuels, once technical approvals are in place, will be critical. DirtiestShips.com tracks these shifts closely, providing granular [emissions data](/charts.html) derived from EU MRV to illustrate compliance trends.

### Implications for CII Ratings and Company Emissions

The slow adoption of economically viable biofuels due to warranty issues has direct consequences for shipping companies' environmental performance metrics. Vessels unable to switch to B100 will continue to burn higher-carbon fuels, directly impacting their annual carbon intensity indicator (CII) ratings. A suboptimal CII rating can lead to commercial disadvantages, reputational damage, and the need for costly corrective actions.

From the perspective of [company rankings](/companies.html), a delay in biofuel uptake means that fleets reliant on conventional fuels will continue to report higher CO2 emissions via their EU MRV data. This not only increases their carbon footprint but also translates into higher EU ETS compliance costs. Companies actively exploring and securing approvals for biofuels will likely see their emissions profiles improve faster, positioning them more favourably in sustainability rankings. The current impasse means that even highly motivated companies might find themselves unable to implement immediate emissions reductions, potentially skewing performance comparisons and delaying the industry's collective decarbonisation progress.

### What this means for shipping emissions

The current situation presents a significant hurdle for the rapid decarbonisation of the shipping industry. While an economically compelling, readily available low-carbon fuel exists, a critical technical and commercial barrier prevents its widespread adoption. This directly translates to missed opportunities for immediate CO2 emission reductions, higher operational costs for shipowners due to ETS exposure, and a slower improvement in vessel [CII ratings](/cii.html). Addressing the engine warranty issue, through industry-wide collaboration between manufacturers, owners, and perhaps even regulators, is paramount to unlocking the full potential of biofuels and accelerating the maritime sector's transition towards a more sustainable future. Until then, the industry risks leaving substantial emissions reductions and financial savings on the table.
