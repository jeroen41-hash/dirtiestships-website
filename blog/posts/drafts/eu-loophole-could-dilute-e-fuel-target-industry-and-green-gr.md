The European Union’s ambitious drive to decarbonize shipping faces a significant hurdle as a "regulatory paradox" threatens to dilute the impact of future synthetic e-fuel mandates. More than 20 organizations, including the SASHA Coalition and Transport & Environment (T&E), have urged EU lawmakers to close a loophole that could undermine the development and adoption of genuine synthetic marine fuels, potentially slowing down the industry’s crucial transition away from fossil fuels and impacting the global trajectory of CO2 emissions. This development casts a shadow over the clear implementation of FuelEU Maritime targets and calls into question the long-term certainty needed for massive investments in green fuels.

## The Synthetic Fuel Semantic Slide

At the heart of the issue lies Delegated Regulation (EU) 2023/1185, which currently fails to clearly exclude renewable hydrogen used as an intermediate input in biofuel production from counting towards the synthetic fuel share. Specifically, organizations highlight that electrolytic hydrogen used in the hydrotreatment of biofuels, such as hydrotreated vegetable oil (HVO), could be misconstrued as contributing to the synthetic fuel target. This blurs the crucial distinction between using green hydrogen as a processing aid for existing biofuels and deploying it as a feedstock to produce dedicated synthetic fuels like e-methanol and e-ammonia, which require entirely new production facilities. As the joint letter warns, this could allow "existing biofuel refiners to claim they are delivering e-fuels, without building new synthetic fuel plants," an accounting method that the signatories say would carry over from aviation to shipping.

## Undermining Investment in True E-Fuels

This regulatory ambiguity poses a direct threat to the nascent e-fuel industry. FuelEU Maritime includes a potential 2% sub-target for synthetic e-fuels from January 1, 2034, contingent on market conditions. However, if the current accounting approach remains unchanged, biofuels processed with green hydrogen could satisfy this future sub-target without stimulating the necessary investment in genuine synthetic fuel production.

T&E points out that Europe currently has a pipeline of 69 e-fuel projects that could supply the shipping sector, yet only six are operational. This loophole risks deterring the long-term offtake agreements crucial for financing these new projects and reaching final investment decisions. Without clear incentives, capital may not flow into the dedicated infrastructure needed for large-scale e-methanol or e-ammonia production, hindering the industry's ability to truly decarbonize and meet increasingly stringent emissions targets, impacting overall [emissions data](/charts.html). The signatories argue this introduces "regulatory instability, signaling to market actors that EU rules can be diluted retroactively and opening a Pandora’s box that permanently erodes trust in the long-term predictability of the EU regulatory framework."

## Implications for Shipping Companies and Carbon Costs

For shipping companies navigating the complex regulatory landscape, this potential loophole introduces significant uncertainty. While a short-term "easier" path to compliance might seem appealing, it risks delaying the inevitable need for genuine e-fuels. This delay could lead to higher prices and scarcity once robust mandates are enforced, directly impacting operational costs and the ability to manage [EU ETS carbon costs](/news.html).

Companies striving to improve their [CII ratings](/cii.html) and meet FuelEU Maritime’s progressively tighter greenhouse gas intensity targets rely on a clear regulatory framework to inform their fleet modernization and fuel procurement strategies. An ambiguous definition of "e-fuels" could complicate transparency in [company rankings](/companies.html) and make it harder to genuinely compare efforts towards decarbonization. Moreover, stifling domestic e-fuel production capabilities could force the EU to import these critical fuels in the future, increasing supply chain risks and costs.

The signatories propose a solution: applying the same clear accounting approach adopted in Delegated Regulation (EU) 2025/2359 for low-carbon fuels, which explicitly excludes renewable fuels of non-biological origin used as intermediate products in conventional fuel or biofuel production when calculating the low-carbon share. This would ensure that only truly synthetic fuels contribute to the e-fuel sub-target, providing the necessary clarity and incentive for genuine decarbonization.

## What this means for shipping emissions

This regulatory paradox, if left unaddressed, risks diluting the effectiveness of the EU's pioneering decarbonization efforts for shipping. By potentially allowing biofuels processed with green hydrogen to count as synthetic e-fuels, it could delay the critical investment and development needed for scalable, truly green alternative fuels. The consequence is a slower transition away from fossil fuels, persistent high CO2 emissions from the global fleet, and a potential failure to meet the ambitious climate targets set for the sector. Clarity and consistency in regulation are paramount to driving actual emissions reductions and fostering trust in the market for sustainable shipping.
