The global shipping industry stands at a critical juncture, with regulatory decisions poised to dramatically reshape its decarbonization trajectory. A new forecast from the International Energy Agency (IEA) underscores the pivotal role of international policy, predicting a significant, albeit limited, surge in marine biodiesel demand without a global framework, but an explosion of demand should the IMO adopt its Net-Zero Framework. This highlights the urgent need for clarity and robust regulation to drive meaningful emissions reductions and avoid disparate regional efforts, directly impacting operational costs, carbon intensity, and ultimately, the "dirtiness" of the global fleet, as tracked by our [emissions data](/charts.html).

### The Slow Burn: Biodiesel's Current Trajectory Without Global Mandates

The IEA projects global marine biodiesel demand to nearly double from just under 1 billion litres/year (around 890,000 metric tonnes/year) in 2025 to approximately 2 billion litres/year (1.78 million metric tonnes/year) by 2031, assuming the International Maritime Organization (IMO) does not adopt its Net-Zero Framework. Despite this growth, biodiesel would constitute a mere 0.6% of total marine fuel use by 2031, remaining the primary renewable energy source in shipping but accounting for only 1% of the overall growth in renewable energy across all transport sectors.

This modest increase is largely driven by Europe, which is expected to account for four-fifths of the growth due to the implementation of FuelEU Maritime. This regulation, effective since the start of 2025, mandates ships above 5,000 GT calling at EU ports to reduce the GHG intensity of their energy use. The required cuts are 6% by 2030 and 14.5% by 2035, measured well-to-wake, encompassing emissions from fuel production, distribution, and combustion. It covers all energy used within EU waters and half for voyages into or out of the bloc. Other key regions contributing to this growth include Singapore and China, primarily servicing vessels trading with Europe. The data collected under EMSA's MRV regulations will be crucial for monitoring compliance with FuelEU Maritime and tracking the actual impact of biodiesel adoption.

### IMO's Net-Zero Framework: A Game Changer for Fuel Demand and Carbon Costs

The landscape changes dramatically should the IMO adopt its Net-Zero Framework, with a vote due in December. Under this scenario, the IEA forecasts marine biodiesel use to skyrocket to over 12 billion litres/year (more than 10.68 million metric tonnes/year) by 2031, representing 4% of total marine fuel demand. This framework would cover roughly 97% of the world's merchant fleet by tonnage and introduce two tiers of targets for cutting ships' fuel GHG intensity.

Crucially, the framework proposes a carbon pricing mechanism. Ships exceeding the tougher target of a 21% GHG intensity cut would face a penalty of $100/mtCO2e, while those failing to meet the base target of an 8% cut would incur a steeper penalty of $380/mtCO2e. These significant carbon costs are designed to accelerate decarbonization efforts across the global fleet. The IEA anticipates that most ships could meet the 8% target by blending lower-emission fuels like marine biodiesel, liquefied natural gas (LNG), or liquefied biomethane (LBM). However, the 21% target would prove considerably harder to achieve, given the scarcity and high cost of fuels such as bio-methanol, e-methanol, and ammonia.

### Implications for Fleet Performance and CII Ratings

The increased adoption of marine biodiesel, driven by both FuelEU Maritime and a potential IMO framework, will directly influence the operational carbon intensity of individual vessels. Ships that leverage biodiesel blends to meet regulatory requirements will likely see improvements in their Carbon Intensity Indicator (CII) ratings. For companies committed to reducing their environmental footprint, this offers a tangible pathway to improve fleet performance from 'D' or 'E' ratings to more favorable 'C' or 'B' categories, thereby enhancing their market competitiveness and reputation.

However, the IEA report highlights a critical limitation: marine biodiesel supply, while sufficient for the 8% IMO target, would be constrained by competing demand from road transport, making it challenging for widespread compliance with the tougher 21% target. This underscores that while biodiesel is a vital near-term solution, it is not a silver bullet for shipping's deep decarbonization. Companies will need to explore a portfolio of solutions, including energy efficiency measures and other alternative fuels, to navigate the evolving regulatory landscape. ### The Carbon Cost Landscape

The potential carbon pricing under the IMO Net-Zero Framework introduces a new layer of financial risk and opportunity for ship operators, mirroring the impact of the existing EU Emissions Trading System (EU ETS). The proposed IMO penalties, ranging from $100 to $380 per tonne of CO2 equivalent, are designed to create a strong economic incentive for cleaner operations. These figures are substantial, comparable to or even exceeding current EU ETS carbon prices, and signify a global shift towards monetizing carbon emissions from shipping.

The integration of such costs into voyage planning and fuel procurement will become imperative. Operators facing higher carbon liabilities will find "dirtier" voyages increasingly expensive, pushing them towards more sustainable fuel choices and operational efficiencies. This alignment of financial incentives with environmental goals is expected to accelerate investment in green technologies and fuels, driving down overall emissions. The EU ETS, in particular, has already demonstrated its effectiveness in Europe by spurring demand for lower-carbon fuels like biodiesel to avoid substantial compliance costs.

### What this means for shipping emissions

The IEA's forecast paints a clear picture: regulatory certainty, particularly from the IMO, is the single most powerful catalyst for accelerating shipping's decarbonization. While FuelEU Maritime provides a regional push for biodiesel adoption and near-term emission cuts, a global framework with robust carbon pricing mechanisms is essential to scale up the demand for alternative fuels to truly impactful levels. Without it, biodiesel will remain a niche fuel, leaving the vast majority of shipping emissions unaddressed. The industry must prepare for a future where carbon costs are a significant operational factor, and compliance with increasingly stringent GHG intensity targets, as tracked by [emissions data](/charts.html) and [news feed](/news.html), dictates commercial viability. The pathway to cleaner shipping hinges on global collaboration and clear, enforceable policies that incentivize the shift away from fossil fuels.
