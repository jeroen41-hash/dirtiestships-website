The announcement that Amazónica LNG, a Colombian company, has joined SEA-LNG signifies a growing commitment to liquefied natural gas (LNG) as a marine fuel in Latin America. While proponents hail LNG as an "immediate route to decarbonisation," DirtiestShips.com views such developments through the lens of verifiable emissions data, challenging the narrative with a focus on real-world CO2 equivalent (CO2e) impact and the critical issue of methane slip, especially as global shipping faces increasingly stringent regulations like the EU ETS and CII ratings. This expansion of LNG bunkering infrastructure warrants a closer examination of its actual environmental implications.

## LNG Bunkering Expands in Colombia: A "Transition" or a Detour?

Amazónica LNG, a joint venture developing bunkering-ready infrastructure on Colombia’s Caribbean coast, primarily operates the Palermo LNG terminal near Barranquilla. This strategic location aims to serve the bustling ports of Cartagena and Barranquilla, which collectively represent a significant maritime hub. In 2025 alone, Cartagena's CONTECAR and Sociedad Portuaria Regional handled over 4 million TEUs of containerized cargo, accounting for approximately 63% of Colombia's total public-service container traffic. Barranquilla, meanwhile, processed 3.5 million tonnes of cargo, and Cartagena's cruise terminal serves 34 cruise lines. This substantial maritime activity provides a fertile ground for the adoption of new marine fuels.

Amazónica LNG positions itself as unlocking LNG as a "practical transition fuel" for the region, promising reduced greenhouse gas and local air emissions. However, the term "decarbonisation" needs careful scrutiny. While LNG offers clear benefits in reducing local air pollutants like SOx and NOx, its impact on total CO2e emissions, when considering the full lifecycle and the potency of methane, is far more ambiguous.

## The Methane Pathway: A Closer Look at Emissions Data

The "methane decarbonisation pathway" championed by SEA-LNG and its new member presents a complex picture for global emissions reduction. While LNG combustion results in marginally lower CO2 emissions than traditional heavy fuel oil (HFO), the primary concern for climate impact stems from methane slip – the release of unburned methane into the atmosphere from LNG engines. Methane is a greenhouse gas far more potent than CO2 over a 20-year timescale, with a Global Warming Potential (GWP20) of 82.5 compared to CO2's GWP20 of 1.

For DirtiestShips.com, which relies on granular data like EMSA MRV to track real-world emissions, the expansion of LNG infrastructure must be evaluated against this scientific reality. While the EU MRV system currently primarily reports CO2, discussions are ongoing regarding the full inclusion and accounting for methane emissions from shipping. Vessels operating on LNG, particularly those with older low-pressure dual-fuel engines prone to higher methane slip, may not deliver the anticipated CO2e reductions. This reality stands in contrast to the article's optimistic framing of an "immediate route to decarbonisation."

## Navigating CII Ratings and EU ETS Carbon Costs

The article notes that LNG will help the sector "meet increasingly demanding international decarbonisation requirements." These requirements directly translate to regulations like the IMO's Carbon Intensity Indicator (CII) and the EU Emissions Trading System (ETS). Vessels using LNG are not automatically exempt from poor [CII ratings](/cii.html). Their operational efficiency, specific engine technology (and thus methane slip), and overall CO2 emissions per transport work will dictate their annual A-E rating. A vessel with high methane slip could still find itself with a sub-optimal CII rating if not managed effectively.

Furthermore, while Amazónica LNG's operations are outside the EU, the EU ETS impacts a significant portion of global shipping, including vessels that may call at these Colombian ports as part of their wider trade routes. Shipping companies whose fleets generate high emissions, regardless of bunkering location, face increasing [EU ETS carbon costs](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX%3A32023R0957) for voyages touching EU ports. Investment in LNG infrastructure, therefore, represents a strategic decision that needs to be carefully weighed against the true lifecycle emissions impact and the associated financial liabilities for companies featured in our [company rankings](/companies.html). Without a clearer pathway to genuinely zero-emission fuels, a transition solely to LNG might leave operators vulnerable to future tightening regulations and carbon costs.

## Implications for Global Shipping Fleets

The decision by Amazónica LNG and SEA-LNG to expand LNG bunkering capacity in a significant maritime region like the Colombian Caribbean reflects a broader industry trend towards "alternative" fossil fuels. While it signals a move away from more polluting HFO in terms of local air quality, its long-term climate benefits are questionable without robust measures to mitigate methane slip and a clear roadmap for transitioning to bio-LNG or e-methane, which are not yet widely available at scale.

This development highlights the urgent need for shipping companies to engage in rigorous emissions planning, leveraging detailed [emissions data](/charts.html) to understand the full climate impact of their fuel choices. Relying on "transition fuels" like LNG without fully addressing their CO2e footprint, particularly methane slip, could lead to stranded assets or increased regulatory and financial burdens as international and regional decarbonisation targets become more ambitious.

## What this means for shipping emissions

The expansion of LNG bunkering infrastructure in Colombia, while seemingly a step towards "decarbonisation," underscores the critical need for a data-driven approach to evaluating shipping's environmental impact. DirtiestShips.com emphasizes that true progress in reducing shipping emissions requires a clear-eyed assessment of total CO2e, including methane. While LNG can be a pathway to future zero-carbon fuels like biomethane, its immediate climate benefits as a "transition fuel" are often overstated due to methane slip. Regulatory bodies and the industry must prioritize transparency and accountability for all greenhouse gases, pushing beyond marginal CO2 reductions to embrace genuinely zero-emission solutions for a sustainable future.
