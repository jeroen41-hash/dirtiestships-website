The global shipping industry's decarbonization efforts face a critical juncture as the International Maritime Organization (IMO) emissions talks resume in London, overshadowed by the recent collapse of COP31 negotiations. The inability of international climate negotiators to agree on language addressing fossil fuels or climate finance at COP31, the first such failure since 2009, casts a long shadow over the IMO's Net-Zero Framework (NZF). For a sector responsible for significant CO2 output, this regulatory stagnation risks delaying crucial policy implementation and exacerbating the challenge of reducing maritime emissions, which our [emissions data](/charts.html) tracks globally.

### The Shadow of COP31 and Elusive Compromise

The failure of COP31 in Antalya, where oil- and gas-producing nations resisted explicit references to transitioning away from fossil fuels, has been widely perceived as a "bad omen" for the IMO's Net-Zero Framework. As Transport & Environment noted, this breakdown highlights how fossil fuel interests continue to impede climate action. Davina Hurt of Pacific Environment articulated the sentiment, stating, "The demand for perfection has become a recipe for stagnation. That doesn’t mean lowering our ambition. It means exactly the opposite and acting on it now. COP31 showed us what happens when delay wins. The IMO doesn’t have to repeat it."

Ahead of MEPC 85, an informal "Risotto Group" — including key shipping nations like Greece, Singapore, and Denmark, alongside major economies like the US and Japan — attempted to forge a compromise on the NZF. However, these discussions reportedly failed to produce a document with broad support, leaving significant disagreements over the framework's economic measures and the establishment of an IMO net-zero fund to manage revenues generated by these measures. This stalemate underscores the difficulty in achieving global consensus on equitable burden-sharing and compliance mechanisms for decarbonization.

### China's Pivotal Role in the Standoff

Amidst the fractured negotiations, China's position has emerged as particularly critical. Sources indicated that China could be instrumental in securing the framework, or conversely, in blocking or undermining the existing text. This emphasis on China highlights the complex geopolitical dynamics at play, especially "after the absence of the US in international climate policy," as one source noted. The outcome of China's engagement will likely dictate whether the IMO can advance a globally accepted decarbonization pathway or if the shipping industry will continue to navigate a patchwork of regional regulations.

### Regional Progress vs. Global Stagnation: Implications for Industry

The ongoing global regulatory uncertainty at the IMO level has profound implications for the shipping industry. Without a clear global framework, regional initiatives like the European Union's Emissions Trading System (EU ETS) continue to be the primary drivers of decarbonization efforts in specific trade lanes. The EU ETS, which now includes shipping, imposes direct carbon costs on voyages to and from EU ports, impacting operational expenses for shipowners and operators. This directly affects [company rankings](/companies.html) based on overall emissions and their financial exposure to carbon pricing.

Companies are already grappling with the complexities of existing regulations such as the Carbon Intensity Indicator ([CII ratings](/cii.html)), which rates individual ships on their carbon efficiency. The lack of a robust IMO framework means that operators must adapt to disparate regulations and potentially higher regional carbon costs, increasing operational complexity and potentially hindering long-term investment in alternative fuels and zero-emission technologies. Our data, derived from EMSA MRV, provides critical insights into how these regional policies influence actual emissions reductions and compliance across various vessel types and routes.

### What's at Stake: The Future of Shipping Decarbonization

Environmental groups, including the Clean Shipping Coalition, Environmental Defense Fund, Pacific Environment, and WWF International, have urged countries like Panama to champion stronger global shipping emissions regulations at the IMO, drawing parallels to their stances at COP31. The continued "constructive dialogue and international cooperation" called for by delegates from nations like Malawi and Poland underscores the high stakes. The shipping industry's future emissions trajectory hinges on these negotiations. A continued failure to agree on a meaningful global framework risks not only prolonged emissions but also a fragmented regulatory landscape that could impede the necessary technological and operational shifts required for genuine decarbonization.

### What this means for shipping emissions

The stalled progress at the IMO's GHG Working Group, mirroring the failures of COP31, signals persistent uncertainty for global shipping emissions. Without a broadly supported Net-Zero Framework, the industry faces an increased reliance on regional regulations like the EU ETS, which will continue to drive carbon costs and compliance pressures in specific markets. Companies must anticipate continued regulatory fragmentation, impacting their CII ratings, emissions profiles, and ultimately their financial performance. Concrete, globally aligned action remains critical to prevent further emissions growth and to steer the sector towards its ambitious decarbonization goals.
