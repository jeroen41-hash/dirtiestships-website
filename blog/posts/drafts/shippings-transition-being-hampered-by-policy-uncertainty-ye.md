The global shipping industry's race towards its 2030 decarbonization targets is critically hampered by a lack of global regulatory clarity, jeopardizing the ambitious goal of integrating zero-emission fuels. A new report from the Global Maritime Forum, the fifth edition of 'Climate action in shipping: Progress towards shipping's 2030 breakthrough,' starkly highlights that the failure to adopt a comprehensive global regulatory framework by the International Maritime Organization (IMO) has been "the single most transition-regressive event" in the past five years. This regulatory paralysis directly impacts the crucial uptake of scalable zero-emission fuels (SZEF), with dire implications for CO2 emissions trajectories and the financial viability of green shipping initiatives.

## The 2030 Breakthrough: A Mixed Progress Report

The core ambition, outlined in the 2023 IMO Greenhouse Gas Strategy, is for 5-10% of fuel used in international shipping to come from SZEF by 2030 – a critical stepping stone to net-zero by 2050. The report, launched at New York Climate Week, paints a picture of uneven advancement. While some areas show encouraging growth, others have stagnated or regressed. Positive strides include the world's first ammonia bunkering and sea trials, a 53% increase in methanol bunkering ports (from 19 to 29), and a tripling of methanol-capable in-service tonnage, marking the largest single-year capability gain on record. The global shipping finance portfolio also saw its greatest improvement in climate alignment.

However, these gains are overshadowed by significant setbacks. Jesse Fahnestock, director of decarbonisation at the Global Maritime Forum, emphasizes that while "Shipping’s energy transition is necessary and is going to happen," geopolitical turmoil and regulatory uncertainty have frustrated the process. The report's five 'levers' for progress — Technology and SZEF supply, Demand, Finance, Policy, and Civil Society — reveal only partial progress or regression in key areas like demand, finance, and policy.

## Regulatory Roadblocks: The Cost of IMO Inaction

The most critical impediment cited is the IMO's failure to adopt its Net-Zero Framework (NZF) last year. Dr Pinar Langer of the UCL Energy Institute states unequivocally, "The failure to adopt the NZF was the single most transition-regressive event we've seen in the five years this report has been running." This lack of a clear, global policy signal has fostered a "wait and see" approach across the industry, directly impacting investment decisions and demand for cleaner vessels.

Without the IMO NZF, shipowners lack the long-term certainty needed to commit to significant capital investments in zero-emission technologies and vessels. This uncertainty directly affects future performance metrics like [CII ratings](/cii.html), as companies are hesitant to order newbuilds that might not be compliant or economically viable under future, undefined regulations. Such delays will inevitably impact [company emissions rankings](/companies.html), potentially pushing operators who delay action further down the ladder as regional regulations tighten.

## Investment Hesitation and the Order Book Slump

The impact of policy uncertainty is clearly visible in investment trends. The report notes a regression in the SZEF-capable order book, which fell from 9.5% to 5.7% of total tonnage on order. This significant decline reflects shipowners' reluctance to invest in zero-emission vessels without a clear understanding of future fuel availability, pricing, and regulatory mandates. Concurrently, shipping-specific sustainable debt issuance decreased from $3.4 billion in 2024 to $3.0 billion in 2025, signaling a pullback in financial flows directed towards decarbonization.

Dan Ioschpe, Climate High-Level Champion for COP30, reiterates that "investment decisions cannot afford to wait." The report stresses that accelerating the pace of decarbonization requires stronger demand signals and increased financial flows for zero-emission shipping. The current "wait and see" attitude, fueled by global policy vacuum, directly threatens the achievement of the 2030 SZEF target and thus, the overall reduction of shipping's immense CO2 footprint. ## The EU's Bold Path: ETS and the Global Policy Vacuum

In stark contrast to the global regulatory stalling, the European Union has moved decisively with its Emissions Trading System (ETS) for shipping and the forthcoming FuelEU Maritime regulation. While the IMO grapples with consensus on a global Net-Zero Framework, vessels calling at EU ports are already incurring significant carbon costs under the ETS. This divergence creates an uneven playing field and places a heavier financial burden on companies operating within EU waters, potentially accelerating their transition out of necessity, while others globally can afford to delay.

The lack of a global carbon price or equivalent mechanism means that the cost of inaction for many operators outside the EU remains relatively low, exacerbating the "wait and see" problem. If the IMO fails to adopt the NZF this December, as recommended by the report, it will intensify the pressure for regional blocs like the EU to strengthen their measures further, potentially creating a fragmented regulatory landscape that complicates global trade and increases operational costs for all. ## What this means for shipping emissions

The message is clear: the shipping industry's ambition to decarbonize by 2030 is at a critical juncture. While technological progress in alternative fuels is promising, the absence of a global regulatory framework from the IMO is severely hindering the necessary investments and demand signals. This paralysis risks missing the 2030 SZEF target, which would inevitably lead to higher cumulative CO2 emissions and a more challenging, and costly, pathway to net-zero by 2050. Without urgent policy clarity, particularly from the IMO, the industry risks being trapped in a cycle of uncertainty, where short-term economic concerns override the long-term imperative to decarbonize, leaving the planet to bear the cost.
