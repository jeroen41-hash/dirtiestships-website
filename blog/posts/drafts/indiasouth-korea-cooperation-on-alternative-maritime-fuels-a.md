The global shipping industry, responsible for approximately 3 percent of worldwide greenhouse gas emissions, faces unprecedented pressure to decarbonise. With the International Maritime Organization’s (IMO) 2023 Revised Greenhouse Gas Strategy setting a pathway towards net-zero emissions by or around 2050, and regional measures like the EU Emissions Trading System (EU ETS) and FuelEU Maritime accelerating demand for cleaner operations, the shift towards alternative fuels and green port infrastructure is no longer optional. A recent article highlights the critical cooperation between India and South Korea, two significant maritime nations, in pioneering this energy transition, underscoring how national strategies are now integral to the global decarbonisation agenda.

## The Global Push for Decarbonisation

The IMO's revised strategy marks a pivotal moment, pushing the sector beyond mere operational efficiency improvements towards fundamental changes in fuel and infrastructure. This aligns with DirtiestShips.com's tracking of EU MRV data and CII ratings, which reveal the urgency for vessels to reduce their carbon intensity. The source article reveals the significant domestic emissions footprint of these nations, with India's maritime activities, including international fleet bunkering, emitting over 6.4 million tonnes of CO₂e in 2022, and South Korea’s domestic and international navigation contributing approximately 38.4 million tonnes of CO₂e in the same year. These figures underscore the domestic imperative for decarbonisation that complements international regulatory drivers. The shift is clear: decarbonisation now hinges on developing entirely new energy value chains, encompassing the production and supply of low-carbon marine fuels and the transformation of ports into energy hubs.

## Alternative Fuels: Ammonia, Methanol, and the Carbon Conundrum

Heavy fuel oil's decades-long dominance is waning as the industry seeks viable alternatives. Green ammonia has emerged as a leading long-term contender for deep-sea shipping, lauded for its carbon-free composition and higher volumetric energy density compared to hydrogen, while avoiding the need for cryogenic storage. Green methanol offers operational advantages, being easier to handle with existing infrastructure, but its sustainable production relies on acquiring a sustainable carbon feedstock, such as captured CO₂ (e-methanol) or biomass-derived carbon (bio-methanol). These technological choices directly impact a vessel's future carbon intensity and, consequently, its [CII ratings](/cii.html). As vessels transition to these fuels, the industry faces the dual challenge of ensuring scalable, sustainable fuel production and developing the necessary bunkering and storage infrastructure at ports.

## India's Role as a Green Fuel Hub

India is strategically positioning itself upstream in the alternative fuel value chain. Its National Green Hydrogen Mission has identified key ports—Deendayal (Kandla), Paradip, and V.O. Chidambaranar (Tuticorin)—as Green Hydrogen Hubs. Leveraging competitive renewable energy costs, India aims to become a major exporter of zero-carbon maritime fuels, specifically green hydrogen and green ammonia. Concrete progress is already visible at Deendayal Port, which commissioned a 1-megawatt electrolyser-based green hydrogen plant in 2025 and completed India's first shore-to-ship methanol bunkering demonstration in April 2026. The port plans to supply around 500 kilotonnes per annum (KTPA) of e-methanol by 2028–29, aligning with the Harit Sagar-Green Port Guidelines requiring major ports to establish green ammonia bunkering facilities by 2035. This ambition, embedded in the Maritime Amrit Kaal Vision 2047, demonstrates a comprehensive national strategy to support global shipping decarbonisation.

## South Korea's Shipbuilding Prowess Meets Policy

South Korea, a global leader in shipbuilding, complements India's fuel production ambitions with its advanced vessel construction capabilities. The country's National Assembly passed the Green Shipping Corridor Support Act in 2026, accelerating the development of low-emission shipping routes and supporting port infrastructure. Home to three of the world’s largest shipbuilders—HD Hyundai, Hanwha Ocean, and Samsung Heavy Industries—South Korea is at the forefront of constructing Liquefied Natural Gas (LNG)-, methanol-, and ammonia-capable vessels. This focus on next-generation maritime technologies ensures that the demand for new, greener vessels can be met, providing tangible pathways for shipping companies to improve their [company rankings](/companies.html) in terms of emissions performance and navigate increasing EU ETS carbon costs.

## What this means for shipping emissions

The collaboration between India and South Korea exemplifies a critical global trend: the concerted effort across the maritime ecosystem to achieve decarbonisation. This involves not only innovative shipbuilding and alternative fuel development but also the crucial transformation of port infrastructure into energy hubs. As these initiatives scale, they will directly influence the global fleet's carbon footprint, impacting EU MRV data and improving overall [emissions data](/charts.html). The availability of low-carbon fuels, combined with new vessel designs and supportive port infrastructure, will be instrumental in enabling shipping companies to meet increasingly stringent regulatory requirements, improve their CII ratings, and ultimately drive down global maritime CO2 emissions towards the IMO's net-zero target. The shift is underway, driven by both market demand and regulatory pressure, and nations like India and South Korea are demonstrating the necessary integrated approach.
