For years, the shipping industry's environmental footprint has largely been assessed through estimations, relying on default emission factors and fuel consumption data rather than real-time, direct measurements. This reliance has introduced uncertainties into critical regulatory frameworks like the EU MRV and IMO's DCS. A significant shift towards verifiable precision is now underway, with major players Lloyd’s Register, Seapeak, Kongsberg Maritime, and Tunable launching a joint development project to rigorously test and validate continuous emissions monitoring systems (CEMS) on board an LNG carrier. This initiative, announced at Gastech 2026, promises to deliver unprecedented accuracy, fundamentally altering how emissions are reported, regulated, and managed across the global fleet.

### The Shift from Estimation to Precision

The core challenge in shipping emissions reporting has been the reliance on proxies. As Panos Mitrou, SVP Shipping Strategy at Lloyd’s Register, points out, "The industry currently relies on default emissions factors and test bed data to estimate environmental performance." While these methods provide a baseline, they may not accurately reflect real-world operational emissions, which can vary significantly based on speed, load, weather, and engine performance. This gap between estimated and actual emissions has profound implications for a data-driven platform like DirtiestShips.com, which analyzes reported emissions data to assess environmental performance and assign [CII ratings](/cii.html).

The new project aims to bridge this gap by deploying Kongsberg Maritime's CEMS, incorporating Tunable’s gas analysis technology, on a new LNG carrier from Seapeak's fleet. Scheduled for delivery in early 2027, the vessel will undergo a year-long monitoring period where the CEMS will directly measure CO2 emissions. This continuous data will then be rigorously compared against traditional engine test bed data and the default emission factors currently used in regulatory reporting. Lloyd’s Register will act as the independent verifier, adding a layer of crucial objectivity to the validation process.

### Pilot Program Specifics: What's Being Tested

The joint development project focuses on validating the accuracy and reliability of direct CO2 measurement in real-world operating conditions. Eirik Agren, Sales Director at Kongsberg Maritime, highlights that "Most maritime CO₂ reporting today is based on fuel consumption and standard emission factors rather than continuous, direct measurement." The project's outcome will directly address this, providing robust evidence on whether CEMS can offer a more precise picture.

The selection of an LNG carrier is particularly relevant. While LNG is considered a transition fuel, its combustion still produces CO2, and the industry faces scrutiny over potential methane slip. Accurately quantifying CO2 emissions from LNG vessels is crucial for understanding their true environmental profile and ensuring that regulatory incentives align with actual performance. This trial moves beyond theoretical models, grounding data in the realities of maritime operations and setting a precedent for enhanced transparency in the sector.

### Implications for EU MRV, CII, and ETS Compliance

The findings from this trial could trigger a seismic shift in how shipping companies approach compliance. The EU MRV regulation currently mandates monitoring and reporting of CO2 emissions based on fuel consumption. If CEMS data proves significantly more accurate and reliable, it could pave the way for direct measurement to become the preferred, or even mandatory, method for reporting. This would directly impact the data integrity that underpins our [emissions data](/charts.html) and [company rankings](/companies.html).

For the Carbon Intensity Indicator ([CII ratings](/cii.html)), which rates vessels annually based on their CO2 emissions per transport work, more precise data could lead to fairer and more accurate ratings. A ship currently rated 'C' or 'D' based on estimated factors might perform better or worse under direct measurement, influencing operational decisions and chartering attractiveness. James Cruickshank, Director – Marine & Technical Services at Seapeak, emphasizes their commitment to "practical, data-driven solutions that enhance both environmental performance and operational efficiency." More accurate data provides a stronger foundation for such decisions.

Furthermore, the EU Emissions Trading System (ETS) has introduced a direct cost for CO2 emissions for shipping within EU waters. The accuracy of reported emissions directly translates into financial liabilities. If CEMS demonstrates that current default factors systematically over- or under-estimate emissions, it could lead to substantial adjustments in the carbon costs borne by shipping companies. Accurate data could offer a competitive advantage, enabling operators to optimize routes and speeds with precise real-time feedback on their carbon footprint and associated costs.

### What This Means for Shipping Emissions

This joint development project represents a crucial step towards a more transparent, data-driven, and accountable future for shipping emissions. By independently verifying the accuracy of continuous emissions monitoring technology, the industry can move beyond estimations to a clearer understanding of its environmental impact. This precision will not only lead to more robust regulatory compliance under frameworks like EU MRV and IMO DCS but also empower shipowners and operators to make more informed decisions to minimize emissions, optimize fuel efficiency, and manage their carbon costs more effectively. The data generated by such systems will provide invaluable insights for future policy-making and ensure that the global shipping industry's journey towards decarbonization is guided by verifiable facts, not just approximations.
