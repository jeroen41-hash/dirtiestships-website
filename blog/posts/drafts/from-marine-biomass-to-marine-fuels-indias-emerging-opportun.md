The global shipping industry, responsible for approximately 3 percent of global greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, is under unprecedented pressure to decarbonize, with the International Maritime Organization (IMO) targeting net-zero by 2050 and a crucial 5 percent adoption of zero- or near-zero-GHG-emission energy sources by 2030. This ambitious trajectory, reinforced by mechanisms like the IMO's proposed global GHG pricing and the EU's FuelEU Maritime regulation, is creating strong economic incentives for scalable low-carbon marine fuels. Amidst this regulatory storm, emerging solutions like marine biomass-derived biofuels are gaining attention, promising a complementary pathway to reduce shipping's reliance on fossil fuels.

### The Regulatory Onslaught Driving Fuel Innovation

The drive for cleaner fuels is not merely aspirational; it's codified in an evolving international regulatory framework. The IMO's revised strategy aims for a significant reduction in GHG fuel intensity (GFI) from the 2008 baseline of 93.3 gCO2eq/MJ. All ships of 5,000 gross tonnes and above, which account for about 85 percent of international shipping emissions, are mandated to report fuel-consumption data. This data, increasingly scrutinized, forms the basis for performance metrics like [CII ratings](/cii.html), which penalize vessels for higher emissions intensity and push operators towards more efficient operations and cleaner fuels.

Complementing global efforts, the 2025 FuelEU Maritime regulation directly impacts ships calling at European ports, setting specific GHG intensity standards. These measures, alongside the escalating costs associated with the EU Emissions Trading System (ETS), mean that investing in alternative, low-carbon fuels is no longer an option but a rapidly becoming a necessity for compliance and cost mitigation. ### Marine Biofuels: Potential and Potholes

Marine biofuels, specifically those derived from marine biomass like microalgae and seaweed, offer a compelling alternative to traditional fossil fuels and even land-based biofuels. Unlike their terrestrial counterparts, marine biomass can be cultivated in seawater, avoiding the contentious "food-versus-fuel" debate and reducing competition for land and freshwater resources. They also possess the advantage of being potentially usable in existing ship engines with minimal or no modifications, easing the transition burden for the current global fleet.

However, scaling up marine biofuel production faces significant techno-economic hurdles. While the marine biofuels sector is projected to grow from approximately US$4.2 billion to US$7.0 billion by 2033, challenges abound. The primary obstacle lies in the energy-intensive and costly processes of biomass harvesting and processing, particularly for microalgae, which has a low solid content. Independent analyses cited by the article estimate microalgae biodiesel production costs at a prohibitive US$300–2,600 per barrel equivalent, significantly higher than crude oil's US$40–80 per barrel. Despite these costs, regional adoption is increasing; Singapore, a major bunkering hub, saw biofuel-blended bunker fuel rise from 0.52 million tonnes in 2023 to 1.36 million tonnes in 2025, largely driven by European policy initiatives like FuelEU Maritime.

### The Economic Equation: Biorefineries and ETS

Bridging the vast cost gap between marine biofuels and conventional fuels is crucial for widespread adoption. One promising approach highlighted is co-product valorization, or the biorefinery model. Instead of solely producing fuel, marine biomass can be processed to yield lipids, carbohydrates, proteins, and other biochemicals for pharmaceuticals, nutraceuticals, cosmetics, and biodegradable materials. This multi-product strategy can dramatically improve economic feasibility, potentially reducing the minimum selling price of marine fuel from US$8 to US$2.5 per gallon gasoline equivalent (GGE).

Such a shift in production economics is vital, especially when considering the rising cost of carbon emissions under schemes like the EU ETS. As shipping companies face direct financial penalties for their carbon footprint, the long-term investment in cleaner, albeit initially more expensive, fuels becomes more justifiable. The integration of biorefinery approaches into policy frameworks would accelerate the commercial viability of marine biofuels, transforming them from niche alternatives to a significant part of the shipping fuel mix.

### India's Untapped Marine Biomass Potential

The article underscores India's vast marine biomass resources, including over 800 identified seaweed species, presenting a strong natural advantage. Despite this potential, marine decarbonisation discussions have largely been absent from India's national energy transition agenda, with policy support remaining fragmented. Leveraging these resources could allow India to leapfrog the land-based "food-versus-fuel" dilemma plaguing its current biofuel mandates and position itself as a key player in the global supply chain for sustainable marine fuels. However, this requires strategic policy intervention and significant investment in infrastructure to overcome the challenges associated with marine cultivation and processing, including exposure to extreme weather conditions. ### What this means for shipping emissions

The journey to decarbonize shipping is complex, demanding diverse fuel solutions and innovative production methods. Marine biomass offers a unique, complementary pathway, particularly due to its avoidance of land-use conflicts and compatibility with existing engine technology. However, its widespread adoption hinges on significant cost reductions, driven by technological advancements and supportive policy frameworks that encourage biorefinery models. As international regulations tighten and carbon costs rise, the economic imperative to invest in and scale up such fuels will only grow.
