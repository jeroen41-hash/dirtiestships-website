The latest developments in the ARA (Amsterdam-Rotterdam-Antwerp) bunker market highlight a critical divergence driven by early regulatory action, demonstrating how regional policy implementation can profoundly impact shipping's decarbonization trajectory. While Rotterdam grapples with a decline in conventional fuel sales, potentially shifting fossil fuel demand to neighboring Antwerp, it has simultaneously emerged as a dominant hub for alternative fuels. This "ARA divide," spurred by the Netherlands' revised Renewable Energy Directive (RED III), offers a real-time case study for the industry, revealing the complex interplay between environmental policy, economic incentives, and the push for lower emissions – all factors that directly influence a vessel's [CII ratings](/cii.html) and a company's carbon costs under the EU ETS.

## The Regulatory Wedge Driving the ARA Divide

The Netherlands' early adoption of RED III has created a distinct regulatory environment, particularly affecting the Port of Rotterdam. The port authority and industry bodies, including Deltalinqs, attribute a significant decline in conventional bunker volumes – trailing Antwerp for the second consecutive quarter – directly to the additional sustainability obligations imposed on fuel suppliers. These measures have increased the cost of conventional bunker fuels relative to competing ports, leading some shipowners to seek cheaper, fossil-based options elsewhere. Deltalinqs' position paper to Dutch ministries on July 20th clearly articulated this concern, stating that "ships are simply bunkering 100% fossil fuels elsewhere," despite the directive's intent to accelerate greener fuel adoption. This scenario underscores the immediate economic pressures on shipowners when faced with varying regional compliance costs, potentially leading to carbon leakage if not carefully managed across the wider EU ETS framework.

## Rotterdam's Green Fuel Momentum and Economic Realities

Despite losing ground in conventional bunkers, Rotterdam is leading the charge in alternative fuel adoption. The port recorded significantly higher sales of low- and zero-emission bunker fuels than Antwerp. Biofuel blend sales in Rotterdam more than doubled, surging from approximately 105,000 metric tons in Q1 2026 to about 240,000 metric tons in Q2 2026. Growth extended beyond biofuels, with bio-methanol deliveries nearly tripling from 1,000 mt to 3,000 mt. Notably, Rotterdam also saw its first ethanol bunker sales at around 1,000 mt, with vessels like X-Press Feeders’ Eco Levant and Maersk’s Laura Maersk utilizing ethanol or ethanol-methanol blends. While liquefied biomethane (LBM) volumes saw a temporary dip, Antwerp's bio-bunker sales, though increasing from 21,000 mt to 27,000 mt, remained well below Rotterdam’s impressive volumes.

A key driver for this shift is the improving economics of alternative fuels. As Prima Markets analyst Ulrich Arnheiter noted, the compression of bio-bunker premiums and higher fossil fuel prices during global conflicts have made biofuels more competitive. Crucially, the analysis by Verde Marine Energy's Joe Tierney highlights that LSMGO-equivalent B100 can now be *cheaper* than conventional LSMGO in Dutch ports. This cost advantage is realized once EU ETS and FuelEU Maritime incentives, coupled with discounts from Dutch ZRE A tickets, are factored into the price. This direct link between regulatory incentives and commercial viability is a powerful catalyst, driving a "huge increase in B100 demand and requirements" and illustrating how carbon costs under the EU ETS can be effectively mitigated through fuel choice.

## Tracking the Transition: CII, Costs, and Company Strategy

The adoption of these greener fuels has direct implications for shipping companies navigating strict environmental regulations. Vessels bunkering alternative fuels will see a positive impact on their [CII ratings](/cii.html), improving their operational carbon intensity score and reducing the risk of being classified in lower, non-compliant categories. Companies like Maersk and X-Press Feeders, by proactively embracing fuels like ethanol and bio-methanol, are positioning themselves as leaders in decarbonization, which will be reflected positively in [company rankings](/companies.html) tracking emissions performance.

While Rotterdam currently leads, the landscape remains dynamic. Equinor's agreement to supply Wallenius Wilhelmsen with bio-methanol in Antwerp and Zeebrugge from late 2026 suggests that the alternative fuel gap across the ARA region may narrow. Furthermore, the European Commission's approval of a €103 million Dutch State aid scheme for methanol- and hydrogen-powered vessels underscores broader governmental support for green shipping technologies. On a global scale, Brazil's move to permit up to 100% biofuel (B100) for marine bunkering mirrors the regulatory push for cleaner fuels, indicating a worldwide trend towards incentivizing lower-carbon operations, all of which will be meticulously tracked by comprehensive [emissions data](/charts.html) and reported on our [news feed](/news.html).

## What this means for shipping emissions

The "ARA divide" clearly demonstrates that robust regulatory frameworks, when coupled with appropriate economic incentives like those within the EU ETS and FuelEU Maritime, can accelerate the shipping industry's shift towards lower-emission fuels. While early implementation may create regional disparities in conventional fuel bunkering, it simultaneously fosters a vibrant market for alternatives. This indicates a pathway where carbon pricing mechanisms effectively re-route investment and operational decisions towards decarbonization, ultimately reducing overall shipping emissions. The rapid growth of alternative fuel sales in Rotterdam is a tangible outcome of this approach, proving that with the right blend of regulation and economics, significant strides can be made towards a greener global fleet.
