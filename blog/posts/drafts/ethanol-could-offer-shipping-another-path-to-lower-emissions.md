The global shipping industry is under unprecedented pressure to decarbonize, driven by escalating climate concerns and an tightening regulatory framework, including the EU Emissions Trading System (ETS) and the IMO's Carbon Intensity Indicator (CII). As shipowners desperately seek viable pathways to reduce their carbon footprint, the exploration of alternative fuels is intensifying. A recent study by the Mærsk Mc-Kinney Møller Center for Zero Carbon Shipping shines a light on ethanol, suggesting it could emerge as a practical alternative, particularly for vessels already equipped for methanol, while also identifying potential — albeit challenging — applications for the vast conventional diesel fleet. This analysis delves into the technical feasibility and economic implications of ethanol, connecting its potential impact to the very metrics we track at DirtiestShips.com.

## Ethanol's Potential: A Natural Fit for Methanol-Capable Vessels

The study, "Ethanol as a Marine Fuel: Exploring Its Usability," highlights a significant opportunity for ships already investing in methanol dual-fuel technology. Due to their similar chemical properties, ethanol demonstrates strong technical compatibility with methanol-capable engines. Researchers found that relatively limited modifications, primarily involving adjustments to fuel injection timing and engine controls, would be needed to enable operation on ethanol. This finding is crucial, as it suggests a path to increased fuel flexibility for a growing segment of the fleet.

Major players are already exploring this synergy. Maersk, a pioneer in methanol adoption, has been testing ethanol aboard its methanol-fueled container vessel *Laura Maersk* since 2025, progressing from a 10% blend to 100% ethanol by June 2026. More recently, the 9,016-TEU *Tangier Maersk* completed a commercial ethanol bunkering operation in Houston, marking a significant step in validating its real-world application for deep-sea container vessels. Engine manufacturers like Everllence, WinGD, Wärtsilä, HD Hyundai, and Caterpillar are also advancing testing and conversion kits, accelerating the potential for wider adoption in this segment. The higher energy density of ethanol compared to methanol also means less fuel storage capacity might be required for the same range, an operational advantage for vessel design and cargo space.

## The Diesel Dilemma: Significant Hurdles for Conventional Engines

While the transition for methanol-capable ships appears relatively smooth, the prospect of using ethanol in the much larger fleet of conventional diesel-powered vessels presents substantial challenges. The study investigated blending ethanol with marine gas oil (MGO) and encountered two primary technical hurdles:

1.  **Low Flashpoint:** Even a modest 10% ethanol blend sharply reduced the fuel mixture's flashpoint to approximately 13.5°C. This is significantly below the 60°C minimum flashpoint generally required under SOLAS regulations for conventional marine fuels, posing considerable safety concerns. Attempts to raise the flashpoint with additives were unsuccessful.
2.  **Fuel Separation:** Ethanol-diesel blends showed a tendency to separate during storage, particularly at lower temperatures (e.g., all three tested blends separated after one week at 10°C). This raises critical questions about fuel handling, reliability, and the potential for engine damage.

While onboard blending systems could potentially mitigate the separation issue by mixing fuels just before combustion, the low flashpoint would still necessitate extensive modifications to onboard fuel systems and enhanced safety equipment, increasing both complexity and cost. These technical barriers indicate that a widespread, direct conversion for the conventional diesel fleet using ethanol blends is far more complex and costly than for methanol-ready vessels.

## Economic Viability and Regulatory Impact

The study did include an economic analysis, using a Kamsarmax bulk carrier as a case study for conversion to ethanol-MGO blends. Researchers estimated that such a modification could cost approximately half as much as a full conversion to methanol dual-fuel capability. However, under the study's baseline assumptions, operating on a 30% ethanol blend was not economically competitive with continued operation on very low-sulfur fuel oil (VLSFO).

Crucially, the economics improved considerably for specific scenarios: ships operating primarily in sulfur emission control areas (SECAs), vessels with higher annual fuel consumption, and those able to source ethanol with a particularly low lifecycle carbon intensity. For instance, a large container ship consuming around 25,000 tonnes of fuel annually could find a 30% ethanol blend economically viable across a wider range of fuel carbon intensities. This underscores the interplay between fuel cost, operational profile, and the future emissions-pricing framework, such as the EU ETS. For companies facing rising carbon costs under the EU ETS, or striving to improve their [CII ratings](/cii.html) to avoid penalties, investing in lower-carbon fuels like sustainably produced ethanol could eventually prove economically rational, despite initial conversion costs. ## Lifecycle Emissions: The Unanswered Question

While the technical and economic aspects of ethanol are being explored, the study "stops short of determining how much ethanol could realistically contribute to shipping’s overall decarbonization." This is a critical distinction for a platform like DirtiestShips.com. The report explicitly states that sustainability, lifecycle emissions (well-to-wake), and scalability will be addressed in separate reports.

The environmental performance of ethanol varies widely based on feedstocks, agricultural practices, land-use changes, and production methods. For ethanol to be a true "lower-emission" fuel, its production must be genuinely sustainable, avoiding issues like deforestation or excessive land use, and minimize upstream greenhouse gas emissions. Without this crucial lifecycle assessment, the true carbon reduction benefits – and thus its impact on vessel emissions data and regulatory compliance – remain speculative. Companies adopting ethanol will need to demonstrate its low carbon intensity to truly benefit their [CII ratings](/cii.html) and reduce their overall carbon footprint.

## What this means for shipping emissions

Ethanol presents a dual opportunity: an accessible and viable option for the growing fleet of methanol-capable vessels seeking greater fuel flexibility, and a more challenging, but potentially economically rational, pathway for some conventional diesel ships under specific operating conditions and future regulatory frameworks. While Maersk's proactive trials are noteworthy, the widespread adoption of ethanol hinges not just on technical compatibility and economic competitiveness, but critically on its verified lifecycle carbon intensity. As regulators tighten emissions targets and carbon pricing mechanisms like the EU ETS take hold, the industry’s ability to transition to truly low-emission fuels will be paramount. Our ongoing tracking of [emissions data](/charts.html) will reflect how these alternative fuel strategies translate into real-world reductions. The journey to zero-carbon shipping is complex, and ethanol, with its established supply chains, may become a significant piece of the puzzle, provided its entire value chain is demonstrably sustainable.
