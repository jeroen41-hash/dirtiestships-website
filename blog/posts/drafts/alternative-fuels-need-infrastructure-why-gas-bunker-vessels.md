The maritime industry's ambitious decarbonization targets hinge not just on innovative engine technologies and new fuel types, but critically on the infrastructure that delivers these fuels. A recent DNV white paper, "Gas bunker vessels – Facilitating the transition to alternative fuels," underscores this urgent reality, highlighting how the burgeoning demand for LNG as a marine fuel is creating a significant bottleneck in bunkering capacity. This structural deficit directly threatens the industry's ability to meet stringent emissions regulations like the EU ETS and improve [CII ratings](/cii.html), potentially slowing the pace of necessary emissions reductions across the global fleet.

## LNG's Rapid Rise and the Looming Supply Gap

Liquefied Natural Gas (LNG) has emerged as the most widely adopted alternative marine fuel, with DNV's Alternative Fuels Insight platform reporting approximately 900 LNG-fuelled vessels in operation globally, excluding LNG carriers. This fleet is projected to expand dramatically, with around 650 additional vessels expected to join before 2030. Unsurprisingly, LNG consumption has skyrocketed, increasing by over 500% between 2020 and 2024, with a further 167% growth anticipated between 2025 and 2030.

However, the DNV report warns that bunkering infrastructure is not keeping pace. Martin Cartwright, Global Business Director, Gas Carriers & FSRUs, DNV Maritime, states that "the global LNG bunker vessel fleet may need to more than double by 2030, with between 165 and 208 vessels potentially required worldwide." This translates into a widening gap between fuel demand and available bunkering capacity, a critical issue for shipping companies invested in LNG. Without sufficient and reliable bunkering, vessels designed for lower-carbon fuels risk delays, rerouting, or even being forced to use conventional, higher-emission fuels, directly impacting their operational efficiency and environmental performance.

## Gas Bunker Vessels: A Critical Link for Decarbonization

The DNV white paper emphasizes that gas bunker vessels are far more than mere fuel delivery assets; they are a vital link connecting fuel producers, storage facilities, ports, and receiving vessels. Their unique operational profile, involving frequent ship-to-ship transfers in busy port environments, demands specialized design, robust safety systems, and highly competent crews.

The article suggests that accelerating capacity growth could involve converting suitable small-scale LNG carriers into dedicated bunker vessels. While this offers a practical shortcut, it requires meticulous attention to compatibility, transfer arrangements, vapour management, and emergency response to ensure safe and efficient operations. For companies investing heavily in modern, LNG-capable fleets, the reliability of this bunkering chain is paramount to achieving the operational efficiencies and emissions reductions necessary to maintain competitive [company rankings](/companies.html) and comply with evolving regulations.

## Safety, Standardization, and the Path to Future Fuels

As gas bunkering operations become more frequent and geographically dispersed, the importance of maintaining consistently high standards of safety and operational performance cannot be overstated. The DNV report stresses that gaseous fuels introduce complex considerations such as cryogenic handling, pressure control, and emergency response. Robert Østraat Kjelstadli, Principal Engineer, DNV Maritime, highlights that "safe and reliable fuel delivery depends on dedicated vessel designs, robust procedures, and the ability to operate efficiently across a wide range of ports and vessel types."

Looking beyond LNG, these bunker vessels will also need to be adaptable for emerging fuels like ammonia. The technical and safety challenges associated with these next-generation fuels will be even greater, requiring significant foresight in vessel design, crew training, and regulatory frameworks. The lessons learned from scaling up LNG bunkering safely and efficiently will be invaluable in preparing for an even more diverse, and potentially more volatile, future fuel landscape.

## Financial and Regulatory Implications

The lagging bunkering infrastructure has direct financial and regulatory consequences. For ship owners, unreliable fuel supply can lead to increased operational costs, potential penalties for non-compliance with the EU Emissions Trading System (ETS) if they cannot access their preferred lower-emission fuels, and ultimately, poorer [CII ratings](/cii.html). Companies planning for future emissions liabilities under the EU ETS must account for the availability and cost of alternative fuels, which are heavily influenced by the efficiency and scale of the bunkering network. Data collected through EU MRV (Monitoring, Reporting, Verification) will clearly illustrate the actual fuel consumption patterns and associated emissions, making the reliable supply of alternative fuels a critical factor in a company's reported environmental footprint.

## What this means for shipping emissions

The DNV white paper serves as a critical warning: the transition to lower-emission shipping is not solely about ships and engines, but equally about the often-overlooked, yet essential, bunkering infrastructure. A robust, safe, and scalable gas bunker vessel fleet is indispensable for realizing the emissions reduction potential of LNG and paving the way for future alternative fuels. Without coordinated investment to address the looming supply gap, the industry risks hindering its own decarbonization efforts, failing to meet regulatory targets, and ultimately slowing the urgent fight against climate change. The reliability of this infrastructure will be a key determinant of whether shipping can truly clean up its act.
