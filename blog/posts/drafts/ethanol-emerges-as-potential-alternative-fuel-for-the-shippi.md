The global shipping industry is at a critical juncture, facing mounting pressure from regulations like the EU Emissions Trading System (ETS) and the IMO's Carbon Intensity Indicator (CII). As operators grapple with escalating carbon costs and the imperative to improve their [CII ratings](/cii.html), the search for viable alternative fuels intensifies. New research, highlighted by globaltrademag.com, suggests ethanol could emerge as a significant contender, offering a pathway to lower emissions for both methanol-capable vessels and, with greater challenges, existing conventional diesel-powered ships. This development could reshape future [company rankings](/companies.html) based on emission performance and investment in green technologies.

### Ethanol's Dual Path: Methanol-Capable vs. Conventional Engines

A comprehensive study by the Mærsk Mc-Kinney Møller Center for Zero Carbon Shipping, developed with partners including NORDEN, TotalEnergies, and ABS, explores ethanol's technical, safety, and commercial viability. The report, "Ethanol as a Marine Fuel: Exploring Its Usability," identifies a near-term opportunity in methanol-compatible engines. Due to similar chemical properties, ethanol can be used in these engines with relatively limited modifications, primarily involving adjustments to fuel injection timing and engine controls.

Notably, ethanol boasts a higher energy density than methanol, potentially allowing vessels to carry less fuel for comparable operational ranges. Shipping giant Maersk has been at the forefront of this exploration, with trials on its methanol-fueled container ship *Laura Maersk* since 2025, progressing from a 10% ethanol blend to successful tests with pure ethanol by June 2026. This has culminated in the first commercial ship-to-ship ethanol bunkering of a deep-sea container vessel in the US, the 9,016-TEU *Tangier Maersk* in Houston, marking a significant step towards commercial adoption. Engine manufacturers like Everllence, WinGD, Wärtsilä, HD Hyundai, and Caterpillar are also actively developing solutions, with Caterpillar announcing conversion kits for selected MaK engines.

### The Challenge of Diesel-Ethanol Blends: Safety and Stability

While the prospect of expanding ethanol use to a larger market of conventional diesel-powered ships is appealing, the research outlines substantial technical hurdles. Blending ethanol with marine gas oil presents two major obstacles: flashpoint and fuel stability.

The study found that even a small proportion of ethanol drastically reduces the flashpoint of the mixture. A 10% ethanol blend registered a flashpoint of approximately 13.5°C, significantly below the 60°C minimum typically required by SOLAS for conventional marine fuels. Attempts to raise this with additives proved unsuccessful. This safety concern necessitates fundamental changes to vessel design and operational protocols for widespread adoption.

Fuel stability poses a second problem. Tested ethanol-diesel mixtures separated during storage, particularly at lower temperatures, with all three blends separating after one week at 10°C. This instability challenges reliable fuel storage and handling on board. Onboard blending, where ethanol and diesel are mixed just before combustion, is a potential solution, but it does not mitigate the flashpoint risks, still requiring modified fuel systems and enhanced safety measures.

### Economic Implications and Regulatory Drivers

Despite the technical challenges for diesel blends, the study suggests that ethanol retrofits could be economically attractive for some existing ships. Using a Kamsarmax bulk carrier as a case study, modifications for ethanol-marine gas oil blends were estimated to cost about half that of a full methanol dual-fuel conversion.

The economic assessment considered a 30% ethanol blend, comparing operating costs under future emissions-pricing assumptions based on the draft IMO Net-Zero Framework. While not immediately cost-competitive with conventional very low-sulfur fuel oil in a baseline scenario, the outlook improved for vessels operating in sulfur emission control areas, those with higher annual fuel consumption, and crucially, operators able to source ethanol with a particularly low lifecycle carbon intensity. A large container ship consuming around 25,000 tonnes of fuel annually, for instance, could make a 30% ethanol blend economically viable across a broader range of fuel carbon intensities. This directly impacts potential EU ETS liabilities, as lower actual emissions translate to reduced carbon costs.

### The Critical Role of Lifecycle Emissions and CII

The research underscores that while existing production and distribution infrastructure for ethanol is an advantage, its true decarbonization potential hinges on its lifecycle emissions. The sustainability of ethanol varies significantly based on feedstocks, farming practices, land-use changes, and production methods. This distinction is paramount for shipowners striving to improve their [CII ratings](/cii.html). Using high-carbon intensity ethanol, even if technically feasible, would undermine efforts to reduce a vessel's operational carbon intensity, potentially leading to poorer ratings and greater regulatory scrutiny reflected in EMSA MRV data. The Center plans further reports to delve into sustainability, lifecycle emissions, and production scalability, which will be vital for industry stakeholders making investment decisions.

### What this means for shipping emissions

Ethanol presents a compelling, albeit complex, alternative fuel option that could accelerate shipping's decarbonization journey. For methanol-capable vessels, it offers a near-term, high-energy-density pathway, as demonstrated by Maersk's commercial bunkering efforts. For the broader conventional fleet, significant safety and stability challenges with diesel blends must be overcome. Ultimately, ethanol's role in slashing shipping emissions, reducing EU ETS exposure, and improving [CII ratings](/cii.html) will be determined not just by engine compatibility or cost, but by the availability of truly low-carbon intensity ethanol produced sustainably across its entire lifecycle.
