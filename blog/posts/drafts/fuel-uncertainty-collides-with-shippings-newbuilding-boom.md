The global shipping industry stands at a critical juncture, with new analysis underscoring that the ambitious shift away from fossil fuels will remain largely aspirational without a dramatic escalation in carbon pricing. This stark warning, stemming from a new study by the Global Centre for Maritime Decarbonisation (GCMD) and Boston Consulting Group (BCG), directly challenges the industry's decarbonization pathway, particularly as a significant newbuilding boom is already underway, setting the emissions trajectory for decades to come under the watchful eye of regulations like the EU ETS and CII ratings.

## The Carbon Price Threshold: $700 per Tonne

The "Navigating the Maritime Fuel Transition" report models 12 fuel pathways and six engine configurations through to 2050, revealing that carbon pricing is the single most influential factor in driving fuel choices. Under a base scenario, which assumes an IMO Tier-2 remedial unit penalty of $380 per tonne of CO2 equivalent, conventional fossil fuels – VLSFO and fossil LNG – are projected to still account for a staggering 40% of the fleet's energy consumption by 2050. Add VLSFO combined with onboard carbon capture, and fossil-derived solutions dominate 70% of the energy mix.

The picture changes dramatically only when the carbon penalty reaches $700 per tonne by 2050. At this level, GCMD and BCG calculate that new, low-carbon fuels become broadly competitive, seizing a 61% share of shipping energy consumption, while fossil fuels are curtailed to just 13%. This analysis highlights a significant gap between current market signals, such as the EU ETS which has seen prices fluctuate but remain well below this transformative threshold, and the necessary economic drivers for deep decarbonization. Without such a robust carbon price, the financial incentive for owners to transition to costly alternative fuels remains insufficient, directly impacting future CO2 emissions reported via EU MRV data.

## Alternative Fuels: Parity and the Hydrogen Swing

The study also provides crucial insights into the economic competitiveness of alternative fuels. Contrary to some assumptions, e-methanol and e-ammonia are modelled to be economically much closer than often believed, reaching around $52/GJ by 2050. While ammonia benefits from lower production costs, this advantage is largely offset by more expensive storage, transport, bunkering, and stringent safety requirements.

The true "swing factor" for the cost of these e-fuels, according to the report, is hydrogen. Renewable hydrogen accounts for a significant 54% to 60% of the levelised cost of both e-methanol and e-ammonia. Should the cost of renewable hydrogen fall from a base assumption of $3/kg to $2/kg by 2050, methanol and ammonia could collectively supply 36% of the global fleet's energy demand. For methanol, the cost of biogenic CO2 is another critical variable, with cheap bio-methanol potentially acting as a bridge fuel, encouraging investment in methanol-capable tonnage now, and easing a later transition to e-methanol. This underscores the need for "optionality" in newbuilding designs, a message echoed by the Society for Gas as a Marine Fuel (SGMF), which also declined to crown a single winning fuel, emphasizing that the production pathway can be as crucial as the molecule itself.

## The Accelerated Newbuilding Boom and Its Decarbonization Risk

Compounding the fuel uncertainty is an unprecedented newbuilding boom. As renowned maritime economist Martin Stopford highlighted at Capital Link’s London Shipping & Marine Services Forum, shipping has generated approximately $3.1 trillion in cash since 2021 – surpassing the celebrated 2004-08 supercycle. Roughly a quarter of this windfall has already been directed towards new ships.

This timing is critical and concerning. Stopford had anticipated the industry's major fleet investment wave to occur in the 2030s. Instead, buoyant markets and flush balance sheets have advanced this boom by roughly eight years. The GCMD and BCG report warns that more than half the ships operating in 2050 will come from orders placed before 2035. This means that many vessels being ordered *today* will define the industry's emissions profile for decades to come. Without clear economic signals or sufficient optionality for future fuel flexibility, there's a significant risk that these new ships will lock in conventional fuel use, making future decarbonization far more challenging and costly.

## Implications for CII Ratings and Company Rankings

The current newbuilding spree, coupled with fuel uncertainty, has direct implications for future environmental compliance, particularly for [CII ratings](/cii.html). Vessels ordered without sufficient consideration for future low-carbon fuel pathways or energy efficiency will inevitably face lower CII ratings (D or E grades) as regulatory targets tighten. This will necessitate costly operational adjustments, speed reductions, or even early retirement, impacting the profitability and sustainability profiles of shipowners.

Companies that prioritize "optionality" and invest in future-proof vessel designs, or those that commit to proven low-carbon solutions, stand to improve their positioning in [company rankings](/companies.html) for emissions performance. Conversely, those that continue to rely heavily on conventional fuels without clear transition strategies risk seeing their emissions per tonne-mile worsen, leading to reputational damage and potential financial penalties under expanding carbon pricing mechanisms. ## What this means for shipping emissions

The latest analysis from GCMD, BCG, and insights from industry figures like Martin Stopford paint a clear, albeit challenging, picture. The maritime industry is investing heavily in its future fleet now, yet the economic signals required for a decisive shift away from fossil fuels are not strong enough. Without a significantly higher carbon price – potentially $700 per tonne of CO2 equivalent – conventional fuels, possibly with carbon capture, will continue to dominate the energy mix through 2050. This means that the ambitious decarbonization targets, and the industry's commitment to a 1.5-degree pathway, are at risk. The choices made on shipbuilding contracts today, concerning engine configurations and fuel optionality, will lock in global shipping emissions for the next quarter-century. For a truly green transition, policymakers must provide the sharp economic signals necessary to make new fuels competitive, ensuring that current investments align with a low-carbon future.
