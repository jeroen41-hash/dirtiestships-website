The global shipping industry, responsible for approximately 3% of annual global emissions—a footprint comparable to the economies of Germany or Japan—stands at a critical juncture in its decarbonization journey. The International Maritime Organization's (IMO) ambitious Net-Zero Framework (NZF), designed to steer the sector towards net-zero by 2050, has faced significant hurdles, underscoring the complexities of establishing a global carbon pricing mechanism. However, recent developments within the European Union's Emissions Trading System (EU ETS) offer a compelling blueprint for how regional regulations can not only coexist with, but actively support, a global framework, challenging the very arguments that stalled the NZF's initial adoption.

## The Stalled Global Mandate and Regional Pressures

In April 2025, the IMO agreed on the NZF, a landmark proposal aiming to set common global targets for reducing marine fuel carbon intensity and, crucially, introduce the world's first meaningful carbon pricing for a global industry. This mechanism was projected to raise $10-15 billion annually through fees on emissions exceeding targets, funding critical decarbonization efforts in developing countries. Yet, as reported by Hellenic Shipping News, the NZF's adoption stalled in October 2025, largely due to opposition from oil-producing states, including the US and Saudi Arabia. Their primary contention: regional carbon pricing measures, specifically the EU ETS for shipping, would create a "double burden" on the industry, obstructing a global agreement.

This failure left the industry craving investment clarity and exposed the vulnerability of states most impacted by climate change, lacking finance for their energy transition. While T&E analysis, cited in the report, suggests the NZF alone might not be sufficient to meet IMO's own ambitious climate targets, its global standard-setting potential remains undisputed.

## EU ETS: A Blueprint for Coexistence

As the IMO prepares for further discussions on the NZF in late 2026, the EU has proactively proposed significant adjustments to its ETS for shipping, directly addressing concerns about overlapping regulations. These changes send three clear signals that could pave the way for a more integrated global-regional approach:

1.  **No Double Pricing of Emissions:** The EU has affirmed that if the IMO establishes a carbon pricing scheme like the NZF, ships operating internationally will not be charged twice for the same emissions under the EU ETS. T&E analysis indicates that the current overlap is minimal, covering only about 15% of relevant emissions between the two potential schemes. The EU ETS is designed to adjust its scope as the NZF gradually increases its coverage, at least until 2040. This pragmatic approach eliminates a core argument against a global framework, providing clarity for shipping companies grappling with their [CII ratings](/cii.html) and overall emissions reduction strategies.

2.  **Alignment, Not Backsliding through "ETS as a Service":** Recognizing that the NZF may not fully close the emissions gap needed to meet IMO's targets, the EU is championing coordinated regional efforts. Currently, the EU ETS charges for 50% of emissions on voyages to and from its ports. The proposed "ETS as a service" initiative would enable EU trade partners to levy revenues on their own 50% shares via the existing EU system. This not only streamlines administrative burdens for partners but also ensures that regional efforts augment, rather than detract from, global decarbonization, leveraging robust EMSA MRV data infrastructure.

3.  **Equitable Transition Support:** Addressing concerns about the equitable distribution of decarbonization benefits, the EU plans to earmark approximately €100 million annually from ETS revenues to fund shipping decarbonization projects in Small Island Developing States (SIDS) and Least Developed Countries (LDCs). Furthermore, countries participating in the "ETS as a service" model could receive subsidies for green investments, fostering bilateral cooperation and ensuring that the financial burden of the transition does not disproportionately fall on vulnerable nations.

## Interaction in Practice: A Glimpse into the Future

The Hellenic Shipping News article illustrates the potential interaction with a hypothetical 2030 voyage from Tema, Ghana, to Algeciras, Spain, emitting 1080 tonnes of CO2 equivalent. Under the EU ETS, the vessel would be charged for 41% of its emissions. The NZF, with its more limited pricing mechanisms, would cover no more than 23%. Critically, only 9% of the total emissions would be liable under *both* policies – precisely the share that would be exempted under the revised EU ETS, effectively nullifying the "double burden" claim.

## Navigating the Political Tides at the IMO

Despite the EU's clarity and proactive measures, the path forward for the NZF remains politically charged. As the IMO meets this November and December, some nations continue to push for weaker targets, loopholes, or the prioritization of less sustainable options like LNG. However, the EU's latest proposals significantly diminish the justification for using regional carbon pricing as an excuse for global inaction.

## What this means for shipping emissions

The EU's revised stance on its ETS is a pivotal development, transforming a perceived barrier into a potential catalyst for global maritime decarbonization. By demonstrating how regional and global carbon pricing can not only coexist but mutually reinforce each other, the EU provides a clear pathway for the IMO's Net-Zero Framework. For the shipping industry, this clarity, coupled with financial support for vulnerable nations, signals a more robust and equitable trajectory towards net-zero.
