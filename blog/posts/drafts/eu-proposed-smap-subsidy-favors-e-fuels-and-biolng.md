The European Union continues to intensify its regulatory pressure on the shipping sector, with a new proposal set to significantly reshape the economic landscape for marine fuels. A revision to the EU Emissions Trading System (ETS) includes a Sustainable Maritime Alternative Propulsion (SMAP) mechanism, aiming to bridge the price gap between fossil fuels and sustainable alternatives. This initiative, analyzed by Rystad Energy and reported by hellenicshippingnews.com, signals a clear prioritization of certain green fuels over others, directly impacting future CO2 emissions, operational costs, and fleet investment strategies across the industry. For companies tracking their environmental footprint via [CII ratings](/cii.html) and managing escalating [ETS costs](/charts.html), understanding these evolving incentives is critical.

### The EU's Evolving Decarbonization Toolkit

The proposed SMAP mechanism, which reserves up to 110 million ETS allowances between 2028 and 2040, is designed to subsidize the transition to cleaner fuels. This is not a standalone policy but rather the latest layer in a complex regulatory framework. From 2026, shipping companies are mandated to surrender ETS allowances for 100% of their verified emissions within scope, now encompassing methane and nitrous oxide alongside CO2 – data we monitor closely via EMSA MRV disclosures. Simultaneously, FuelEU Maritime imposes a steadily tightening requirement for reductions in the greenhouse-gas intensity of energy consumed on board. Both these regimes are underpinned by the sustainability criteria of the EU’s revised Renewable Energy Directive (RED III). The SMAP proposal effectively introduces a tiered financial incentive, favoring specific fuel pathways over others and creating a powerful market signal.

### Shifting Fuel Economics: Winners Emerge

Rystad Energy's analysis highlights a dramatic shift in marine fuel economics, particularly for intra-EU voyages. The SMAP mechanism is structured to provide significant support to a select group of alternative fuels:

*   **BioLNG and Advanced Biofuels:** SMAP would cover 55% of the remaining price gap to fossil fuels.
*   **Renewable Fuels of Non-Biological Origin (RFNBOs):** Fuels like e-ammonia and e-methanol receive even more generous treatment, with 90% of their price gap covered, plus an additional 10 percentage points if the feedstock is sourced within the EU. The article suggests EU-produced e-ammonia could potentially see its *entire* remaining premium covered.

When combined with FuelEU Maritime credits, these incentives can push alternative fuels significantly below the cost of very low sulfur fuel oil (VLSFO). According to the article, BioLNG, despite costing more than twice as much as VLSFO at 3Q26 prices before incentives, could end up at about half the cost of VLSFO on intra-EU voyages with the full suite of support. This significantly strengthens the business case for LNG dual-fuel ships operating in Europe, affecting investment decisions for many companies featured in our [company rankings](/companies.html). While RFNBOs receive the most generous support on paper, their current market readiness and production scale remain limited.

### The Clear Losers: Waste-Based Liquid Biofuels

In contrast to the strong support for bioLNG and RFNBOs, the SMAP proposal delivers a blow to liquid biofuels derived from waste oils and fats, such as used cooking oil (UCO) and animal fats. Despite meeting all EU sustainability tests and earning full credit under both ETS and FuelEU Maritime, these widely adopted drop-in biofuels are excluded from SMAP support. The reason for this exclusion is specific: SMAP only supports advanced biofuels made from feedstocks listed in Annex IX Part A of RED III.

This exclusion is particularly impactful given the current structure of Europe's biodiesel industry. Projections indicate that by 2030, crops and waste lipids like UCO and other waste fats and oils are expected to account for approximately 96% of the region’s biodiesel and renewable diesel production capacity. This leaves only around 2% clearly eligible for SMAP support. Furthermore, the growing mandate for sustainable aviation fuel (SAF) is drawing on the same limited pool of waste lipids, making it unlikely that shipping will secure substantial volumes of eligible liquid biofuel for SMAP. This creates a challenging outlook for companies that may have seen UCO-based biofuels as a viable immediate strategy for improving their [CII ratings](/cii.html) and reducing [ETS costs](/charts.html).

### Strategic Implications for Fleet Investment and Operations

The EU's layered regulatory approach, especially with the introduction of SMAP, sends a powerful signal to shipowners, charterers, and fuel suppliers. The incentives strongly favor significant capital investment in LNG dual-fuel vessels and, looking further ahead, ships capable of burning e-ammonia or e-methanol, particularly for fleets engaged in intra-EU trade. The halving of support for voyages into or out of the EU, and the preference for EU-produced fuels, adds further complexity for global operators, necessitating a nuanced approach to fuel sourcing and fleet deployment.

Companies must meticulously evaluate their long-term fuel strategies. Those with existing LNG-ready or dual-fuel fleets may find themselves in an advantageous position, benefiting from reduced operational costs for compliant fuels. Conversely, operators who relied on waste-based liquid biofuels for their decarbonization pathway may need to reassess their options to avoid potential cost disadvantages and ensure compliance with tightening GHG intensity targets. Our [emissions data](/charts.html) and [company rankings](/companies.html) will increasingly reflect these strategic shifts, penalizing those who fail to adapt effectively.

### What this means for shipping emissions

The EU's proposed SMAP subsidy marks a significant, albeit targeted, intervention in the market for alternative marine fuels. By strongly incentivizing specific pathways like bioLNG and e-fuels while excluding others such as UCO-based biodiesels, the EU is attempting to direct significant investment towards the most ambitious decarbonization technologies. While this could accelerate the development and adoption of truly zero-emission fuels in the long run, it simultaneously creates financial hurdles for more readily available 'drop-in' solutions. The overall impact on shipping emissions will depend heavily on the industry's ability to scale the production and uptake of these favored fuels, and how effectively companies integrate these complex financial incentives into their long-term operational and investment strategies. The journey towards a truly sustainable shipping sector remains fraught with regulatory complexity and evolving economic realities.
