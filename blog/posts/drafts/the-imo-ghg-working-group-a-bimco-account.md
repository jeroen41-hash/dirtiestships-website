The global shipping industry, responsible for approximately 3% of global greenhouse gas emissions, faces increasing pressure to decarbonize, with the International Maritime Organization (IMO) tasked with developing the framework. However, a recent account from BIMCO following the 22nd intersessional working group meeting on GHG reduction reveals that despite the ambitious 2023 IMO GHG Strategy, crucial disagreements on mid-term measures persist, potentially slowing the pace of necessary emissions reductions and casting a long shadow over the industry's net-zero ambitions.

### The Enduring Stalemate: IMO's Mid-Term Measures

The latest IMO working group meeting, held from 1-4 September, was the first under new terms of reference aiming to forge a broader consensus on the Net-Zero Framework (NZF) after its fierce opposition at MEPC 84. While delegations reportedly engaged constructively, BIMCO's observation suggests "disagreements on several crucial issues relating to a possible future IMO mid-term measure persist." Notably, there appears to be a lack of universal agreement on the 2023 IMO Strategy's ambition and indicative checkpoints, a worrying sign for a roadmap intended to guide the industry towards net-zero by or around 2050. The article indicates that convergence on these core issues by December — when the next meeting is scheduled — seems challenging, highlighting the complexity of achieving global consensus among diverse member states.

### Navigating the GFI Trajectory: Shape and Stringency

A central point of contention lies in the design of the Greenhouse Gas Fuel Intensity (GFI) trajectory itself. The debate revolves around whether it should adopt a 2-tier structure, as initially proposed in the NZF, or a single-tier approach, similar to the FuelEUMaritime concept. This isn't merely a technicality; it’s about the fundamental regulatory approach. Should the trajectory aggressively drive the availability of green fuels, or should it be shaped by the current availability, scalability, and affordability of fuels in the market?

This discussion directly parallels the challenges faced with existing intensity metrics like the IMO's Carbon Intensity Indicator (CII). Our [CII ratings](/cii.html) tool demonstrates how a single-tier, fleet-average approach can create complex compliance scenarios. A global GFI, whether single or two-tier, would aim to enforce a similar intensity reduction across the global fleet, building upon the principles of energy efficiency measures like EEXI and CII, but with far greater stringency. The stringency of this trajectory will dictate how quickly ships on our [company rankings](/companies.html) need to adapt their operations or invest in new technologies to avoid becoming non-compliant.

### The Carbon Funding Conundrum: Levies and Distribution

Beyond the GFI trajectory's shape, the generation and spending of funds within the framework remain deeply divisive. The NZF’s original 2-tier GFI structure was designed with the distinct ability to generate substantial funds, envisioned to be channelled through a central IMO-operated mechanism. This concept faces strong opposition from several member states who view it as infringing on their sovereign right to levy taxes.

Conversely, other states strongly advocate for the fund-generating aspect, seeing it as critical for facilitating a "just and equitable transition" (JET) and compensating eligible states for "disproportionate negative impacts" (DNI). This core disagreement, according to BIMCO, appears to have widened significantly.

This debate has direct parallels to the European Union's Emission Trading System (EU ETS), which already imposes a carbon cost on shipping within its jurisdiction. While the EU ETS primarily channels funds towards the general EU budget or member states for climate-related projects, an IMO-level carbon levy would aim for a global mechanism. The lack of consensus on a global fund mechanism at the IMO prolongs uncertainty for shipowners regarding future operating costs and potential revenue streams for decarbonization investments, a key factor we track in our [emissions data](/charts.html) analysis.

### Implications for Fuel Transition and Asset Risk

The debate extends to how any generated funds would be spent. Should they be entirely re-invested in the shipping sector to accelerate green fuel development and uptake, or should states have discretion over their use? Furthermore, the question of whether zero or near-zero fuels and technologies should be rewarded, perhaps through a "multiplier" effect, is another area of divergence. Such incentives are crucial for de-risking investments in alternative fuels and propulsion systems.

BIMCO highlights a critical practical implication: a purely technical measure without an economic "safety-valve" (like the ability to pay for compliance) risks rendering ships "stranded assets" unable to trade if over- and under-compliance don't balance. This underscores the urgency of a comprehensive framework that includes both technical and economic elements to support the transition, preventing economic disruption and ensuring a level playing field. Without clarity on these mechanisms, investment decisions for future fleets and fuels remain speculative, directly impacting the emissions profiles of companies.

### What This Means for Shipping Emissions

The ongoing disagreements at the IMO's GHG working group signal continued uncertainty for the global shipping industry. While constructive engagement is reported, the fundamental differences on issues like the GFI trajectory's stringency, the generation of carbon funds, and their equitable distribution mean that a cohesive, globally accepted mid-term measure remains elusive. This slow pace of policy development risks delaying critical investment in decarbonization technologies and alternative fuels, potentially hindering the industry's ability to meet its emissions reduction targets. For stakeholders tracking actual emissions and compliance costs, this underscores the critical role of regional regulations like the EU ETS in driving change in the interim, and the need for continued vigilance through platforms like DirtiestShips.com to monitor real-world progress and hold the industry accountable.
