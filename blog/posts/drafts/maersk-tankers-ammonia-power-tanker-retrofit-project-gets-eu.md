The global shipping industry, under mounting pressure to decarbonize, has seen a significant development with Maersk Tankers' "Ripple Project" securing funding from the EU Innovation Fund. This pioneering initiative to retrofit Very Large Ammonia Carriers (VLACs) for ammonia fuel use represents a tangible step towards cleaner maritime operations, directly addressing the urgent need to slash CO2 emissions and navigate stringent environmental regulations like the EU Emissions Trading System (ETS) and the Carbon Intensity Indicator (CII). With a projected avoidance of over 1.2 million metric tonnes of CO2e over 10 years from just four retrofitted vessels, this project underscores the transformative potential of alternative fuels in reshaping the emissions profile of one of the world's dirtiest industries.

## The EU Innovation Fund: Catalysing Decarbonization

The EU Innovation Fund plays a critical role in accelerating the transition to a low-carbon economy by reinvesting revenues generated from the EU Emissions Trading System (ETS) into innovative decarbonization technologies. According to maritime-executive.com, Maersk Tankers’ Ripple Project was among 77 additional projects selected for funding in a round totaling €3.4 billion. This direct link between carbon pricing mechanisms like the EU ETS and the funding of cutting-edge green technologies illustrates the EU's strategic approach to driving down emissions. For shipping companies, the rising cost of carbon allowances under ETS provides a powerful financial incentive to invest in cleaner alternatives, making projects like Ripple not just environmentally beneficial but increasingly economically rational.

## Maersk Tankers' "Ripple Project": A Deep Dive

Maersk Tankers’ "Ripple Project" specifically targets the retrofit of four existing VLACs, equipping them with dual-fuel ammonia two-stroke engines. This marks a first-of-its-kind retrofit in the EU for this vessel type, aiming to demonstrate the commercial viability of ammonia-fueled operations. While Maersk Tankers is also taking delivery of new VLACs designed to transport liquefied ammonia or LPG (such as the 93,000 cbm Jane Maersk and Jessie Maersk), the Ripple Project focuses on adapting *existing* vessels to *consume* ammonia as a marine fuel. The projected avoidance of over 1.2 million metric tonnes of CO2e over a decade from these four ships highlights the significant emissions reduction achievable through such conversions, offering a blueprint for the wider fleet and demonstrating how substantial investments in vessel upgrades can lead to measurable environmental gains.

## Beyond the Project: Implications for CII and ETS

The adoption of ammonia as a marine fuel carries profound implications for regulatory compliance, particularly for the Carbon Intensity Indicator (CII) and EU ETS costs. Vessels operating on ammonia, especially green ammonia derived from renewable energy, would see a dramatic improvement in their operational carbon intensity, potentially elevating their [CII ratings](/cii.html) from undesirable D or E grades to highly compliant A or B categories. This directly impacts a vessel's market competitiveness and avoids potential penalties under the IMO's CII framework.

Furthermore, the 1.2 million tonnes of CO2e avoided by the Ripple Project translates directly into substantial cost savings for Maersk Tankers by reducing their exposure to EU ETS carbon allowance purchases. As carbon prices continue to fluctuate and generally trend upwards, innovative projects like this mitigate financial risks associated with high-emission operations. Such initiatives are crucial for improving a company's standing in [company rankings](/companies.html) that increasingly factor in environmental performance and carbon footprint. This project offers a tangible example of how regulatory pressure is driving the transformation of [emissions data](/charts.html) across the maritime sector.

## The Broader Ammonia Fuel Landscape

Maersk Tankers' initiative is part of a burgeoning industry trend towards ammonia as a marine fuel. DNV calculates there are now 41 ammonia-fueled vessels on order globally, indicating a growing commitment across the sector. Companies like Exmar have already taken delivery of ammonia-fueled gas carriers, and CMB.TECH recently highlighted its first ammonia-fueled bulkers. Eastern Pacific Shipping celebrated the naming of its own 93,000 cbm Very Large Ammonia Carrier powered by the first Everllence B&W ammonia dual-fuel two-stroke engine. While challenges remain concerning ammonia infrastructure, safety protocols, and the scalable production of green ammonia, the rapid progression from pilot projects to actual vessel orders and retrofits signals a clear industry direction. These developments, documented on our [news feed](/news.html), demonstrate a collective push towards practical, scalable decarbonization solutions.

### What this means for shipping emissions

Maersk Tankers' EU-funded Ripple Project is more than just a single company's endeavor; it's a powerful indicator of the maritime industry's accelerated shift towards decarbonization. By demonstrating the commercial viability and significant emissions reduction potential of ammonia as a marine fuel for existing vessels, this project provides a critical pathway for the vast global fleet. It underscores how robust regulatory frameworks like the EU ETS, coupled with targeted innovation funding, are essential drivers in compelling companies to adopt cleaner technologies. As more projects like Ripple come online, we can anticipate a tangible and measurable reduction in shipping's overall CO2 footprint, reshaping the future of maritime emissions.
