The ongoing evolution of the EU Emissions Trading System (ETS) is set to reshape the economic landscape for global shipping, with billions in revenue projected from carbon costs. As the European Commission discusses targeted revisions to the ETS framework, a critical debate is emerging over how these substantial funds should be allocated to genuinely drive decarbonisation. While shipowners largely welcome the earmarking of these revenues for shipping, a key voice, PortXchange, is sounding the alarm, arguing that the current focus on alternative fuels and onboard technologies overlooks an immediate and potent avenue for emissions reduction: ports and operational efficiency. This perspective has profound implications for how effectively the industry can reduce its carbon footprint, directly impacting everything from [CII ratings](/cii.html) to the overall effectiveness of EU climate targets.

## The Core Debate: Earmarking ETS Revenues

The European Commission's recent discussions regarding a targeted revision of the EU Emissions Trading System aim to bolster European industrial competitiveness and ensure the EU meets its ambitious 2040 climate target. European Shipowners (ECSA) have generally supported the proposal to earmark ETS revenues specifically for shipping decarbonisation efforts. This is a crucial step, ensuring that funds generated from the industry’s emissions are reinvested into its green transition. However, ECSA also cautioned that the current approach risks leaving many vital energy-efficiency projects and proven clean technologies outside the funding scope.

PortXchange, as reported by hellenicshippingnews.com, echoes this concern but goes further, asserting that ports cannot be relegated to the sidelines. Sjoerd de Jager, Managing Director & Co-Founder, PortXchange, states, "Earmarking shipping revenues for shipping decarbonisation is absolutely the right direction. But ports cannot be treated as spectators in this transition. They are one of the few places where emissions from today’s fleet can be understood, influenced and reduced immediately." This highlights a significant disconnect: collecting billions from shipping emissions while potentially overlooking some of the quickest and most actionable measures to cut those emissions now.

## Beyond Fuels: The Untapped Potential of Port Operations

Much of the shipping industry's decarbonisation narrative rightly focuses on the long-term transition to sustainable alternative fuels like ammonia, methanol, or hydrogen. However, PortXchange’s argument underscores that this focus often overshadows the immediate gains achievable through operational improvements. Better voyage planning, enhanced port-call coordination, and streamlined information sharing between vessels and ports can drastically reduce avoidable waiting times, unnecessary acceleration, and congestion-related emissions.

"Most vessel emissions occur during the voyage," de Jager notes, "But some of the quickest opportunities to reduce emissions are found in the final stages of a port call." By providing vessels with reliable, real-time information on berth availability and operational readiness, ships can adjust their speed, optimise their approach, and significantly reduce fuel consumption, avoiding costly and carbon-intensive delays at anchor. Such operational efficiencies directly contribute to lower CO2 emissions per nautical mile, positively influencing a vessel's [CII ratings](/cii.html) and improving a company's standing in [company rankings](/companies.html).

## Immediate Impact: Emissions Reductions for Today's Fleet

A critical point raised by PortXchange is the longevity of the existing global fleet. Many vessels operating today are expected to remain in service well into the 2040s and beyond. Waiting solely for the next generation of zero-emission ships or the widespread availability of green fuels means overlooking a decade or more of potential emissions reductions from the current fleet.

Digital emissions intelligence, coupled with port-call optimisation tools, enables ports and shipping companies to pinpoint where emissions occur, understand their drivers, and implement targeted interventions. These are not futuristic technologies but rather proven strategies that can deliver measurable reductions across the current global fleet. Ignoring these "operational waste" opportunities in favour of exclusive funding for future fuels, which remain expensive, scarce, and uncertain, represents a significant missed opportunity for tangible, near-term environmental impact.

## Data and Consistency: Key to a Level Playing Field

Beyond the allocation of funds, PortXchange also stresses the necessity of consistent emissions reporting. For any ETS-funded project to demonstrate meaningful progress, there must be a common and reliable methodology for measuring reductions. "We cannot talk seriously about a level playing field while every port is measuring a slightly different race," de Jager cautions.

This call for consistency directly aligns with the objectives of platforms like DirtiestShips.com, which leverage EU MRV data and other verifiable sources to provide transparent emissions insights. Robust, consistent data is fundamental not only for regulatory compliance but also for enabling ports, regulators, and customers to accurately assess whether decarbonisation efforts are genuinely yielding results. Without a unified approach to measurement, the impact of even the best-intentioned funding allocations will be difficult to verify or compare, hindering overall progress and accountability within the industry. ## What this means for shipping emissions

PortXchange's stance underscores a critical truth: the path to decarbonising shipping is multifaceted, demanding immediate action on operational efficiencies alongside the long-term transition to alternative fuels. By advocating for the inclusion of port-side and operational optimisation measures in ETS revenue allocation, PortXchange is pushing for a more holistic and pragmatic approach. Embracing these "quickest opportunities" can lead to significant, measurable reductions in CO2 emissions from today’s fleet, improving individual vessel performance and company-wide environmental profiles, and making a substantial dent in global shipping emissions well before future fuels become universally viable.
