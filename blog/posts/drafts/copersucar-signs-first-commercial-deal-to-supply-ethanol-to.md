The global shipping industry, under mounting pressure to decarbonize, has seen a significant step forward with Copersucar, Bunker One, and CMA CGM signing the world’s first commercial contract to supply ethanol to ships. This landmark agreement, detailed by valorinternational.globo.com, signals a tangible shift towards alternative fuels, directly impacting vessel carbon intensity, compliance with regulations like the EU ETS, and ultimately, company emissions profiles tracked by platforms like DirtiestShips.com. The move underscores the growing economic viability of lower-carbon options when factoring in the escalating costs of carbon emissions, offering a critical pathway for operators striving to improve their [CII ratings](/cii.html) and reduce their environmental footprint.

### Ethanol: A Viable Low-Carbon Fuel Option

The agreement involves seven bunkering operations by the end of the year, supplying an estimated 10 million liters of anhydrous ethanol. While this represents only a partial fuel load for vessels, it marks a crucial commercial-scale deployment. CMA CGM, a prominent global shipping line whose environmental performance is closely monitored in our [company rankings](/companies.html), is directly involved, utilizing a 13,000 TEU container ship for the initial operation. According to Copersucar CEO Tomás Manzano, ethanol offers a carbon footprint 75% lower than very low sulfur fuel oil (VLSFO), a significant reduction that directly addresses the industry's challenge to meet greenhouse gas emission targets. This substantial reduction positions ethanol as an immediately available and low-carbon alternative, essential for an industry grappling with 2-3% of global emissions.

### The Economics of Decarbonization: ETS and Abatement Costs

Historically, the higher upfront cost of alternative fuels has been a barrier to adoption. The article reveals that anhydrous ethanol costs $300 to $400 per tonne more than VLSFO on an equivalent energy basis. However, the game-changer lies in the cost of carbon. When factoring in the expense of purchasing carbon credits to offset emissions, estimated at $100 to $130 per tonne of carbon, ethanol becomes competitive. As Manzano explains, replacing one tonne of bunker fuel with ethanol reduces emissions by three tonnes of carbon. This direct correlation highlights the increasing influence of carbon pricing mechanisms, such as the EU Emissions Trading System (ETS), which levies a cost on CO2 emissions from shipping. For vessels operating within or to/from EU ports, the economic incentive to adopt fuels with lower carbon intensity becomes ever more compelling, directly impacting operational profitability and fleet strategy.

### Impact on CII Ratings and Fleet Strategy

The adoption of ethanol, with its 75% lower carbon intensity, can profoundly impact a vessel's operational carbon intensity indicator (CII) rating. Vessels are increasingly scrutinized for their Annual Efficiency Ratio (AER), which directly feeds into their CII. Utilizing lower-carbon fuels directly improves this metric, helping ships achieve better ratings (A, B, or C) and avoid penalties or corrective actions associated with D or E ratings. The contract targets tri-fuel vessels, capable of running on VLSFO, methanol, and ethanol, with 70-100 such vessels currently in service and another 300 on order for delivery by 2030. Furthermore, the article points to the growing viability of retrofitting existing methanol-powered or even conventional bunker-fuel vessels during scheduled dry-dockings. This flexibility in fuel choice and vessel adaptation offers operators a cost-effective pathway to extend asset lifespan while complying with evolving environmental regulations, rather than being forced into expensive newbuilds.

### Scaling Up: Supply Chains and Future Projections

This initial commercial agreement is just the beginning. Copersucar aims to scale its supply to 200 million liters for maritime transport by 2027. Achieving this requires significant logistical development, already underway with Bunker One adapting barges for ethanol transport at the Port of Santos. The ambition extends beyond Brazil, with Copersucar targeting key global bunkering hubs like Singapore and the United States, which handle vastly greater cargo volumes. This expansion is critical to establishing ethanol as a widely available marine fuel, addressing a common barrier for alternative fuel adoption: the global availability of bunkering infrastructure. As more suppliers and ports come online, the data captured by [emissions data](/charts.html) platforms will reflect a broader uptake, providing clearer insights into the industry's decarbonization progress.

### What this means for shipping emissions

This commercial deal for ethanol bunkering is a tangible step towards decarbonizing shipping. It demonstrates that alternative, lower-carbon fuels are not just theoretical options but are becoming economically viable and operationally feasible today, especially when the escalating cost of carbon emissions is factored in. For DirtiestShips.com, this development suggests that while the journey to zero emissions is long, the transition to fuels like ethanol offers an immediate, impactful pathway to reduce the industry's significant CO2 footprint, improve individual vessel [CII ratings](/cii.html), and directly influence [company rankings](/companies.html) as the EU ETS and other global regulations tighten their grip. It signals a future where fuel choices are increasingly driven by environmental performance and carbon costs, rather than solely by conventional energy prices.
