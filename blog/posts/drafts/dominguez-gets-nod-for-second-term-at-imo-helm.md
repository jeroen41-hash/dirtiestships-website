The potential extension of Arsenio Dominguez's tenure at the International Maritime Organization (IMO) until the end of 2031 places the Panamanian directly at the helm during what is arguably the most critical decade for shipping decarbonisation. As the global shipping industry grapples with the imperative to reduce its substantial CO2 footprint, the IMO's ability to forge a unified, impactful regulatory framework under Dominguez's continued leadership will determine the pace and equity of global emissions reduction efforts, directly impacting operational costs and environmental performance across the world's fleets.

## IMO Leadership and the Decarbonisation Agenda

Arsenio Dominguez, who commenced his term as Secretary-General in January 2024, is now poised for a potential second term from January 2028 to December 2031, pending formal approval in late 2027. This extended leadership comes as the IMO navigates deeply divided positions among its member states regarding its ambitious Net-Zero Framework. This framework, initially agreed upon in principle in April 2025, aims to introduce a global marine fuel standard and an emissions pricing mechanism – two pillars crucial for driving systemic change in shipping's CO2 output.

However, progress has been fraught. The *splash247.com* article highlights a significant setback in October (implied 2026), when governments, notably led by the United States and Saudi Arabia, voted to delay the framework's adoption by a year. This delay underscores the complex political dynamics and competing national interests that hinder global climate action. Efforts to revive the agreement continue, with critical negotiations slated for the Marine Environment Protection Committee (MEPC) meeting from November 30 to December 3, followed by an extraordinary session on December 4. The outcome of these talks will be pivotal for the future trajectory of global shipping emissions.

## The Global Emissions Pricing Conundrum

The IMO's struggle to implement a global emissions pricing mechanism stands in stark contrast to regional initiatives already in force. While the IMO framework faces delays, the European Union has moved ahead with the inclusion of shipping in its Emissions Trading System (EU ETS). This means vessels calling at EU ports are now subject to carbon costs for their CO2 emissions, a tangible financial incentive for decarbonisation that a global IMO mechanism aims to replicate and expand. The absence of a uniform global price on carbon creates market distortions and complicates strategic planning for international carriers.

A globally agreed fuel standard and emissions pricing mechanism would fundamentally alter the operational and financial landscape for every vessel. Such regulations would directly influence the [CII ratings](/cii.html) of ships, pushing owners to invest in more efficient technologies, alternative fuels, or slower steaming to maintain competitive operational grades. For companies, the clarity and predictability of a global framework are essential for long-term investments in fleet modernisation. Without it, companies are left to react to a patchwork of regional regulations, potentially impacting their overall standing in [company rankings](/companies.html) for emissions performance. DirtiestShips.com, leveraging EU MRV data, provides transparency on these regional impacts, but a global system would offer a more comprehensive dataset for analysis.

## Broader Challenges and Their Emissions Implications

Beyond decarbonisation, Dominguez's second term would also see him addressing escalating maritime security challenges. The article notes ongoing attacks and disruptions in vital shipping lanes such as the Strait of Hormuz, Red Sea, and Black Sea. While distinct from environmental concerns, these security issues have direct implications for global shipping emissions. Rerouting vessels away from conflict zones, as seen with diversions around Africa instead of through the Suez Canal, significantly increases voyage lengths, fuel consumption, and, consequently, total CO2 emissions. This demonstrates how geopolitical instability can inadvertently undermine environmental progress, adding layers of complexity to the emissions reduction challenge.

## What this means for shipping emissions

Arsenio Dominguez's potential extended term signifies a period of continued high-stakes negotiation for global shipping decarbonisation. The protracted delays in adopting the IMO's Net-Zero Framework underscore the immense difficulty of achieving global consensus on emissions pricing and fuel standards. While regional efforts like the EU ETS continue to drive specific segments of the fleet towards lower emissions, the overall trajectory of global shipping's CO2 footprint remains highly dependent on the IMO's ability to overcome political hurdles. Without a robust, globally applicable framework, the industry risks fragmented regulations, uneven progress, and a slower-than-necessary transition towards net-zero. The upcoming MEPC meetings are therefore not just about political agreements; they are about defining the future emissions profile of an entire industry.
