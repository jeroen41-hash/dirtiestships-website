The global shipping industry's decarbonization trajectory faces a critical juncture as Japan tables proposals at MEPC 85 that could significantly soften the IMO's Greenhouse Gas Fuel Intensity (GFI) reduction targets and fundamentally alter its carbon pricing mechanism. These proposed amendments, if adopted, could redefine the pace of emission reductions from 2030 onwards, potentially impacting the industry's ability to meet its net-zero ambitions and creating a stark contrast with more stringent regional regulations like the EU ETS.

## Easing the GFI Reduction Pathway

The IMO's Net-Zero Framework (NZF), approved at MEPC 83, introduced a two-tier GFI standard, mandating ships to progressively reduce well-to-wake GHG emissions. Benchmark targets, set against a 2008 baseline of 93.30 gCO2e/MJ, currently range from 4-17% in 2028 and tighten to 30-43% by 2035. Japan's submission, however, advocates for a less aggressive reduction trajectory from 2030 onwards, while maintaining the 2028-2029 targets.

Specifically, Japan proposes easing the 2030 base target from 85.84 gCO2e/MJ to 86.8 gCO2e/MJ. More significantly, the 2035 base target would soften from 65.31 gCO2e/MJ to 72.9 gCO2e/MJ. Similar adjustments are proposed for the direct compliance target, easing it from 73.71 gCO2e/MJ to 74.6 gCO2e/MJ in 2030, and from 53.18 gCO2e/MJ to 60.7 gCO2e/MJ in 2035. An alternative flat direct compliance target of 72.9 gCO2e/MJ from 2028-2035 is also presented, which would be stricter than the current draft through 2030, but looser thereafter. Furthermore, Japan suggests removing the 65% base target for 2040 entirely, deferring its setting until 2032. This represents a substantial loosening of the IMO's planned decarbonization targets, potentially delaying necessary investments in cleaner fuels and technologies.

## From Collective Fund to "Direct Contributions"

Perhaps even more impactful is Japan's proposed overhaul of the IMO's carbon pricing mechanism. The original NZF stipulated that ships missing their GFI targets would purchase "remedial units" – paying $100/mtCO2e for Tier 1 emissions and $380/mtCO2e for Tier 2 – into an IMO Net-Zero Fund. Over-complying ships could earn and trade surplus units.

Japan proposes removing the opportunity to purchase remedial units entirely. Instead, ships that fail to meet GFI targets would make "direct contributions" to eligible projects of their own choosing, rather than contributing to a central fund. While the pricing for these direct contributions remains the same ($100/mtCO2e for Tier 1, $380/mtCO2e for Tier 2), the shift from a collective, centrally managed fund to individual, self-directed project funding fundamentally alters the framework. This also involves dropping regulation 39, which provided rewards for zero and near-zero GHG emission technologies and fuels (ZNZs) from the IMO Net-Zero Fund, essentially removing a key incentive for innovation within the IMO's financial mechanism. This shift could dilute collective responsibility and potentially fragment efforts to scale up industry-wide decarbonization solutions.

## Divergence from Regional Standards and Impact on Emissions Tracking

The proposed easing of IMO targets creates an even wider divergence between global aspirations and regional mandates, particularly in the EU. While IMO debates softened GFI targets, shipping companies operating in the EU already face escalating carbon costs under the [EU ETS](https://www.eumrv.eu/). Our analysis of [EMSA MRV data](https://www.eumrv.eu/) clearly shows the direct financial impact of CO2 emissions for vessels calling at European ports, an obligation that remains regardless of IMO's GFI trajectory.

Softer IMO targets could, paradoxically, place greater pressure on company-specific efforts and transparency. With a potentially less demanding global framework, the performance of individual shipping companies becomes even more critical for platforms like DirtiestShips.com. Our [CII ratings tool](/cii.html) and [company emissions rankings](/companies.html) will continue to highlight operational efficiency and actual emissions performance, providing crucial context regardless of the IMO's pace. The current proposals, if adopted, could mean that companies relying solely on minimum IMO compliance might find themselves lagging in competitiveness and facing higher costs in regions with more robust carbon pricing.

## What this means for shipping emissions

Japan's proposals at MEPC 85 signal a potential move towards a less ambitious, more flexible global regulatory framework for shipping emissions. While intended to foster consensus, the proposed easing of GFI targets and the shift to direct contributions could slow the overall pace of decarbonization, particularly from 2030 onwards. This creates a regulatory gap that may need to be filled by more stringent regional policies and increased pressure from stakeholders demanding greater transparency and accountability from individual shipping companies. DirtiestShips.com will continue to leverage [emissions data](/charts.html) from sources like EU MRV to provide an unbiased view of actual performance, emphasizing that despite potential shifts in IMO ambition, the imperative to reduce CO2 emissions remains urgent and measurable.
