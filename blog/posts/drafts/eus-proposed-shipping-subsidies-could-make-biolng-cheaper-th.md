The European Union is poised to significantly reconfigure the economics of marine fuel, potentially subsidizing certain low-carbon alternatives to the point where they undercut fossil fuels. A recent proposal from the European Commission to revise the EU Emissions Trading System (ETS) includes a new Sustainable Maritime Alternative Propulsion (SMAP) mechanism, designed to bridge the price gap between traditional marine fuels and more sustainable options. This development, analyzed by Rystad Energy, signals a crucial shift for the global shipping industry, impacting everything from fleet investment strategies to the effective cost of compliance with stringent emissions regulations like FuelEU Maritime and the EU ETS. For a data-driven platform like DirtiestShips.com, understanding these regulatory nuances is essential for tracking actual emissions reductions and holding companies accountable for their carbon footprint, as reported through mechanisms like EMSA MRV data.

### Reshaping the Fuel Cost Landscape with SMAP

The proposed SMAP mechanism earmarks up to 110 million ETS allowances between 2028 and 2040. This substantial allocation is intended to cover a portion of the price differential for sustainable marine fuels. When combined with existing policies like the EU ETS, which from 2026 will require shipping companies to surrender allowances for 100% of their verified emissions (including methane and nitrous oxide), and FuelEU Maritime's tightening greenhouse gas intensity targets, the competitive landscape for marine fuels is set for a radical overhaul.

According to Rystad Energy, SMAP offers varying levels of support: 55% of the remaining price gap to fossil fuel for biogas and advanced biofuels, and a more generous 90% for renewable fuels of non-biological origin (RFNBOs) such as e-ammonia and e-methanol. An additional 10 percentage points of support are available if the feedstock is sourced within the EU. This tiered approach inherently favors the most expensive, yet potentially most impactful, alternative fuels, with EU-made e-ammonia theoretically seeing its entire premium covered.

### BioLNG and RFNBOs: The New Contenders

The most striking finding from Rystad's analysis is the potential cost competitiveness of bioLNG. While bioLNG currently costs more than twice as much as Very Low Sulfur Fuel Oil (VLSFO) before incentives, the combined effect of FuelEU credits and the proposed SMAP subsidy could see it trading at approximately half the cost of VLSFO on intra-EU voyages. Crucially, much of this advantage already stems from FuelEU Maritime, with bioLNG capable of undercutting VLSFO even without SMAP.

This robust economic case for bioLNG significantly strengthens the business argument for LNG dual-fuel ships operating within European waters. Companies that have already invested in LNG-capable fleets could see a substantial competitive advantage in operational costs. RFNBOs, while receiving the most generous subsidy, face current limitations. Despite e-ammonia potentially becoming the cheapest fuel on intra-EU routes on paper, very few ships are currently capable of burning ammonia, and EU production capacity remains nascent. These fuels represent a long-term goal for decarbonization, but their immediate impact is constrained by technology readiness and infrastructure.

### The Overlooked: Waste-Oil Biodiesels Face a Widening Gap

In stark contrast to bioLNG and RFNBOs, liquid biofuels derived from waste oils and fats, such as used cooking oil (UCO) and animal fats, emerge as clear losers under the SMAP proposal. Despite these fuels meeting all EU sustainability criteria and earning full credit under the ETS and FuelEU Maritime, SMAP exclusively supports advanced biofuels made from feedstocks listed in Annex IX Part A of the EU’s revised Renewable Energy Directive (RED III).

This exclusion is highly significant given Europe's biodiesel industry structure. By 2030, crops and waste lipids like UCO are projected to account for approximately 96% of the region’s biodiesel and renewable diesel production capacity, leaving only about 2% clearly eligible for SMAP support. This regulatory blind spot effectively creates a widening cost gap for shipowners and charterers who have relied on these widely available and compliant biofuels for their decarbonization efforts. Moreover, shipping will be competing with aviation's sustainable fuel mandates for the same limited pool of eligible waste lipids, intensifying supply pressures. This situation underscores the complexity for companies aiming to improve their [CII ratings](/cii.html) through biofuel adoption.

### Budget Limitations and the Path Ahead

While SMAP promises significant incentives, its budget is fixed at around $900 million per year if spread evenly. Claims are subject to pro-rata cuts if oversubscribed, introducing an element of uncertainty for shipowners who will only learn the exact support per tonne after the year ends. Furthermore, mass balance rules limit bioLNG supply to bunkers from grid-connected liquefaction within the EU, potentially constraining availability.

The proposal is still undergoing negotiation with the European Parliament and Council, meaning final rules could yet evolve to narrow eligibility or adjust compensation levels. However, the overarching direction for shipowners operating in European trades is unequivocal: strategic fuel choices that align with the combined incentives of the ETS, FuelEU Maritime, and SMAP will dictate economic viability and competitive advantage.

### What This Means for Shipping Emissions

The EU's proposed SMAP mechanism, integrated with existing regulations, is a powerful signal guiding the shipping industry towards specific decarbonization pathways. While it offers a significant boost for bioLNG and nascent RFNBOs, it simultaneously creates a disadvantage for commonly used waste-oil biodiesels. This intricate regulatory environment demands sophisticated fleet management and fuel procurement strategies from shipowners and operators. For our readers tracking the industry's environmental performance, this means observing a complex, policy-driven shift in fuel consumption patterns, with a clear incentive for investment in LNG dual-fuel vessels and early adoption of certain e-fuels in European waters. Real emissions reductions will depend on the successful scaling of these supported alternative fuels.
