The ambitious goal of the EU Emissions Trading System (EU ETS) to attribute the cost of carbon emissions to the parties with direct operational control is facing significant headwinds, according to a new academic study. This finding casts a shadow over the mechanism intended to directly incentivize decarbonization within the shipping industry, potentially undermining a core pillar of Europe's efforts to clean up maritime transport. For a data-driven platform like DirtiestShips.com, which tracks real-world emissions, CII ratings, and carbon costs, this development highlights a critical disconnect between regulatory intent and practical enforcement, threatening to slow genuine progress on emissions reduction.

## The EU ETS Reimbursement Right: A "Paper Tiger"?

Since January 2024, shipping companies operating vessels in and out of the EU have been obligated to buy and surrender emission allowances under the EU ETS. The Directive places this financial burden primarily on the "shipping company," typically the registered owner or the entity holding the ISM safety management certificate. However, as the Erasmus School of Law study, "EU ETS payments in shipping: parties’ responsibilities towards authorities and within the commercial chain," reveals, this party often has minimal say over crucial operational decisions like fuel choice, routing, or vessel speed—decisions that directly impact a ship's carbon footprint.

To counteract this potential misalignment, EU law provides a statutory right for the shipping company to be reimbursed by the party controlling these operational factors, usually a time charterer. Yet, the study concludes that this statutory reimbursement right is, in practice, a "paper tiger," largely unenforceable within the complex web of shipping's contractual and jurisdictional realities.

## Why the Statutory Mechanism Falls Short in Practice

The Erasmus School of Law research identifies three primary obstacles that render the EU ETS's reimbursement right largely ineffective for most of the market:

1.  **Multi-layered Charter Chains:** Modern shipping often involves intricate chains of charterers, splitting responsibility for cargo, route, speed, and fuel across multiple entities. The Directive offers no clear guidance on identifying which specific party in such a chain must bear the reimbursement obligation.
2.  **Choice of Law:** A significant majority of international shipping contracts are governed by English law. It remains uncertain whether a national reimbursement right within an EU member state can override this established choice of law under the EU's private international law rules.
3.  **Arbitration Outside EU Jurisdiction:** Disputes in shipping are frequently referred to arbitration in key maritime hubs like London or Singapore. These jurisdictions operate outside direct EU court authority, and English law, notably, does not provide an equivalent reimbursement mechanism. English courts can, in practice, block EU proceedings or refuse to recognise EU judgments, effectively neutralising the statutory right.

As Hannah Mosmans, a co-author of the study, notes, the statutory right gives "the impression that shipowners and managers who pay the carbon bill can automatically recover it," but their research shows this to be "largely an illusion once you look at how shipping contracts actually work."

## Contractual Gaps and Uneven Protection

In response to this legislative gap, the shipping industry has seen the emergence of standard clauses, particularly those developed by BIMCO, for various charter types and management agreements. While a useful starting point, the study finds these clauses to be incomplete. They operate bilaterally, requiring alignment across every contract in a chain, and often leave critical gaps concerning issues like price volatility, credit risk, off-hire periods, and demurrage. Their optional nature also means their inclusion depends heavily on the relative bargaining power of owners and charterers.

Shipping lines have also begun passing ETS surcharges to cargo interests through bills of lading. The consequence, the study warns, is "uneven protection across the market," with larger, better-resourced parties better positioned to negotiate robust terms than smaller operators. This directly impacts how [EU ETS carbon costs](/?#ets) are ultimately distributed and absorbed, potentially creating an unfair burden for some companies and affecting their overall [company rankings](/companies.html) based on financial exposure.

## Implications for Emissions Accountability

This fundamental flaw in the EU ETS's cost allocation mechanism has profound implications for global shipping emissions. If the financial burden of carbon allowances cannot be reliably passed to the party directly controlling operational decisions (and thus emissions levels), the core incentive for decarbonization is weakened.

For instance, the drive to achieve better [CII ratings](/cii.html)—which measure operational carbon intensity—could be undermined if charterers or operational managers do not directly face the financial consequences of higher emissions. While [emissions data](/charts.html) from EMSA MRV will continue to reveal actual CO2 output, the intended market-based mechanism to encourage its reduction becomes less effective. The study highlights that not all emissions are purely operational; some relate to a vessel's technical efficiency, which is an owner's investment. However, a significant portion *is* operational, and if charterers are not consistently bearing the cost, their motivation to optimize for fuel efficiency, speed, and routing is reduced. This creates a disconnect where the financial pain is felt by one party (owner/manager) while the power to mitigate emissions rests primarily with another (charterer).

## What this means for shipping emissions

The Erasmus School of Law study exposes a critical vulnerability in the EU ETS's design for the maritime sector: the intended mechanism for allocating carbon costs to the party with operational control is largely unenforceable in practice. This risks misaligning incentives, leaving the party paying the carbon bill with limited control over emissions, and potentially reducing the overall pressure on the industry to genuinely decarbonize. For the EU ETS to truly drive down shipping emissions, urgent attention must be paid to ensuring that the financial consequences of carbon output are robustly and consistently borne by those who have the power to reduce them. Without this, the path to a cleaner shipping industry remains fraught with contractual complexities and uneven accountability.
