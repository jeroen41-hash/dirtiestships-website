The recent India-Denmark ministerial visit to Copenhagen underscores a critical inflection point for the global shipping industry: the accelerating pivot towards decarbonization. As regulators worldwide intensify pressure to curb maritime emissions, evidenced by the EU's inclusion of shipping in its Emissions Trading System (ETS) and the IMO's Carbon Intensity Indicator (CII) framework, the exploration of alternative fuels like ammonia and port electrification is no longer a futuristic concept but an immediate strategic imperative. This bilateral cooperation highlights how major maritime nations are actively seeking solutions to the industry's substantial CO2 footprint, which will inevitably reshape company emissions profiles and dictate future compliance costs.

## Advancing Ammonia: A Deep-Sea Decarbonization Pathway

A key focus of the Indian delegation's visit to Copenhagen was examining the development and use of two-stroke marine engines capable of running on ammonia. Discussions with engine developer Everllence put propulsion technology and the crucial element of fuel infrastructure on the same agenda. This is vital, as the widespread adoption of ammonia-fuelled vessels hinges not just on engine readiness but on the availability of bunkering facilities to support their operations. The article notes a tangible step forward: earlier in November (prior to the 2026 visit), the first Everllence ammonia dual-fuel main engine for Höegh Autoliners’ Aurora-class programme was installed in a car carrier under construction in China. This move by Höegh Autoliners is a clear signal from a major operator, demonstrating real-world investment in technologies designed to reduce CO2 emissions. For companies like Höegh, such investments are crucial for improving [CII ratings](/cii.html) and mitigating future EU ETS carbon costs, as ammonia, when produced greenly, offers a near-zero CO2 emissions solution. The data gathered through EU MRV will eventually reflect the impact of such technological shifts on fleet-wide emissions.

## Electrifying Ports: Shore Power's Immediate Impact

Beyond alternative fuels, the delegation's visit to the Port of Copenhagen focused on shore power installations and port electrification strategies. Shore power, or Onshore Power Supply (OPS), allows compatible vessels to connect to land-based electricity while berthed, switching off their auxiliary engines. This directly reduces local exhaust emissions and noise, contributing significantly to improved air quality in port communities. Copenhagen's inauguration of a shore power facility for cruise vessels at Oceankaj in June 2025 exemplifies this trend. India is also progressing, with Jawaharlal Nehru Port Authority awarding a ₹76.92 crore contract for a shore power project at Gateway Terminals India. For vessels calling at equipped European ports, utilizing shore power can directly reduce their reported CO2 emissions under EU MRV for time spent at berth, thus improving their operational [CII ratings](/cii.html) and potentially lowering their ETS liabilities by eliminating emissions from auxiliary engines. While the overall emissions reduction may seem modest compared to main propulsion, it addresses a specific, persistent source of emissions in populated areas and contributes to a company's total emissions profile.

## Green Shipping Corridors: Blueprint for a Decarbonized Future

The India–Denmark Green Strategic Partnership, launched in 2020, forms the bedrock for these cooperative efforts. The establishment of the Indo-Danish Centre of Excellence on Green Shipping in 2025 and the launch of a pre-feasibility study on green shipping corridors in India are particularly significant. Green shipping corridors are vital pathways designed to accelerate the deployment of zero-emission solutions on specific trade routes. By establishing a framework for green shipbuilding, port digitalization, and alternative fuel bunkering infrastructure along these corridors, they provide a structured environment for early adopters. The 2026 workshop involving key Indian maritime stakeholders further solidifies this commitment. Such corridors are critical for companies aiming for "A" or "B" CII ratings, as they offer the necessary infrastructure to reliably operate vessels on low or zero-carbon fuels. Their development provides a roadmap for how future EU MRV data will increasingly show reductions as vessels move between ports within these designated green routes, impacting overall [company rankings](/companies.html) over time.

## What this means for shipping emissions

The India-Denmark collaboration signifies a proactive stance by maritime nations to tackle the industry's environmental footprint through a dual approach: investing in groundbreaking propulsion technologies and enhancing port-side infrastructure. For DirtiestShips.com, this progress suggests future shifts in the raw data we track. As ammonia-fuelled vessels become operational and shore power adoption increases globally, we anticipate a gradual but measurable reduction in reported CO2 emissions via EU MRV. These initiatives are direct responses to the imperative to improve [CII ratings](/cii.html) and manage rising EU ETS costs. While large-scale emissions reductions are still years away, the decisions made today in places like Copenhagen and Jawaharlal Nehru Port are laying the groundwork for a cleaner shipping industry, which will eventually be reflected in our [emissions data](/charts.html).
