The maritime industry is at a critical juncture, grappling with stringent decarbonization targets and escalating carbon costs under regimes like the EU Emissions Trading System (ETS) and the IMO's Carbon Intensity Indicator (CII). Against this backdrop, onboard carbon capture (oCCS) technology is emerging from pilot projects to early commercial installations, promising a direct pathway to emissions reduction. However, a closer look at its current capabilities, operational demands, and systemic challenges reveals a complex landscape where theoretical capture rates meet real-world limitations in delivering verifiable CO2 reductions, impacting a vessel’s actual emissions profile as tracked by [emissions data](/charts.html) and influencing [CII ratings](/cii.html).

## Early Commercial Deployments and Net Emissions Realities

The transition of oCCS from conceptual to commercial deployment marks a significant step, with amine-based post-combustion capture proving the most mature marine option. According to Lloyd's Register (LR), early commercial installations are achieving gross CO2 capture rates of 30% to 40%. Notable examples include Eastern Pacific Shipping’s *Pacific Cobalt*, which earned LR’s EACCS class notation in 2024, and Solvang’s *Clipper Eris*, piloting a full-scale system targeting approximately 70% gross capture from its main engine.

However, the headline capture figures do not tell the full story regarding actual emissions reduction. Operating these systems demands significant energy, increasing a vessel's fuel consumption by an estimated 15% to 30%. LR analysis indicates that a 70% gross capture system, coupled with a 15% fuel penalty, delivers an estimated *net tank-to-wake CO2 reduction* of around 44%. While waste-heat recovery could mitigate some of this penalty, the core challenge remains: the energy intensity of oCCS means that a substantial portion of the captured CO2's benefit is offset by increased fuel burn to power the capture process itself. This net reduction is the figure that ultimately matters for a ship's EU MRV reporting and its path to improved [CII ratings](/cii.html).

## Vessel Suitability: A Critical Factor for Operational Viability

The business case for oCCS is highly dependent on a vessel’s operational profile and physical characteristics. Panos Mitrou, LR Senior Vice President of Shipping Strategy, highlights that oCCS offers a realistic pathway for "the right vessel types and trades." Ideal candidates typically possess sufficient remaining trading life and adequate onboard space for the capture equipment.

Medium range and chemical tankers operating on EEA routes present a strong opportunity, as frequent port calls facilitate CO2 offloading, and their exposure to EU ETS carbon prices significantly increases the value of emissions reductions. Similarly, LNG-fuelled vessels and LNG carriers can leverage the cold energy released during LNG vaporization to liquefy captured CO2, reducing the auxiliary power demand. Short-sea and feeder vessels also benefit from predictable routes and regular offloading opportunities. Conversely, ships with limited trading life, low carbon-price exposure, or poor access to offloading facilities will struggle to justify the investment, demonstrating how crucial operational fit is to actual emissions mitigation.

## The Decisive Challenge: Shore-Side Infrastructure and Carbon Costs

For oCCS to deliver its intended benefits, captured CO2 must be offloaded and permanently stored or utilized. This is where the maritime industry faces its most significant hurdle: a severe lack of adequate port reception infrastructure. Few ports globally are currently equipped to receive ship-captured liquefied CO2, and many existing facilities are designed for food-grade CO2, unsuitable for exhaust-derived streams. While north-west Europe is seeing some progress, supported by nascent North Sea transport and storage networks, access remains highly limited elsewhere.

This infrastructure gap has direct implications for EU ETS carbon costs. While the EU ETS *can* recognize captured CO2 that reaches qualifying permanent storage or utilization, the inability to offload means that, in practice, the CO2 remains "unaccounted" in terms of ETS compliance. This places an ongoing financial burden on shipowners, potentially negating the economic incentive for oCCS investment and impacting [company rankings](/companies.html) based on their overall emissions performance and ETS exposure. Voyage length and offloading opportunities thus directly dictate how much CO2 can truly be captured and certified.

## Regulatory Frameworks: A Patchwork of Recognition

The effectiveness of oCCS is also contingent on its recognition within international and regional regulatory frameworks. The EU ETS offers a pathway for recognizing captured CO2 under specific conditions related to permanent storage or eligible utilization. However, a significant gap exists with FuelEU Maritime, which *does not yet* include oCCS in its greenhouse gas intensity calculations, creating an inconsistency for shipowners navigating concurrent EU regulations. Globally, the IMO is still developing a comprehensive framework that will address the safety, testing, verification, and critical carbon accounting aspects of oCCS. This fragmented regulatory landscape adds uncertainty for shipowners attempting to integrate oCCS into their decarbonization strategies, making it difficult to project long-term compliance benefits and cost savings.

## What this means for shipping emissions

Onboard carbon capture is a promising technological tool with the potential to reduce maritime CO2 emissions, especially for specific vessel types and trade routes. However, its widespread impact hinges not just on gross capture efficiency but on the *net* emissions reduction after accounting for the energy penalty, the rapid development of shore-side offloading and storage infrastructure, and consistent, clear regulatory recognition across frameworks like the EU ETS and FuelEU Maritime. Without these systemic advancements, the actual, verifiable reduction in global shipping emissions will be significantly constrained. As we track the industry's progress through EU MRV data, it's clear that the path to decarbonization via oCCS is complex, requiring a holistic approach that extends far beyond the ship's engine room.
