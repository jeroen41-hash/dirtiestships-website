The global shipping industry faces immense pressure to decarbonize, with ever-tightening regulations and the increasing financial burden of carbon emissions. Against this backdrop, the International Maritime Organization (IMO)'s recent decision to adopt a proposal for onboard carbon capture and storage system (OCCS) safety guidelines marks a pivotal moment. This development, led by a comprehensive Korean public-private partnership, signals a serious step towards standardizing a technology that could significantly alter the emissions trajectory of existing fleets, directly impacting vessels' environmental performance, their [CII ratings](/cii.html), and operational costs, particularly within frameworks like the EU Emissions Trading System (EU ETS).

### The IMO's Framework for Carbon Capture Safety

At its 12th session of the Sub-Committee on Carriage of Cargoes and Containers (CCC 12), the IMO agreed to use a proposal from a Korean partnership as the foundational document for developing interim OCCS safety guidelines. This decision is critical, establishing a global baseline for integrating carbon capture technology safely into ship design and operation. The partnership, formed in April 2026 and led by Korea’s Ministry of Oceans and Fisheries with Korean Register (KR) overseeing regulatory development, pooled expertise from major players including HMM, Samsung Heavy Industries, Hanwha Ocean, and HD Korea Shipbuilding & Offshore Engineering. Their collective experience, spanning shipbuilding, manufacturing, and operational insights, was consolidated by KR into a regulatory proposal presented to the IMO.

The CCC 12 also established a Correspondence Group on GHG Safety, coordinated by Korea, to further refine these guidelines. This group will gather input from IMO Member States and international organizations, aiming to develop common safety requirements, with the possibility of technology-specific annexes. This collaborative approach underscores the complexity and multi-faceted nature of ensuring OCCS systems are not only effective but also safe for crew, cargo, and the marine environment. The group is slated to report its progress to CCC 13 in 2027.

### Korean Leadership and Practical Expertise

The Korean partnership's proposal draws heavily on real-world application and extensive research. A key example is the *HMM Mongla*, a vessel fitted with an OCCS solution in 2024 as part of a joint project involving HMM, Samsung Heavy Industries, PANASIA, and KR. Risk assessments based on the *HMM Mongla*'s OCCS design identified 64 distinct hazard scenarios. This practical operational experience and the subsequent identification of safety concerns have directly informed the development of the proposed guidelines, ensuring they address the realities of running such systems at sea.

The proposed guidelines cover critical practical safety considerations including CO2 handling and storage, leakage prevention, emergency response procedures, and the safe transfer of captured carbon ashore. Importantly, the framework adopts a technology-neutral main body, accommodating various capture methods such as chemical absorption, membrane separation, and mineralization, while allowing for specific hazards to be addressed in separate annexes. This flexibility is crucial for fostering innovation without prematurely locking into a single technological pathway.

### Impact on Compliance and Carbon Costs

For shipowners and operators, the formalization of OCCS safety guidelines by the IMO is a significant step towards enabling the commercial adoption of these systems. Successful implementation of OCCS has direct implications for a vessel’s environmental compliance and economic viability. By actively capturing CO2 emissions, ships equipped with OCCS can significantly reduce their reported operational carbon intensity. This reduction directly influences a vessel’s [CII ratings](/cii.html), potentially elevating a "D" or "E" rated ship to a more desirable "C" or "B" rating, thereby avoiding corrective action plans and maintaining competitive advantage.

Furthermore, for vessels trading in European waters, OCCS could offer a substantial hedge against the escalating costs of the EU ETS. Each tonne of CO2 captured onboard translates directly to fewer allowances needing to be surrendered, potentially saving millions for companies with large fleets. This directly impacts our [company rankings](/companies.html) and the financial analysis presented in our [emissions data](/charts.html). As Youngsun Kim of HMM highlighted, "Shipowners need standards covering safe operation, maintenance and offloading, not capture performance alone," reinforcing the real-world operational and financial pressures driving this regulatory push.

### Beyond the Blueprint: Challenges and Future Outlook

While the selection of Korea's proposal is a major milestone, it represents a foundational step rather than an immediate solution. The development of robust international safety guidelines will continue under Korea's coordination, with the Correspondence Group reporting its progress next year. The challenges remain significant, encompassing the long-term storage and offloading infrastructure for captured CO2, the energy penalty of OCCS systems, and the ultimate scalability of the technology across the diverse global fleet.

The collaborative spirit demonstrated by the Korean partnership and the IMO's commitment to technology-neutral, experience-informed guidelines are positive indicators. However, the true impact on global shipping emissions will depend on the successful commercialization, widespread adoption, and rigorous enforcement of these safety and performance standards.

### What this means for shipping emissions

The IMO's move to establish safety guidelines for onboard carbon capture is a critical enabler for one of the most promising deep decarbonization technologies for the existing fleet. While not an immediate emissions reducer, it provides the regulatory certainty necessary for shipowners and manufacturers to invest further in OCCS.
