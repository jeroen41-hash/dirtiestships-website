The global shipping industry stands at a critical juncture, with the International Maritime Organization (IMO) once again grappling with the final adoption of its Net-Zero Framework (NZF). Recent negotiations have revealed a persistent "déjà vu" for delegates, echoing past deadlocks, yet analysis suggests a subtle but significant shift in member state positions. For an industry under increasing pressure from stringent regulations like the EU ETS and demanding [CII ratings](/cii.html), the outcome of these discussions will directly dictate the pace of decarbonization and the financial burden on shipping companies.

## IMO's Déjà Vu: Another Year, Same Debates?

The 22nd IMO Intersessional Working Group on Reduction of GHG Emissions from Ships (ISWG-GHG 22) saw delegates express a sense of déjà vu, with similar interventions and debates mirroring those at MEPC 83. This sentiment, noted by researchers John Taukave and John Fatuimoana Kautoke of the Micronesian Center for Sustainable Transport (MCST), underscores the protracted nature of international climate negotiations within the shipping sector. While the feeling of repetition lingered, the article highlights that "the numbers tell a different story," indicating a shift in the alignment of member states, even if consensus remains elusive.

The journey to the current impasse is long-standing. A draft NZF was approved for circulation at MEPC 83 in April 2025, with 63 of 79 voting members in favour, marking a "rare multilateral compromise" despite the US walking out. However, an extraordinary MEPC session for formal adoption in October 2025 was adjourned for a year as divisions persisted. Now, 11 months later, the IMO faces another tight deadline to bridge these gaps.

## Shifting Sands: The Core Divides

Analysis by University College London’s Shipping and Oceans Research Group, cited in the article, reveals the specific areas where member states' positions have evolved, albeit not solidified:

*   **GHG Fuel Intensity (GFI) Targets:** 30 member states are now open to easing GFI reduction requirements for 2029-35, a softer start compared to the 24 who favour retaining the more ambitious trajectory approved in April 2025. This divergence directly impacts the stringency of future emissions targets and the pace required for fleet renewal or retrofits to maintain good [CII ratings](/cii.html).
*   **GFI Structure:** 30 supported a two-tier GFI structure (base and direct targets), while 17 favoured Liberia's proposed single-tier system, adding complexity to how compliance will be measured and enforced.
*   **Financial Mechanism:** A clear majority of 38 member states leaned towards a fund, facility, or similar financial structure to support the transition, against 17 that preferred a technical-only system without a central financial mechanism. The existence and design of such a mechanism are crucial for equitable transition, especially for lower-income countries, and will influence the overall cost of carbon for operators.
*   **Incentives for Low/Zero-Emission Fuels:** 32 supported retaining specific incentives for low- and zero-emission fuels, while 14 believed a separate reward was unnecessary or opposed it. This debate directly affects the economic viability and accelerated adoption of alternative fuels, which are critical for deep decarbonization.

These persistent divides highlight the significant challenges in creating a framework that is both ambitious enough to meet climate goals and palatable to a diverse set of national interests.

## National Stances and the Path Forward

The article details several national positions, shedding light on the underlying concerns driving the negotiations:

*   **Panama** advocates for a "practical, balanced and inclusive" transition, emphasizing the need for the framework to reflect the "availability, scalability and affordability of alternative fuels" while still delivering verifiable emissions reductions.
*   **Saudi Arabia** calls for "realism" and "technological neutrality," focusing on the potential impact of proposed measures on global trade.
*   **India** suggests an initial preparatory period to gauge fuel readiness and practical feasibility of new bunker fuels, followed by a phase to build operational experience before main measures take effect.
*   **Argentina** acknowledged technical progress but highlighted "significant differences" over the design and governance of a financial mechanism, particularly regarding support for Small Island Developing States (SIDS) and Least Developed Countries (LDCs).

MCST's Kautoke warned that Caribbean and Pacific Island nations would resist further compromises without movement from oil-exporting member states on financial mechanisms and the trajectory of emissions reductions.

Despite the hurdles, a route to adoption this year remains open. ISWG-GHG 23 meets in late November, followed by MEPC 85. If common ground is found, or an amendment proposal under the six-month rule is put to a vote, an adjourned extraordinary session could resume on December 4th for formal adoption. However, procedural rules mean "a vote to delay always takes precedence," posing a significant risk of further deferral to MEPC 86 in October 2027. Such a delay would leave the industry in prolonged uncertainty regarding future carbon costs and the regulatory landscape for [company rankings](/companies.html).

## Alt Fuels Reality Check: The LNG Bunker Bottleneck

Compounding the regulatory uncertainty is the practical challenge of alternative fuel infrastructure. DNV's white paper, mentioned in the article, highlights a critical bottleneck in the LNG bunkering sector. The global LNG bunker vessel fleet needs to expand dramatically, from roughly 70 vessels currently, to 165-208 vessels by 2030, just to keep pace with the rapid growth of the LNG-capable fleet. Despite more than 30 additional vessels contracted for delivery between 2026 and 2029, the report warns that bunkering capacity is struggling.

This infrastructure gap underscores the "availability, scalability, and affordability" concerns raised by nations like Panama and India. Even for LNG, considered a more established alternative fuel, the physical infrastructure required to support its widespread adoption is lagging. This reality impacts the feasibility of transitioning away from conventional fuels, directly affecting future emissions trajectories and the ability of operators to improve their [emissions data](/charts.html) and comply with future regulations.

## What this means for shipping emissions

The ongoing IMO negotiations present a stark picture of the challenges facing the shipping industry's decarbonization efforts. While a "strong policy solution" is still possible this year, the deep divisions over GFI targets, financial mechanisms, and incentives for alternative fuels mean that any adopted framework could be a watered-down compromise. Prolonged uncertainty delays crucial investment decisions in new technologies and fuels, making it harder for companies to improve their [CII ratings](/cii.html) and manage rising EU ETS costs. Without clear, ambitious, and globally coherent regulations, the industry risks falling further behind its net-zero targets, with significant implications for global shipping emissions and the environment.
