The global maritime sector, a colossal engine of international trade, remains one of the most stubborn contributors to CO2 emissions, heavily reliant on fossil fuels. This dependence not only jeopardizes global climate targets but also exposes the industry to severe vulnerabilities: volatile fuel prices, supply chain chokepoints, and an increasingly complex web of regulations like the EU ETS and CII ratings. While commitment to a sustainable transition is evident, the pathway to decarbonization, particularly for Zero and Near Zero emission (ZNZ) solutions, is fraught with financial hurdles, leading to slow implementation and persistent emissions challenges.

### The Stalling Engine: Why Decarbonization Projects Are Delayed

Despite a clear industry commitment to resilience and sustainability, the actual shift away from fossil fuels is faltering. As highlighted by orfonline.org, critical ZNZ fuel projects are frequently "stuck at pre-Final Investment Decision (FID)," and essential enabling infrastructure—such as bunkering facilities, pipelines, and storage—remains largely unbuilt. This delay in investment and infrastructure development has direct consequences for shipping's environmental footprint, ensuring that the current emissions trajectory continues largely unabated. For companies tracking their environmental performance, this translates into stagnant or worsening positions in [company rankings](/companies.html) and increased exposure to future carbon costs.

### The Unpriced Externality: The True Cost of Fossil Fuels

At the heart of the slow ZNZ transition lies an economic disparity: ZNZ fuels simply cannot compete on price with conventional bunker fuels. Green methanol, for example, has historically cost two to five times more, and green ammonia at least two to six times more, on an energy-equivalent basis. The reason, as orfonline.org points out, is profound: the "externality of consuming carbon is not accounted for." Fossil fuels are effectively "underpriced by at least US$6.7tn per year," a cost the world already pays through climate-related disasters and economic losses. This stark price gap, termed the "offtake bottleneck," makes securing demand commitments—and thus financing—for ZNZ projects exceedingly difficult. Without a robust carbon price that accurately reflects the environmental cost, the economic incentive to switch remains insufficient.

### Regulatory Pressure vs. Global Inaction

While the industry's Net Zero Framework (NZF) proposes a global price on emissions, its adoption is unlikely before 2029, and the actual carbon price is far from settled. In this vacuum, sub-global policies are stepping up. The EU ETS, for instance, is actively pricing carbon for shipping within its jurisdiction, creating an immediate financial incentive for emissions reduction. Similarly, FuelEU Maritime mandates a gradual reduction in GHG intensity of fuels used by ships. These regional policies, although vital, still operate within a global context where the financial playing field remains uneven. They represent a significant cost factor for vessels operating in or through EU waters, directly impacting operational budgets and accelerating the need for improved [CII ratings](/cii.html) to avoid penalties. The latest EU MRV data continues to provide the backbone for tracking these emissions and assessing the efficacy of such regulatory measures, offering transparency on which vessels and companies are leading—or lagging—the transition.

### Building Bridges: Financing the Future of Shipping

Despite the challenges, the article highlights promising developments, particularly in regions like India, where supportive industrial policy and renewable energy endowments are driving "Final Investment Decisions (FIDs)." India's National Green Hydrogen Mission, for example, has resulted in some of the lowest observed green ammonia contract prices (US$572–744 per tonne). These "lighthouse cases" demonstrate that viable business models for ZNZ fuels *do* exist, especially when policy, capital, and ambition align.

The challenge now is scaling these successes. While over US$40 billion has been committed to hydrogen in the last year alone, FID conversion remains limited. The key, according to orfonline.org, lies in enhanced coordination between concessional and commercial capital, coupled with targeted policy support. Lessons from European offshore wind, where public levers like contracts for difference, concessional lending, and the socialisation of site assessment and grid connection costs de-risked projects, offer a blueprint. Such strategic deployment of capital can "crowd in private capital," turning hesitant investments into concrete projects that will eventually provide the necessary fuels and infrastructure for a decarbonized fleet.

### What this means for shipping emissions

The analysis underscores a critical juncture for shipping's decarbonization journey. The industry's fossil fuel dependence continues to drive significant emissions, exacerbated by an unpriced carbon externality that makes ZNZ fuels economically uncompetitive. While global policy lags, regional initiatives like the EU ETS are creating immediate financial incentives for change, compelling companies to track their emissions via EU MRV data and improve their [CII ratings](/cii.html). The path forward requires not just technological innovation, but a fundamental restructuring of financing mechanisms and policy coordination to de-risk ZNZ projects and build essential infrastructure. Without a concerted effort to close the cost gap and accelerate FID, the shipping sector risks continued high emissions and escalating carbon costs, further cementing its position as a significant climate challenge. Ultimately, sustained pressure from regulators and greater transparency through accessible [emissions data](/charts.html) will be vital to propel the transition from talk to tangible action.
