The global shipping industry's journey towards decarbonization is complex, extending beyond mere CO2 reduction to encompass all greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions. A recent development highlighting this broader challenge is Wärtsilä's successful conversion of engines on Chevron Transport Corporation's LNG carriers, specifically targeting the notoriously potent issue of methane slip. This project underscores the critical need to address fugitive methane emissions from LNG-fueled vessels, a factor often overlooked in the dominant CO2 narrative but increasingly significant as regulators, including those overseeing the EU MRV framework, broaden their focus to the full spectrum of GHGs contributing to climate change.

## Tackling Methane Slip: A Critical Step for LNG's Credibility

Liquefied Natural Gas (LNG) has been widely touted as a transition fuel, offering significant CO2 reductions compared to traditional heavy fuel oil. However, its environmental benefits are heavily qualified by the problem of methane slip – the release of uncombusted methane into the atmosphere during engine operation. Methane has a far greater Global Warming Potential (GWP) than CO2 over shorter timescales, making even small amounts impactful.

Wärtsilä's 50DF to spark gas (SG) conversion project directly addresses this. By modifying existing Wärtsilä 50DF engines to operate with spark ignition instead of diesel pilot fuel, the combustion process is optimized, significantly reducing methane slip. The initial phase has seen successful conversions on three Chevron LNG carriers, with three more planned. The estimated result, according to Wärtsilä, is a reduction in methane emissions down to 1.1% of fuel use on an IMO weighted (E2-cycle) average. This is a crucial metric, as the efficiency of methane combustion directly impacts LNG's overall GHG footprint.

## Chevron's Proactive Stance and Industry Adoption

Chevron Transport Corporation's initiative to convert six of its LNG carriers highlights a proactive approach to mitigating the environmental drawbacks of LNG propulsion. As we track [company rankings](/companies.html) based on overall emissions, such steps, while currently not fully captured in all regulatory frameworks, reflect a commitment to a more holistic GHG reduction strategy. Many LNG carriers, which largely utilize Wärtsilä 50DF engines for diesel-electric propulsion, stand to benefit from this conversion solution. This suggests a significant potential for broader industry adoption, particularly as scrutiny on methane emissions intensifies globally.

The collaboration between a technology provider like Wärtsilä and a major operator like Chevron is vital for validating such innovations. Operational insights gained from these initial conversions will be crucial for wider deployment, potentially setting a new benchmark for "cleaner" LNG propulsion and improving the overall GHG intensity of the affected fleet.

## Connecting to EU MRV, CII, and ETS

While the EU MRV regulation primarily mandates the reporting of CO2 emissions, the EU's broader "Fit for 55" package signals a clear trajectory towards encompassing all relevant GHG emissions. Methane is explicitly mentioned in various EU climate policy discussions, and it is conceivable that its monitoring and reporting, and potentially its regulation, will become more stringent for shipping in the future.

For **CII ratings** ([CII ratings](/cii.html)), the direct impact of reducing methane slip on a vessel's current CII grade is indirect. The CII calculation is based on CO2 emissions per transport work. However, any measure that reduces the *overall* GHG intensity of a vessel ultimately improves its environmental profile. As regulatory frameworks evolve to consider a full lifecycle assessment or include methane in direct emissions calculations, such engine conversions could significantly bolster a vessel's compliance and rating. Companies demonstrating a commitment to reducing all GHGs are better positioned for future regulatory shifts.

Similarly, the **EU Emissions Trading System (ETS)** currently applies a carbon cost solely based on reported CO2 emissions. Methane emissions are not presently factored into the ETS calculations for maritime transport. However, the precedent exists in other sectors (e.g., oil and gas) where methane emissions are either regulated or subject to carbon pricing. As the shipping ETS matures and climate policies tighten, the inclusion of methane in the ETS scope or the introduction of separate methane-specific levies cannot be ruled out. Proactive measures like Wärtsilä's conversion project could thus represent a future-proofing strategy against potential additional carbon costs.

## What this means for shipping emissions

Wärtsilä's methane slip reduction project on Chevron's LNG carriers represents a significant technological stride towards addressing one of the key environmental drawbacks of LNG as a marine fuel. It underscores that decarbonization requires a comprehensive approach, tackling all GHGs, not just CO2. While current regulatory instruments like EU MRV, CII, and ETS primarily focus on CO2, the growing recognition of methane's potent impact means that such innovations are not just good practice but will likely become increasingly critical for compliance and competitive advantage in a carbon-constrained future. It signals a necessary evolution in how the industry and regulators assess the true environmental performance of alternative fuels.
