The relentless expansion of the EU Emissions Trading System (ETS) continues to reshape the global shipping landscape, bringing new vessel types and operational nuances under its increasingly stringent net. A recent proposal from the European Commission, analyzed by the International Marine Contractors Association (IMCA), outlines critical revisions impacting the offshore sector, including smaller vessels and specialized operations. While generally welcomed as a step towards recognizing the unique challenges of offshore work, the proposals highlight ongoing compliance hurdles and the crucial need for clear, actionable guidance to ensure effective decarbonization and prevent regulatory overlap.

## Refining the Scope: Expanding ETS & MRV Definitions

One of the most significant aspects of the Commission's proposal is the **inclusion of smaller offshore ships between 400 and 5,000 GT within the scope of EU ETS from 2031**. This move extends the regulatory reach beyond conventional cargo and passenger vessels, also encompassing a series of other smaller ships, including specific oil and chemical tankers. For DirtiestShips.com, this expansion means our [emissions data](/charts.html) and [company rankings](/companies.html) will increasingly reflect the carbon footprint of a broader range of specialized vessels, offering a more comprehensive picture of the industry's total impact.

Equally important are the proposed updates to terminology, particularly the long-standing "Port of Call" definition. Recognizing that offshore vessels operate differently from conventional ships, the proposals introduce specific definitions like "offshore operations" and "offshore worksite," alongside an updated "port of call" definition tailored for ships performing or supporting these activities. As IMCA's Director of Policy & Advocacy, Sophia Haywood, notes, "Offshore vessels do not operate in the same way as conventional cargo ships." These refinements are essential for accurate EU MRV data collection, which forms the bedrock of both ETS compliance and performance metrics like [CII ratings](/cii.html). Without precise definitions, tracking emissions from complex, project-based offshore activities would be inconsistent and difficult to verify.

The proposals also extend existing derogations for ice-class ships and for voyages involving outermost regions and small islands without a fixed land connection until 2035, providing specific sectors with more time to adapt to the evolving regulatory landscape.

## Fueling the Future: The SMAP Initiative's Promise and Pitfalls

A positive development highlighted by IMCA is the introduction of the Sustainable Maritime Alternative Propulsion (SMAP) initiative. This mechanism aims to accelerate the adoption of alternative fuels and zero-emission technologies by reserving up to **110 million allowances** between January 1, 2028, and December 31, 2040. These allowances are intended to help bridge the significant price gap between fossil and sustainable maritime fuels and to support the deployment of cleaner technologies.

However, a critical concern raised by IMCA is the proposal's limitation on the origin of renewable fuel production, restricting it to the EU and countries with a linked ETS. Given the nascent stage of the global renewable fuel industry, and particularly the EU's own production capacity, this restriction poses a "significant barrier" to industry uptake. For effective decarbonization, a global approach to fuel sourcing will be necessary in the short term, regardless of origin, provided the fuels meet recognized sustainability criteria under frameworks like FuelEU Maritime. This challenge underscores the need for policy flexibility to truly foster innovation and achieve widespread adoption of low-carbon solutions, which is crucial for overall shipping emissions reduction.

## Navigating the Labyrinth: Compliance Challenges and Dual Regimes

While the Commission's proposals are a step forward, they simultaneously underscore significant compliance challenges, particularly concerning the interaction between the EU ETS and the UK ETS. With both schemes set to apply to offshore vessels over 5,000 GT from January 2027, the risk of "double penalty" and administrative burden looms large. The industry requires robust and updated guidance *before* these new rules take effect, rather than being expected to operate under a "broken framework" in the interim.

The timeline for these changes is also critical: the revised EU ETS rules, if adopted, would come into force from 2031, while the revised EU MRV Regulation would apply from January 1, 2029. Until then, the existing frameworks remain in place, creating a period of uncertainty that demands proactive communication and clear interim guidelines from regulators. IMCA's continued engagement with EU institutions is vital to ensure these proposals mature into workable and proportionate rules that support compliance without hindering operations.

## What this means for shipping emissions

The proposed revisions represent a necessary evolution in maritime emissions regulation, bringing specialized offshore vessels into the fold and refining operational definitions crucial for accurate MRV data and, by extension, effective EU ETS and CII application. While the SMAP initiative offers a valuable pathway for alternative fuels, its restrictive sourcing criteria could slow progress. The most immediate challenge, however, is the pressing need for detailed guidance to navigate the complex interplay of new and existing regulations, especially between the EU and UK ETS, to prevent undue financial and administrative burdens. For the shipping industry to meet its decarbonization targets, clear, harmonized, and globally pragmatic regulatory frameworks are paramount, enabling companies to invest confidently in cleaner technologies and fuels.
