The International Maritime Organization's (IMO) Marine Environment Protection Committee (MEPC 85), convening from November 30 to December 3, is poised to address a critical juncture in shipping's decarbonisation journey. With mounting pressure to solidify a global framework for reducing greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, the outcomes of these negotiations will significantly shape the future regulatory landscape for the entire industry, directly impacting everything from [CII ratings](/cii.html) to operational costs and ultimately, the sector's overall carbon footprint.

### The IMO's Decarbonisation Dilemma at MEPC 85

Central to MEPC 85's agenda is the unresolved fate of the IMO's proposed Net-Zero Framework (NZF), a comprehensive package of mid-term GHG reduction measures. This framework, initially approved during MEPC 83 in April 2025, combines a global fuel standard with an economic mechanism designed to incentivise the adoption of lower-carbon fuels and technologies. Its final approval, however, was delayed in October 2025 due to opposition from a bloc of member states, leaving the global shipping industry in a state of uncertainty regarding future emissions regulations. According to hellenicshippingnews.com, delegates will now consider outcomes from intersessional negotiations, aiming to address "remaining concerns" and bridge the divide.

### Environmentalists Push for Immediate Action

Environmental organisations, represented by the Clean Shipping Coalition (CSC), are urging member states to adopt the Net-Zero Framework "as is" without further delay. Lukas Leppert, president of the CSC, stated that the framework represents "the product of years of negotiations," a "long-negotiated compromise" supported by a broad majority and reflecting findings from the IMO’s Comprehensive Impact Assessment. The coalition argues that subsequent attempts to introduce alternative proposals have failed to demonstrate comparable emissions reductions or garner sufficient support, thereby solidifying the NZF as the "best course ahead." For DirtiestShips.com, this underscores the urgency for concrete, binding regulation that can translate into measurable emissions reductions, moving beyond rhetoric to enforceable global standards.

### Industry Concerns and the EU's Role

Conversely, segments of the shipping industry express significant reservations, particularly concerning the European Union's negotiating stance. Melina Travlos, president of the Union of Greek Shipowners (UGS), warned that political divisions risk undermining efforts for a workable global solution. She criticised the EU's "uncompromising position," arguing it is "steering the IMO process to a new dead end" by lacking the necessary flexibility. The UGS advocates for a pragmatic, proportionate, and implementable global regulatory framework that recognises the diverse operational realities across shipping segments and trading patterns. Travlos emphasised the need for a broader approach beyond just alternative fuels, incorporating operational and technical efficiency measures, and aligning climate ambitions with the actual availability, scalability, and affordability of new fuels and supporting infrastructure. This divergence highlights the tension between ambitious climate targets and the practical challenges of implementation for global fleets.

### Connecting Global Policy to On-the-Water Emissions

The outcome of MEPC 85 will have profound implications for global shipping emissions and the tools we use to track them.

*   **CII Ratings:** The adoption of a global fuel standard or new technical/operational efficiency measures as part of the NZF would directly influence how ships are designed, operated, and ultimately, how their [CII ratings](/cii.html) are calculated. Stricter global rules would necessitate faster fleet upgrades and more efficient operational practices to maintain compliant ratings.
*   **Carbon Costs:** The proposed economic mechanism within the NZF could establish a global carbon levy or equivalent, mirroring, in principle, regional initiatives like the EU Emissions Trading System (ETS). If adopted, this would introduce direct carbon costs for international voyages, compelling companies to factor emissions into their financial planning and incentivising investment in decarbonisation. Delays at the IMO, however, risk further proliferation of regional carbon pricing schemes, creating a patchwork of regulations for global operators.
*   **Company Performance:** For companies currently scrutinised in our [company rankings](/companies.html), the IMO's decision will either accelerate the imperative to decarbonise or prolong regulatory uncertainty. Those already investing in efficiency upgrades and low-carbon fuel pathways will be better positioned to adapt, while those relying on older, less efficient tonnage will face increasing pressure and potential penalties. The data gathered via EU MRV already provides a baseline for tracking CO2, and a global framework would offer a broader, more consistent dataset.

### What this means for shipping emissions

MEPC 85 represents a critical juncture for the IMO's ability to translate years of negotiation into binding, impactful regulation. Failure to adopt a comprehensive framework like the Net-Zero Framework would likely prolong the shipping industry's reliance on fossil fuels, delay critical investment in green technologies, and potentially lead to a fragmented regulatory landscape with varying regional carbon costs and efficiency standards. Conversely, a decisive agreement could provide the necessary global clarity and incentive structure to accelerate decarbonisation, driving down emissions across the world's merchant fleet and providing a clearer path forward for sustainable shipping.
