The global shipping industry is under increasing pressure to decarbonize, with European regulations like FuelEU Maritime driving a critical demand for alternative, lower-emission fuels. Brazil is emerging as a potential powerhouse in the biodiesel market, boasting competitive costs and significant production capacity. However, its ambition to fuel the international fleet faces a complex regulatory landscape, particularly concerning the sustainability criteria of feedstocks, which will critically influence [CII ratings](/cii.html) and future carbon costs for vessels operating in regulated zones.

## Brazil's Biodiesel Potential: A Supply-Side Advantage

Brazil's proposition as a leading biodiesel supplier is compelling. The nation benefits from an abundance of raw materials, primarily soybean oil, alongside substantial idle production capacity. According to Argus indicators, Brazilian biodiesel prices have averaged $1,065/m³ on the spot market and $1,016/m³ for contract delivery at Paranaguá over the last 12 months. This compares favorably to European biodiesel from the Netherlands, which averaged $1,146/m³.

With a current production capacity of 15.8mn m³/y, Brazil's actual output is only around 10mn m³/y, indicating an idle capacity of approximately 37pc. This excess capacity, coupled with competitive pricing, positions Brazil to scale up production rapidly should international demand materialize. Domestically, companies like state-controlled Petrobras and Raizen have already received approval to market B24 (24pc biodiesel blend in VLSFO), with Petrobras currently supplying it at Brazilian ports.

## The EU's Regulatory Wall: RED III and FuelEU Maritime

Despite Brazil's cost and supply advantages, a significant hurdle exists in meeting international demand, particularly from Europe. The EU's RED III directive and the specific maritime regulation, FuelEU Maritime, are designed to aggressively reduce greenhouse gas emissions but carry strict sustainability criteria. These regulations classify soybeans as a feedstock with a high risk of indirect land-use change (ILUC), which results in higher "well-to-wheel" emissions – the total CO2 released from production through consumption.

For shipping companies seeking to improve their [CII ratings](/cii.html) and reduce their EU ETS liabilities, FuelEU Maritime prioritizes biodiesel made from waste feedstocks, such as used cooking oil (UCO), agricultural waste, or industrial waste, due to their lower climate impact. This preference effectively limits the eligibility of soybean-derived biodiesel under current EU frameworks, creating a significant barrier for Brazilian producers targeting the European market. The selection of compliant, lower-carbon fuels directly influences a vessel's operational carbon intensity and its overall EU ETS liabilities when calling at EU ports.

## IMO's Net-Zero Framework: A Potential Game-Changer

The regulatory landscape, however, is not static. A potential turning point lies with the International Maritime Organization (IMO), which is expected to approve a new regulatory package in December, known as the Net-Zero Framework. This proposal aims to establish a global standard for fuel emissions intensity and an economic mechanism linked to ship emissions, with progressive reduction targets through 2050.

Should the IMO's life-cycle assessment (LCA) criteria for fuels be more accommodating to soybean oil biodiesel than the EU's RED III, it could significantly expand the market for Brazilian biodiesel in international waters. The potential effective date of these new IMO requirements, possibly starting in 2028, could unlock substantial demand and reshape consumption decisions in the maritime sector globally. Such a shift could profoundly impact [company rankings](/companies.html) based on their fleet's decarbonization progress.

## Brazil's Domestic Advances in Biobunker

In parallel with these international discussions, Brazil is actively developing its domestic biobunker regulations. In late July, the ANP (Brazil's hydrocarbons regulator) initiated a public consultation to revise marine fuel specifications, aiming to align national rules with the IMO's latest decarbonization targets. The proposal recognizes biodiesel, hydrotreated vegetable oil (HVO), and synthetic fuels as "drop-in" alternatives, meaning they can be used in existing engines and refueling infrastructure without modification.

This proactive approach facilitates domestic testing and adoption. Already, tests with drop-in fuels have been conducted under special ANP authorization. Efen, a joint venture, performed a debunkering operation using HVO at the port of Acu, while local firm Be8 tested its biofuel, BeVant, in southern Brazil. Denmark's Bunker One also conducted ethanol tests at Santos port this year. These domestic advancements position Brazil to capitalize rapidly if international regulations, particularly from the IMO, create a more favorable environment for its biodiesel.

## What this means for shipping emissions

Brazil's competitive pricing and substantial idle capacity present a compelling supply-side opportunity for the global shipping industry seeking to transition to lower-emission fuels. However, the critical determinant of its market penetration remains the evolving regulatory landscape, particularly the life-cycle assessment criteria adopted by the IMO. While the EU's current framework poses a significant hurdle for soybean-based biodiesel due to ILUC concerns, a more inclusive IMO standard could open vast new avenues. The choice of compliant, lower-carbon fuels will directly impact a vessel's operational costs, its [CII ratings](/cii.html), and its exposure to carbon pricing mechanisms like the EU ETS. Tracking these developments is crucial for understanding future [emissions data](/charts.html) and staying informed via our [news feed](/news.html), as the global shipping industry navigates its complex path to decarbonization.
