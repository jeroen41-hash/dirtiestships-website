The global shipping industry's ambitious decarbonization targets, underpinned by stringent regulations like the EU Emissions Trading System (ETS) and the IMO's Carbon Intensity Indicator (CII), face a critical bottleneck: the rapid ordering of alternative-fuel vessels is outstripping the development of essential bunkering infrastructure. A recent report from Lloyd’s Register (LR), "Fuel for Thought: Ports," starkly highlights this disparity, warning that while shipowners are investing heavily in cleaner propulsion, the ports required to fuel these vessels are lagging behind, creating a significant impediment to achieving meaningful emissions reductions.

## The Alternative Fuel Orderbook Surge vs. Reality

As of January 2026, the global orderbook reflected a substantial shift towards alternative fuels, with 1,942 alternative-fuel-capable vessels on order, representing 47% of the total orderbook by tonnage, according to Clarksons Research data cited by LR. This surge includes 385 methanol-capable vessels and 45 ammonia-capable ships, signaling a strong commitment from shipowners to transition away from conventional fossil fuels. Indeed, DNV’s Alternative Fuels Insight platform recorded 69 alternative-fuel vessel orders in September alone, the highest monthly total in nearly two years, with 48 of those being LNG-capable. This brought total orders for the first nine months of 2026 to 311 vessels, a 53% increase year-on-year.

However, the infrastructure to support these vessels tells a different story. While Liquefied Natural Gas (LNG) bunkering is relatively established at 222 ports worldwide, methanol is actively offered at just 25 ports. The situation for ammonia is even more nascent, with commercial bunkering recorded at only one port globally following its first operation in Ulsan, South Korea, in April 2026. This disconnect, as LR Ports Advisory Senior Vice President Kamran Ul Haq notes, means "the fleet is moving faster than the infrastructure needed to support it," rendering a vessel's alternative fuel capability moot without actual fuel availability.

## The Uneven Landscape of Fuel Availability

The disparity in infrastructure reflects the maturity of different fuel pathways. LNG's dominance in new orders is largely attributable to its more established global supply network and proven propulsion technology, offering shipowners greater operational flexibility. Methanol, while gaining traction, still faces geographic limitations, with bunkering concentrated in key hubs. Ammonia and hydrogen remain in much earlier stages, largely confined to pilot projects and safety assessments, with bunkering facilities virtually non-existent for commercial operations.

LR's report also highlights a significant geographical concentration of existing alternative-fuel infrastructure. Key shipping hubs in Northern Europe, North Asia, and Singapore dominate availability, leaving vast stretches of global trade routes underserved. This concentration makes strategic route planning and fuel procurement a critical, and often complex, challenge for shipowners. Biofuels offer a more immediate "drop-in" solution, usable with existing infrastructure, and were delivered at 90 ports. Yet, even here, availability is concentrated, with the top 13 ports accounting for 77.5% of quantities, and Singapore alone representing over a quarter.

## Beyond Bunkering: The True Cost of "Cleaner" Fuels

Availability is only one piece of the emissions puzzle; the actual climate benefit is another. Our analysis at DirtiestShips.com, leveraging EU MRV data, consistently shows the critical need to scrutinize not just the fuel type, but its entire lifecycle impact. The rapid adoption of LNG, for instance, has brought increased scrutiny of methane slip – the unburned methane released from ship engines. Methane is a potent greenhouse gas, and its unchecked emissions can significantly erode LNG’s overall climate benefits. As European emissions regulations place greater financial consequences on greenhouse gas performance, high methane slip translates directly to increased [EU ETS carbon costs](/ets.html) for operators.

Similarly, the emissions reduction potential of fuels like methanol and ammonia heavily depends on their production methods. "Grey" methanol, produced from fossil fuels, offers minimal greenhouse gas savings compared to "green" methanol from renewable sources. Without the availability of genuinely low-carbon fuel supplies at bunkering ports, even vessels designed for alternative fuels may struggle to achieve the desired improvements in their [CII ratings](/cii.html) and reduce their overall carbon footprint as tracked in our [emissions data](/charts.html).

## Strategic Choices for Ports and Shipowners

Given this complex landscape, LR advises ports to focus investment where credible demand will emerge, rather than attempting to accommodate every emerging technology. This mirrors the recent assessment by the Ports of Los Angeles and Long Beach, which identified LNG and methanol as the most viable alternative marine fuels for the near and medium term, advocating for a phased approach to infrastructure development.

For shipowners, the message is clear: ordering an alternative-fuel-capable vessel is only the first step. Ensuring that the chosen fuel will be available, affordable, and genuinely low-carbon along the ship’s intended trading routes is paramount. This necessitates a tighter integration of fuel strategy with trading strategy, a factor that will increasingly influence [company rankings](/companies.html) based on overall emissions performance. LR emphasizes that targeted investment, regulatory readiness, and closer coordination among shipping companies, ports, and fuel suppliers—including early investment in safety frameworks, licensing, and workforce training—are crucial to moving alternative fuels from trials into routine commercial operations.

## What this means for shipping emissions

The disconnect between alternative-fuel vessel orders and bunkering infrastructure development poses a significant threat to the shipping industry’s decarbonization efforts. While the investment in new, cleaner tonnage is encouraging, without a corresponding build-out of available and genuinely low-carbon fuel supplies, these vessels risk becoming "stranded assets" in their emissions reduction potential. This situation underscores the critical need for comprehensive, well-to-wake emissions accounting and transparent data.
