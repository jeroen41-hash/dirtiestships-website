The global shipping industry faces a critical juncture, with major companies making strides in reducing greenhouse gas emissions intensity but struggling to achieve meaningful absolute emission cuts. This paradox, revealed in recent annual reports, underscores the urgent need for robust regulatory frameworks and accessible low-carbon fuels, directly impacting everything from [CII ratings](/cii.html) to EU Emissions Trading System (ETS) compliance.

## The Rising Tide of Absolute Emissions

Despite significant investments in energy efficiency measures, the world's top 10 maritime companies by market capitalization reported declining GHG emissions per transport work but projected a rise in overall emissions for 2025. This concerning trend is primarily attributed to geopolitical conflicts forcing ships to take longer routes, leading to increased fuel consumption. As DNV's decarbonization director Tore Longva notes, "With longer distances, the emissions per ton-mile goes down, but the total emissions of course go up." While an estimated 25% further energy efficiency improvement is still possible by 2050, the core challenge remains the industry's reliance on high-carbon conventional fuels. This dynamic means that while individual voyages might appear more efficient on an intensity basis, the aggregate impact on the atmosphere, as captured by EU MRV data, continues to worsen.

## The Green Fuel Price Barrier and EU ETS Impact

A significant roadblock to absolute emission reduction is the prohibitively high cost and limited availability of green marine energy. Current market data illustrates this stark reality: July's average delivered bunker price for 0.5%-sulfur fuel oil was $18.66 per gigajoule in Singapore, while 100% sustainable methanol commanded a hefty $48.69/GJ. Even bioblends (B24 bioblend at $22.72/GJ) and LNG ($20.05/GJ) are notably more expensive than traditional fuels.

This substantial price gap is precisely where regulatory mechanisms like the EU Emissions Trading System (ETS) become critical. Since 2024, the EU ETS has extended to cover shipping, introducing a direct carbon cost for emissions. In 2025, FuelEU Maritime rules will further cap GHG bunker fuel intensity. For companies operating in EU waters, these regulations translate directly into financial penalties for high emissions, pushing up operational costs and potentially impacting [company rankings](/companies.html) based on overall environmental performance and carbon expenditure. Our [emissions data](/charts.html) will increasingly reflect these financial pressures.

## IMO's Stalled Global Framework

While regional policies like the EU's are taking effect, the global shipping industry, by its very nature, requires globally aligned regulations. The International Maritime Organization's (IMO) Net-Zero Framework, designed to place a cost on GHGs from ship operations globally from 2028, is facing significant delays due to fierce US opposition and subsequent revisions.

As UCL Energy Institute's Tristan Smith argues, "Regional policy lacks the stringency to make a significant impact on shipping’s energy transition." This sentiment is echoed by industry leaders like Hapag-Lloyd's Wolfram Guntermann, who stresses that a common international fuel standard and emissions-pricing mechanism would provide crucial investment certainty and scale low- and zero-emission fuels. The absence of a robust global framework creates uncertainty for long-term investments in green fuels and vessel technology, complicating companies' strategic decisions regarding their future [CII ratings](/cii.html) and overall decarbonization pathways.

## Waning Voluntary Demand for Green Shipping

In the absence of sufficient global regulatory drivers, efforts by shipping companies to offer sustainable freight services at higher prices are faltering. Voluntary demand from cargo owners for green shipping options is on the decline. A.P. Møller-Mærsk A/S, for instance, reported a near halving of its biofuel and green methanol use in 2025 to 1,524 GWh from 3,034 GWh in 2024, citing lower voluntary demand.

A Boston Consulting Group survey corroborates this trend, showing cargo owners' willingness to pay a premium for green fuels dropping to 3% in 2025 from 4.5% in 2024, the lowest since 2022. The share of cargo owners unwilling to pay any premiums increased by 4 percentage points in 2025. This market signal indicates that "cost management has moved to the top of the agenda," effectively deprioritizing low-carbon shipping without external mandates. This lack of market pull makes it harder for carriers, like those represented by the World Shipping Council (which has invested $180 billion in sustainable-fuel capable ships), to scale green operations.

## Interim Solutions vs. Long-Term decarbonization

Facing these challenges, major Japanese shipping firms Nippon Yusen Kabushiki Kaisha (NYK) and Mitsui O.S.K. Lines Ltd. (MOL) are focusing on immediate, controllable measures. NYK emphasizes "energy-efficiency improvements, fleet modernization, retrofits and operational optimization," while MOL is "advancing decarbonization primarily through the use of LNG, while working toward a future transition to zero-emission fuels such as ammonia." These strategies aim to achieve substantial GHG cuts in the interim without excessive reliance on uncertain regulatory or technological breakthroughs. While these steps are vital for improving individual vessel efficiency and [CII ratings](/cii.html), they represent a transitional approach. The ultimate path to net-zero will still necessitate a widespread and affordable shift to truly zero-emission fuels.

## What this means for shipping emissions

The shipping industry is caught between improving emissions intensity through efficiency and seeing absolute emissions rise due to operational demands and external factors. The high cost of green fuels and stalled global regulations are undermining voluntary decarbonization efforts. While regional policies like the EU ETS are introducing direct costs and driving some change, the lack of a comprehensive global framework, coupled with weakening market demand for green freight, signals a difficult and slow transition. Achieving net-zero will demand not just continued efficiency gains but, more critically, globally harmonized policies that bridge the green fuel price gap and incentivize fundamental shifts in fuel consumption. This scenario suggests continued upward pressure on overall emissions and heightened financial burdens from carbon costs for non-compliant fleets, as tracked by our [latest news](/news.html) and [emissions data](/charts.html).
