The global shipping industry, responsible for moving over 80% of world trade and contributing a staggering 2.3% of global CO2 emissions – equivalent to Japan’s entire annual output – is at a critical crossroads. As the International Maritime Organization (IMO) renews its efforts to establish a comprehensive Net-Zero Framework, the stakes could not be higher. Decisions made in upcoming negotiations, including a proposed global fuel standard and carbon pricing mechanism, are poised to lock in the emissions trajectory of the world’s fleet for the next two decades, profoundly impacting both environmental targets and the financial bottom line for shipping companies worldwide.

## The Stalled Global Push for Net-Zero Shipping

The path to a global agreement on shipping decarbonisation has been fraught with political headwinds. As detailed by hellenicshippingnews.com, the IMO's previous attempt at a breakthrough framework, which included a global fuel standard and a pricing mechanism on greenhouse gas emissions, was derailed by political opposition, leading to a year-long postponement. With new discussions scheduled between late November and early December, and a formal adoption targeted for late 2026, the UN agency is once again attempting to secure a deal that would redefine maritime operations. This proposed carbon pricing mechanism mirrors the EU's Emissions Trading System (ETS), which now imposes direct costs on shipping emissions within European waters, highlighting the significant financial implications of such global frameworks. The protracted nature of these negotiations underscores the complexity of aligning international interests on an issue with such profound economic and environmental consequences.

## Who Are the Biggest Polluters? An Emissions Snapshot

Data from Future Cleantech Architects (FCA), as cited in the article, paints a clear picture of the industry's heaviest emitters. Approximately 90% of the global shipping fleet still relies on oil-based fuels. The "biggest polluters" are identified as tankers, container ships, and bulk carriers, followed by cruise ships and general cargo ships. Collectively, these five vessel types are responsible for an enormous 664 million tonnes of CO2 emissions annually. To put this into perspective, there are roughly 6,000 container ships, 8,200 oil tankers, 13,000 bulk carriers, and 960 cruise ships navigating the world’s oceans, each contributing to this footprint. Regionally, Europe accounts for 14% of these shipping emissions, while China contributes 17% and the USA 9%. ## Emerging Pathways to Decarbonisation: Efficiency, Wind, and Electrification

Despite the regulatory hurdles, technological solutions for reducing shipping emissions are rapidly advancing. The FCA's analysis highlights a multi-faceted approach, starting with immediate efficiency gains. Slow steaming, for instance, can reduce fuel consumption by up to 20% for every 10% reduction in speed, particularly effective for container ships. Broader efficiency improvements—such as better hydrodynamics, smarter logistics, and digital optimisation—can yield 5-20% GHG emission reductions, directly impacting a vessel's [CII ratings](/cii.html). Beyond operational adjustments, innovative propulsion methods are gaining traction. Wind-assisted propulsion systems, like rotor sails or wing sails, could cut GHG emissions by up to 30% per nautical mile, proving especially beneficial for tankers and bulk carriers on windy transatlantic or transpacific routes, with around 150 ships already implementing such systems. For even deeper cuts, the focus shifts to alternative fuels and electrification. Battery-electric solutions are touted for smaller cargo vessels, with China already pioneering a 120-metre all-electric container vessel. Containerised battery swapping systems at ports could further reduce CO2 emissions by 35 million tonnes per year. Ultimately, a switch to green marine fuels could offer up to a 90% reduction, while fully electric or hybrid propulsion technologies promise up to 100% emission cuts.

## Europe's Leadership in Regulatory Action

While global consensus remains elusive, regional initiatives are pushing the industry forward. The European Union, through its FuelEU Maritime Regulation, is a prime example of proactive policy. This regulation mandates a gradual reduction in the average annual greenhouse gas intensity of energy used by ships, directly impacting vessels that continue to run on heavy fuel oil. Such measures leverage extensive [emissions data](/charts.html) derived from EU MRV (Monitoring, Reporting, Verification) regulations, providing the transparency needed to enforce compliance and drive investment in cleaner technologies. The imminent costs associated with the EU ETS for emissions within European waters further incentivise decarbonisation, demonstrating that even without a comprehensive IMO deal, a significant transition in the shipping sector is underway, albeit at a potentially slower pace than a globally unified framework.

## What this means for shipping emissions

The ongoing struggle for a global net-zero shipping framework at the IMO underscores the immense challenge and the urgent need for action. With key vessel types like tankers, container ships, and bulk carriers still dominating the emissions landscape, the industry's environmental footprint is substantial. While political agreements lag, technological solutions—from efficiency upgrades and wind-assisted propulsion to full electrification and alternative fuels—are maturing. Regional regulatory pressures, particularly from the European Union, are already creating tangible incentives for change, impacting operating costs and pushing companies towards greener practices. The future of shipping emissions hinges not just on breakthroughs in technology, but critically on robust policy frameworks and significant investment that can accelerate the adoption of these solutions across the global fleet.
