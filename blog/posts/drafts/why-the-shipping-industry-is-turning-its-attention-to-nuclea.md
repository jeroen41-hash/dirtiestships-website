The global shipping industry, responsible for approximately 3% of all global greenhouse gas emissions—a staggering billion tonnes of CO2-equivalent annually—is under intensifying pressure to decarbonize. With stringent regulations like the EU Emissions Trading System (ETS) and the IMO's Carbon Intensity Indicator (CII) ratings now directly impacting operational costs and commercial viability, the search for truly low-carbon alternatives has moved beyond incremental improvements. A recent paper by the British Ports Association and Lloyd's Register suggests a radical, long-considered solution is now "shifting from the theoretical into practical consideration": advanced nuclear technologies.

## The Heavy Burden of Maritime Emissions

Commercial shipping's environmental footprint extends far beyond CO2. The combustion of heavy fuel oil (HFO) by the vast majority of the global fleet releases significant quantities of sulfur oxides (SOx), nitrogen oxides (NOx), and black carbon, contributing to worsening coastal air quality and respiratory illnesses. While technologies like electric shore power (Onshore Power Supply or OPS) exist to reduce emissions at berth, their adoption remains woefully low; only 3% of ports offer OPS services, even as 46-72% of cruise ships are equipped to use it. This means most vessels continue to burn heavy bunker fuel at anchor or dockside, contributing to localized pollution and adding to their overall carbon profile, which is meticulously tracked by data from systems like EMSA MRV and made transparent through our [emissions data](/charts.html).

## Nuclear Power: A Game Changer for Ports and Propulsion?

The joint paper highlights the potential for advanced nuclear technologies, specifically Small Modular Reactors (SMRs) and microreactors, to revolutionize maritime energy. These technologies could serve dual purposes: powering port operations and potentially propelling vessels. For ports, SMRs offer a reliable, low-carbon baseload power source to meet the surging electricity demand from electrified cargo-handling equipment, cold storage, shore power, and even the production of green hydrogen and synthetic fuels. Ports are increasingly becoming major electricity consumers, often located at the intersection of constrained grids and heavy industrial demand, making SMRs an attractive solution to provide stable, independent power. While only four civilian nuclear-powered merchant ships have ever been built, with Russia's *Sevmorput* being the sole commercial vessel currently operating with nuclear propulsion, the concept of nuclear-powered vessels is gaining renewed attention as the industry grapples with profound decarbonization targets.

## Economic and Regulatory Implications: CII and ETS

For shipping companies navigating the complex regulatory landscape, nuclear technologies present a compelling, albeit long-term, answer to escalating compliance costs. A nuclear-powered vessel, emitting virtually zero operational CO2, SOx, or NOx, would achieve an unparalleled [CII rating](/cii.html). Such a vessel would immediately leap to the top of [company rankings](/companies.html) for environmental performance, effectively future-proofing against increasingly stringent carbon intensity targets.

Similarly, ports powered by SMRs offering extensive shore power capabilities would enable docked vessels to drastically reduce their CO2 emissions, directly impacting a company's EU ETS liabilities. As the price of carbon allowances continues to rise, investing in solutions that eliminate or significantly reduce emissions, even if initially expensive (a nuclear container ship is estimated at $228-582 million compared to $119.6 million for a conventional one), could become economically viable. The Port of Corpus Christi's study into SMR-powered microgrids signals the industry's serious exploration of these benefits.

## The Path Forward: Challenges and Strategic Significance

The paper wisely cautions that advanced nuclear technology is "not imminent for every port, nor is it a universal solution." Significant hurdles remain, including initial investment costs, complex regulatory frameworks, safety perceptions, and the need for specialized infrastructure. The proposed tiered readiness model, from "nuclear-aware" to "nuclear-enabled," underscores the phased approach required. However, for ports with large, growing electricity demands, grid constraints, and a strategic imperative for low-carbon resilience, nuclear presents a uniquely compact, reliable, and low-emission package. It offers 24/7 baseload power, overcoming the intermittency of renewable sources and reducing reliance on regional grids.

## What this means for shipping emissions

While the widespread adoption of nuclear power in shipping and ports is still years, if not decades, away, its serious consideration marks a significant turning point. This technology offers a pathway to fundamentally eliminate direct operational greenhouse gas emissions, SOx, NOx, and black carbon, which contribute significantly to the 1 billion tonnes of CO2-equivalent emitted annually. As regulatory pressures like CII and EU ETS costs continue to mount, nuclear power could provide the ultimate, long-term solution for decarbonization, transforming the "dirtiest ships" into the cleanest. The journey will be complex, but the strategic significance for achieving deep emissions cuts is undeniable.
