The global shipping industry faces immense pressure to decarbonize, and the choice of marine fuels is paramount to achieving ambitious emissions reduction targets. A new comprehensive set of lifecycle assessments (LCAs) from the Society for Gas as a Marine Fuel (SGMF) has provided a critical reference point, unequivocally demonstrating that simply switching to an "alternative fuel" is not enough; the **production pathway** of that fuel can matter as much, if not more, than the fuel molecule itself for true greenhouse gas (GHG) reductions. This insight has profound implications for how we evaluate shipping's environmental performance, impacting everything from [CII ratings](/cii.html) to the effectiveness of the EU Emissions Trading System (ETS).

## Beyond the Funnel: The Well-to-Wake Imperative

For too long, the focus in marine emissions has been primarily on "tank-to-wake" – what comes out of a ship's exhaust stack. However, as the SGMF's latest studies highlight, a true measure of environmental impact requires a "well-to-wake" perspective, encompassing emissions from fuel production, transport, and consumption. SGMF has now completed LCAs for LNG, ammonia, and methanol, establishing these three as frontrunners in the race for future marine fuels. The consistent conclusion across these assessments, as noted by SGMF's Claudia Beumer, is that "the pathway matters." This poses a significant challenge for current regulatory frameworks like EU MRV data, which predominantly capture direct tank-to-wake emissions.

## Deconstructing the "Alternative" Fuels: A Closer Look

The SGMF studies offer nuanced insights into each fuel's potential and pitfalls:

*   **Ammonia:** On headline GHG performance, ammonia makes the strongest showing for maximum decarbonization potential. The 2024 assessment found well-to-wake reductions of up to 61% against conventional oil fuels. However, this impressive figure is heavily contingent on the hydrogen used to manufacture it being produced renewably (e.g., green ammonia). While ammonia combustion produces no CO2, careful control of nitrous oxide (N2O) and NOx emissions, alongside significant toxicity and handling challenges, are critical considerations.
*   **LNG:** Positioned as the mature, near-term option, fossil LNG can cut well-to-wake emissions by 11% to 25% against MGO, depending on engine technology, with tank-to-wake savings reaching up to 29%. Its Achilles' heel is methane slip, where uncombusted methane – a potent GHG – can account for as much as 12% of lifecycle emissions in some engines. The longer-term potential of LNG lies in its ability to utilize existing infrastructure for biomethane or synthetic methane, with some biogenic pathways even producing net-negative lifecycle emissions.
*   **Methanol:** This fuel offers operational advantages, being liquid at ambient temperatures and easier to handle than cryogenic LNG or toxic ammonia. It's one of shipping's fastest-growing alternative fuels. However, its upstream carbon emissions are a significant weakness. Lifecycle studies consistently show that conventional fossil methanol offers little benefit, and can even perform worse than oil fuels on a well-to-wake basis. In stark contrast, bio- and e-methanol produced with renewable energy can deliver "very large reductions," illustrating the critical importance of the production pathway.

## The Regulatory Blind Spot: CII, EU ETS, and Upstream Emissions

The SGMF findings expose a potential disconnect in current and evolving maritime regulations. For instance, a vessel's [CII rating](/cii.html) is based on its Annual Efficiency Ratio (AER), primarily a tank-to-wake metric. A ship running on fossil methanol might achieve a good CII score due to lower direct CO2 emissions, even while its overall well-to-wake impact could be worse than traditional fuels. This creates a risk of "carbon leakage" where emissions are simply shifted upstream, rather than genuinely reduced.

Similarly, the [EU ETS carbon costs](/news-feed.html) currently apply to reported CO2 emissions under EU MRV, which is largely tank-to-wake. Companies transitioning to fuels with high upstream emissions but lower direct CO2 might pay less in ETS costs, yet still contribute significantly to global warming. For a true and equitable decarbonization pathway, future iterations of the ETS and other carbon pricing mechanisms will need to evolve to incorporate well-to-wake emissions, ensuring that the true environmental cost of a fuel is reflected in its price. Without this, the incentive structure may inadvertently steer investment towards less impactful "alternative" fuels. This also complicates accurate [company rankings](/companies.html) if only direct emissions are considered.

## Making Informed Decisions: Beyond the Hype

SGMF explicitly avoids declaring a single "winner" among the alternative fuels, instead emphasizing that the crucial question for owners making billion-dollar fleet decisions is "where that fuel came from and how it was made." For maximum decarbonization potential, low-carbon ammonia and renewable methanol lead. For technology maturity, infrastructure, and immediate emissions reductions, LNG (especially fossil LNG) remains strongest. For ease of storage, handling, and conversion, methanol has an advantage.

This underscores the need for greater transparency and full lifecycle accounting from fuel suppliers and shipping companies. As new vessels are ordered and existing ones converted, the environmental credentials cannot be judged on the fuel type alone. Detailed provenance of the fuel and its production methods must become a standard part of procurement and reporting.

## What this means for shipping emissions

The SGMF's comprehensive lifecycle assessments serve as a stark reminder: genuine decarbonization of the shipping industry requires a holistic well-to-wake approach. Relying solely on tank-to-wake metrics risks incentivizing choices that offer little real climate benefit. Regulators must adapt policies like CII and the EU ETS to account for upstream emissions, pushing the industry to invest in truly green fuel production pathways. For shipowners and operators, the message is clear: the pathway matters. Understanding and prioritizing truly sustainable fuel production will be critical in driving down global shipping emissions and achieving long-term climate goals, radically changing how we interpret [emissions data](/charts.html).
