The global shipping industry is under unprecedented pressure to decarbonize, with regulations like the EU Emissions Trading System (ETS) and the Carbon Intensity Indicator (CII) transforming operational economics and compliance. Understanding where emissions originate and how they are attributed across the complex maritime supply chain is no longer an academic exercise, but a critical imperative. A new initiative by DatamarLab, detailed in their upcoming newsletter, cuts to the heart of this challenge, exploring how "One Emission" can be accounted across "Three Accounts" – shipowners, ports, and cargo owners – offering a granular view that is essential for effective decarbonization strategies and accurate regulatory compliance.

## Deconstructing the Maritime Carbon Footprint: Beyond the Shipowner

The DatamarLab newsletter, titled "One Emission, Three Accounts: Understanding a Complex Chain," highlights the often-overlooked complexity of emissions accounting in shipping. While the shipowner operates the vessel and is often seen as the primary polluter, the reality is that emissions are influenced by decisions made across the entire value chain. The publication outlines the distinction between Scope 1, 2, and 3 emissions:
*   **Scope 1:** Direct emissions from sources owned or controlled by the company (e.g., fuel burned by vessels).
*   **Scope 2:** Indirect emissions from the generation of purchased energy (e.g., electricity used at terminals).
*   **Scope 3:** Other indirect emissions that occur in a company's value chain, both upstream and downstream (e.g., emissions related to contracted transport by an exporter).

This framework is applied to three key groups: shipowners/operators, ports/terminals, and exporters/importers/freight forwarders. For example, while a ship’s fuel consumption is Scope 1 for the operator, the emissions from transporting that cargo become a Scope 3 concern for the importer. This multi-party involvement complicates data collection for platforms like DirtiestShips.com, which primarily relies on vessel-level data from EU MRV, but underscores the need for a collaborative approach to truly address shipping's carbon footprint and accurately reflect environmental performance in [company rankings](/companies.html).

## The EVER FAME Case Study: Granularity in Action

To illustrate these concepts, DatamarLab revisits a proof of concept based on a real voyage by the container ship EVER FAME, operated by Evergreen. The analysis tracks a journey between the East Coast of South America and Singapore in September and October 2025. What makes this study particularly insightful is the depth of operational data utilized: bills of lading, vessel drafts, and port call times.

This data allowed for the calculation of emissions not just by voyage leg, but by specific operational components, including the main engine, auxiliary engines, refrigerated containers, and boilers. The study examined 1,177 bills of lading and nine fuel and speed scenarios. A crucial finding highlighted by DatamarLab is that "distance alone does not explain the carbon footprint of a voyage." Factors such as cargo type (especially refrigerated cargo requiring additional energy), route profile, and propulsion technology significantly influence the actual emissions. This level of detail offers a far more accurate picture than generalized models, providing actionable insights for all stakeholders.

## Implications for CII Ratings and EU ETS Costs

The insights from DatamarLab’s research have profound implications for regulatory compliance, particularly for [CII ratings](/cii.html) and EU ETS carbon costs. The current CII framework, while a vital step, primarily assesses a ship's carbon intensity based on capacity and distance traveled. DatamarLab's finding that "distance alone does not explain the carbon footprint" suggests that a vessel might achieve a good CII rating, yet its actual emissions could be significantly higher due to factors like carrying a large volume of refrigerated cargo or complex port maneuvers, which current CII calculations may not fully capture. This emphasizes the need for more granular data and a holistic view of operational emissions to truly incentivize decarbonization.

Furthermore, the "One Emission, Three Accounts" approach directly impacts EU ETS liabilities. As the article notes, the newsletter discusses the "relationship between emissions accounting, information requirements and carbon costs." With the EU ETS now covering shipping emissions, accurately attributing these emissions across the supply chain becomes paramount. Who ultimately bears the cost of the carbon allowances – the shipowner, the charterer, or can it be passed on to the cargo owner through freight surcharges – hinges on contractual agreements and transparent emissions data. DatamarLab’s work provides a framework for understanding these complex connections, crucial for managing financial risks and driving collaborative emissions reductions.

## Data-Driven Decarbonization and Company Accountability

DatamarLab’s proposal to build a Collaborative Decision-Making model for maritime shipping in South America underscores the broader shift towards data-driven decarbonization. By converting operational information into useful knowledge, companies can make informed choices to evaluate impacts and guide improvements. This collaborative approach, rooted in independent data and scientific foundations, moves beyond blaming individual actors to fostering shared responsibility.

For platforms like DirtiestShips.com, which track and analyze [emissions data](/charts.html) from various sources, such detailed insights are invaluable. Understanding the nuances of how different operational components contribute to overall emissions allows for more accurate assessments of individual vessel performance and more robust comparisons in [company rankings](/companies.html). Companies that embrace this level of detailed analysis and engage in collaborative decision-making will be better positioned to adapt to the evolving regulatory landscape and demonstrate genuine progress in reducing their environmental footprint.

## What this means for shipping emissions

The DatamarLab initiative highlights a critical truth: effective decarbonization in shipping demands a far more granular and collaborative approach than current industry standards often provide. Moving beyond simplistic metrics and embracing comprehensive operational data is essential for accurately attributing emissions, managing regulatory costs under schemes like the EU ETS, and driving genuine improvements in [CII ratings](/cii.html). True progress in reducing shipping emissions will not come from isolated efforts but from a shared understanding of carbon footprints across the entire maritime value chain, leveraging science and data to guide every decision.
