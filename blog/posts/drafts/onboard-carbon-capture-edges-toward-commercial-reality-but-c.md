Onboard carbon capture and storage (OCCS) is transitioning from theoretical concept to maritime reality, presenting a complex but potentially critical pathway for the shipping industry to meet increasingly stringent emissions regulations. With the EU Emissions Trading System (ETS) now fully encompassing maritime transport and global carbon intensity targets looming, the ability to verifiably capture and dispose of CO2 directly from ships' exhausts could significantly alter compliance strategies and emissions profiles, as evidenced by pioneering projects and evolving regulatory frameworks.

## Onboard Carbon Capture: A Promising, Yet Complex, Reality

The development of OCCS systems has advanced beyond laboratory testing, with several pilot projects now operating at sea. A notable example is the Solvang ethylene carrier *Clipper Eris*, which has been fitted with a full-scale Wärtsilä carbon capture system since early 2025. This system demonstrates the technical feasibility of OCCS, capturing over seventy percent of CO2 from the main engine exhaust, equating to approximately fifty tonnes of CO2 per day. Some performance tests have even pushed capture efficiency to around seventy-eight percent, as reported by Wärtsilä and Solvang.

The operational success has spurred further investment, with Solvang committing to seven new very large gas carriers (VLGCs), under construction at HD Hyundai Heavy Industries for 2026-2027 delivery, being built "CCS-ready." This indicates a growing confidence in OCCS as a viable emissions reduction technology for specific vessel types, particularly those with available space and energy for the capture process. However, it is crucial to distinguish between gross capture from the exhaust stream and the net lifecycle reduction, which must account for the energy consumption of the capture plant itself, including fuel for operation, CO2 compression, transport ashore, and ultimate disposal. This distinction forms a central tenet of the ongoing regulatory debate.

## Regulatory Hurdles and Market Incentives

The regulatory landscape for OCCS is rapidly evolving but remains fragmented. The IMO has made progress, with MEPC 84 in April 2026 agreeing on a work plan to develop a regulatory framework, targeting completion by 2028. This includes guidance on accounting, verification, and certification aligned with the calculation of a ship's greenhouse gas fuel intensity. Yet, significant questions persist, such as the regulation of captured CO2 discharged at sea. Furthermore, the broader IMO Net-Zero Framework, which includes a global GHG pricing mechanism, faces adoption uncertainty, with a crucial vote scheduled for December 2026. This global framework's success is paramount, as it directly impacts the financial incentive for shipowners to invest in expensive capture technology.

In Europe, the situation is more concrete due to the full integration of shipping into the EU ETS as of January 1, 2026. Operators must now surrender allowances for 100% of applicable CO2 emissions on EU-linked voyages, with allowance prices running at €75-90 per tonne through 2026. Verifiable captured CO2, sent for permanent storage or qualifying utilization, can, in principle, reduce the emissions against which allowances must be surrendered. For a ship with 30,000 tonnes of annual emissions, a verified fifty percent net reduction could avoid surrendering allowances for 15,000 tonnes, translating to an avoided cost of €1-1.3 million annually at current prices. This direct financial incentive is a strong driver for OCCS adoption within the EU.

However, the EU's parallel regulation, FuelEU Maritime, takes a far more cautious stance. The European Commission explicitly states that onboard capture is not currently recognized as a compliance technology under FuelEU, citing limited maturity and the absence of an international framework for traceability and permanent storage. This regulatory dichotomy presents a challenge for shipowners seeking integrated compliance solutions.

## The Cost-Benefit Equation for Shipowners

The economic viability of OCCS is a critical factor for its widespread adoption. Wärtsilä estimates the all-in cost of its system, combining capital and operating expenses, at $54-76 per tonne of CO2 captured. When compared to current EU ETS allowance prices, which can range from €75-90 per tonne (approximately $80-97 USD at current exchange rates), OCCS presents a potentially competitive alternative to purchasing allowances. This calculation highlights that OCCS, particularly in regions with established carbon pricing mechanisms like the EU, can offer a tangible avoided cost, though not a direct profit.

However, shipowners must weigh these avoided costs against the substantial capital investment for installation, as well as ongoing operational expenses for fuel, sorbent, maintenance, CO2 liquefaction, transport, and storage. ## OCCS and Future Compliance Metrics

The integration of OCCS into compliance metrics like [CII ratings](/cii.html) and FuelEU Maritime is pivotal. While OCCS has the potential to significantly improve a ship's operational carbon intensity by reducing its reported CO2 emissions, the actual benefit for CII will depend on how captured emissions are accounted for and verified. The IMO's efforts to develop accounting and verification guidance are crucial here.

For FuelEU Maritime, the current non-recognition of OCCS is a major hurdle. This regulation focuses on the carbon intensity of energy used on board, making post-combustion capture a more complex fit than a direct fuel switch. Until a verifiable accounting method and international framework are in place, OCCS will likely not count towards FuelEU compliance, creating a situation where it can reduce ETS liability but not necessarily FuelEU penalties. This highlights the need for a harmonized global regulatory approach to truly unlock OCCS's potential for comprehensive emissions reduction. ## What this means for shipping emissions

Onboard carbon capture is undeniably moving towards commercial reality, offering a tangible, albeit complex, pathway for certain segments of the global shipping fleet to reduce their absolute CO2 emissions. For vessels unable to transition immediately to alternative fuels, OCCS can serve as a vital transitional technology, especially within the EU where it can directly reduce significant ETS liabilities. However, its ultimate impact on global shipping emissions hinges on the development of robust, internationally recognized accounting and verification frameworks, the establishment of comprehensive CO2 offloading and permanent storage infrastructure, and a clear, harmonized regulatory signal that incentivizes its adoption across all major compliance mechanisms. While not a universal solution, OCCS is poised to become a significant component of many shipping companies' future compliance strategies, driving down their emissions footprint if the surrounding ecosystem matures sufficiently.
