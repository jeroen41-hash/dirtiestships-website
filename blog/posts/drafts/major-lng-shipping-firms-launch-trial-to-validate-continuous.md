The global shipping industry, under increasing pressure to decarbonize, currently relies predominantly on estimated emissions data derived from fuel consumption. However, a significant joint development project (JDP) featuring Lloyd’s Register, Seapeak, Kongsberg Maritime, and Tunable, aims to challenge this status quo by validating continuous emissions monitoring systems (CEMS) on board an LNG carrier. This initiative promises to usher in an era of more precise, real-world emissions tracking, with profound implications for regulatory compliance, [CII ratings](/cii.html), and the financial impact of carbon costs on shipping companies.

### The Drive for Precision: Continuous Emissions Monitoring

Announced at Gastech 2026, this JDP seeks to rigorously compare actual CO2 emissions recorded by Kongsberg Maritime's CEMS (which integrates Tunable’s gas analysis technology) against both engine test bed data and conventional default emissions factors. This trial will unfold on a newly built LNG carrier, with data collection and analysis continuing throughout 2027 following its early 2027 delivery. The core objective is to quantify the differences between estimated and directly measured emissions and to assess the regulatory applicability of direct CO2 measurement within existing frameworks like those set by the IMO and the EU.

As Panos Mitrou, SVP Shipping Strategy at Lloyd’s Register, highlights, the industry's current reliance on "default emissions factors and test bed data to estimate environmental performance" leaves a gap in understanding. This project directly addresses that by introducing "direct measurements from a vessel operating in real world conditions," providing valuable insights for accurate reporting and operational optimization. For platforms like DirtiestShips.com, which thrive on accurate, verifiable data, the validation of CEMS represents a critical step towards transparent and undeniable emissions tracking.

### Bridging the Data Gap: EU MRV, IMO DCS, and CII Accuracy

The implications of validated CEMS for existing and upcoming regulations are substantial. Both the IMO Data Collection System (DCS) and the EU Monitoring, Reporting, and Verification (MRV) regulation currently permit the use of CEMS, but the default and most common method remains fuel-based calculation using standardized emission factors. By independently verifying the accuracy and reliability of CEMS, this JDP could pave the way for wider adoption of direct measurement.

This shift would have a direct impact on the accuracy of reported emissions data, which underpins critical metrics such as [CII ratings](/cii.html). A ship's CII rating, which assesses its operational carbon intensity, is calculated using reported annual emissions data. If CEMS provides a more accurate, granular picture of CO2 output, it could lead to more precise CII scores, potentially revealing that some vessels perform better or worse than their calculated estimates suggest. This enhanced precision is crucial for robust [company rankings](/companies.html) and for truly understanding which fleets are making genuine progress in decarbonization. The project's focus on "assessing the regulatory applicability" could accelerate the acceptance of CEMS as a primary method for statutory reporting.

### Implications for Operators and Emissions Costs

For shipping firms like Seapeak, a partner in this JDP, the benefits extend beyond mere compliance. James Cruickshank, Director – Marine & Technical Services at Seapeak, emphasizes the commitment to "practical, data-driven solutions that enhance both environmental performance and operational efficiency." Continuous, real-time emissions data allows operators to move beyond annual snapshots, providing immediate feedback on the carbon impact of different operational profiles, voyage routes, and engine settings. This granular understanding can directly support emissions minimization strategies, identifying "opportunities to further reduce greenhouse gas emissions across our fleet."

From an economic perspective, more accurate emissions data directly impacts EU Emissions Trading System (ETS) costs. Under the EU ETS, ships pay for a percentage of their reported CO2 emissions. If CEMS reveals that actual emissions are consistently lower than estimated due to more efficient operations or a discrepancy in default factors, it could lead to reduced carbon liabilities. Conversely, if estimates have been optimistically low, CEMS could highlight higher actual emissions, leading to increased ETS costs. The ultimate outcome is not necessarily lower costs, but *fairer* and more *accurate* costs based on verifiable data, driving targeted investment in decarbonization technologies and operational improvements.

### The Independent Verification & Future of Reporting

Lloyd’s Register's role as the independent verifier is critical to the credibility of this project. Their evaluation of measured data will provide the "robust evidence needed to assess the accuracy, transparency and regulatory applicability of CEMS-based reporting," as noted by Eirik Agren of Kongsberg Maritime. This independent validation is key to building trust among shipowners, charterers, and regulators, enabling a broader acceptance of CEMS.

The project signals a clear direction for the future of maritime emissions reporting: a move away from generalized estimates towards verifiable, real-time measurements. By integrating CEMS data with other operational metrics, the industry gains a more holistic view of performance. This will not only improve the quality of data available on platforms like DirtiestShips.com, informing our [emissions data](/charts.html) and analysis, but also empower stakeholders to make more informed decisions towards a greener future.

### What this means for shipping emissions

This pioneering JDP represents a crucial step towards standardizing continuous, real-time emissions measurement in shipping. By validating CEMS, the industry is moving closer to an era of undeniable emissions data, directly impacting the accuracy of [CII ratings](/cii.html), refining EU ETS cost analysis, and enabling more effective decarbonization strategies. The insights gained will undoubtedly reshape how emissions performance is understood, reported, and ultimately, reduced across the global fleet.
