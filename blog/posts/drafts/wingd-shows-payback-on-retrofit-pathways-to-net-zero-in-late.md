The global shipping industry stands at a critical juncture, with mounting pressure to decarbonize. A recent report from marine power company WinGD highlights a significant but often overlooked pathway: retrofitting existing vessels for alternative fuels. While the technology for such conversions is increasingly available, the report underscores that aligning fuel economics and robust regulatory frameworks is paramount to achieving ambitious net-zero targets and transforming the emissions profile of the global fleet.

## Retrofitting: A Viable Path to Decarbonization

WinGD's latest Fuel Economics Report, based on sophisticated modelling, presents a compelling case for retrofitting. The analysis specifically models a 16,000 TEU container vessel undergoing conversion in 2030 to utilize WinGD’s high-pressure dual-fuel engines for LNG (X-DF-HP), methanol (X-DF-M), or ammonia (X-DF-A). The key finding? All three retrofit pathways demonstrably outperform continued operation with Very Low Sulphur Fuel Oil (VLSFO) over the vessel's lifetime, both in terms of overall costs and, critically, emissions reduction.

This is a significant insight for shipowners grappling with compliance and future-proofing their assets. Rather than solely focusing on newbuilds, the report suggests a powerful avenue to address the emissions of the vast existing fleet. For operators tracked on DirtiestShips.com, this means a direct opportunity to improve their environmental footprint and potentially climb our [company rankings](/companies.html) by proactively investing in cleaner technologies.

## The Economics of Green Fuels: Payback Periods and Policy Gaps

While all retrofits show long-term benefits, the WinGD report delves into the varying financial returns and emissions reductions across different alternative fuels. Low-GHG LNG emerges as offering the strongest and earliest financial return, with a modelled payback period of just six years. This makes it an attractive "first step" for many operators.

However, for substantially deeper emissions reductions necessary for net-zero, fuels like ammonia and bio-methanol are highlighted. These options, while superior environmentally, require longer payback periods under current economic assumptions. This points to a crucial policy gap: the report indicates that a subsidy of US$126-330 per tonne of CO2 equivalent emissions would be necessary to achieve a five-year payback for these more ambitious decarbonization pathways. This level of incentive is comparable to the US$100-380 remedial units already being considered by the IMO for excess emissions, suggesting that 'reward' mechanisms for clean fuels need to be as robust as 'penalty' mechanisms for polluting ones.

A vital takeaway for stakeholders is that the upfront capital expenditure (CAPEX) for a retrofit is not the dominant factor in the business case. Instead, the lifetime economics are far more influenced by fluctuating fuel prices, the GHG intensity of those fuels, and the specific regulatory treatment applied. As WinGD's Head of Strategic Marketing Carmelo Cartalemi states, operators need "visibility not only on what a retrofit costs, but on what every ton of fuel will cost and how its emissions will be treated by regulations over the vessel’s remaining life."

## The Regulatory Imperative: EU ETS, CII, and IMO's Role

The findings from WinGD's report directly intersect with existing and upcoming regulatory pressures on the shipping industry. The modelling of "carbon costs" under a global pricing regime mirrors the reality of the EU Emissions Trading System (ETS), where shipping has been included since January 2024. For vessels operating in EU waters, these carbon costs are no longer theoretical; they are an immediate financial reality that directly impacts operational profitability. Retrofitting to lower-emission fuels can significantly reduce exposure to these costs, offering a tangible financial incentive beyond mere compliance.

Furthermore, the operational emissions reductions achieved through retrofits will have a direct and positive impact on a vessel's Carbon Intensity Indicator (CII) rating. Vessels achieving better [CII ratings](/cii.html) will be more attractive to charterers and reduce the risk of falling into 'D' or 'E' categories, which trigger mandatory improvement plans. By embracing retrofits, shipowners can proactively manage their CII compliance and maintain market competitiveness.

The report also acts as a clear message to regulators, particularly the IMO, emphasizing that without strong and clear policy signals, the industry's most cost-effective decarbonization options may not include the truly transformative fuels. The gap between technologically feasible solutions and economically viable deep decarbonization highlights the need for a global framework that provides the necessary financial incentives and long-term regulatory certainty.

## Emissions Data and the Future Fleet

For a platform like DirtiestShips.com, which relies on EU MRV data, CII ratings, and ETS cost analysis to track the industry's progress, the implications of this report are profound. As more operators potentially move towards retrofitting, our [emissions data](/charts.html) will capture the shift in actual CO2 output. The ability to track the adoption rates of alternative fuels and their real-world impact on emissions will be crucial for validating the effectiveness of these pathways and informing future policy decisions. The transition from VLSFO to LNG, methanol, or ammonia will be measurable, providing concrete evidence of the industry's decarbonization efforts and highlighting the leaders in this vital shift.

## What this means for shipping emissions

WinGD's report clarifies that the technical solutions for decarbonizing existing ships through retrofitting are increasingly mature and, over the long term, more cost-effective than simply continuing with VLSFO. However, the pace and depth of this transition are critically dependent on strong, consistent policy support and reward mechanisms for truly low and zero-emission fuels. Without clear regulatory signals and financial incentives, shipping risks missing its net-zero targets, with the most impactful decarbonization options facing uphill battles for adoption. The challenge for the industry and regulators alike is to bridge this gap, translating technical feasibility into widespread commercial reality to accelerate the reduction of shipping's CO2 footprint.
