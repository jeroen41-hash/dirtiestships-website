The global shipping industry is facing a significant surge in a specialized segment: Pure Car and Truck Carriers (PCTCs). New data reveals a hardening dominance by Chinese shipyards in PCTC orders, fueled by an unprecedented boom in China's automobile exports. This rapid expansion of PCTC capacity, while meeting immediate market demand, carries profound implications for global shipping emissions, regulatory compliance under frameworks like the EU ETS, and the future performance of carriers on critical metrics such as their CII ratings.

## China's PCTC Dominance and the Surge in Demand

The shift in PCTC shipbuilding is stark. According to Clarksons Research, out of 58 new PCTC orders placed worldwide from the start of the year through last month, an astonishing 56 went to Chinese shipyards. This near-monopoly highlights a strategic realignment, where even European companies, traditionally relying on Korean yards, are now placing large-scale orders in China. This surge reverses a trend from last year when oversupply concerns led to a mere nine orders.

The driving force is China's booming automobile export market. The China Association of Automobile Manufacturers (CAAM) reports that in the first half of this year, China exported 5,096,000 vehicles, a substantial 65.3% increase year-on-year. June alone saw exports surpass 1 million for the first time. This unprecedented export volume has created a significant shortage of transport capacity, even driving up prices for secondhand vessels; a 26-year-old, 4,310 CEU PCTC recently sold for about $42 million in China, tripling its minimum bid price. Executives, such as those at BYD, have openly stated that "overseas exports were hampered by transport constraints this year," underscoring the urgency.

Companies are reacting decisively. Chinese automakers and shipping companies are securing their fleets domestically, with BYD reportedly ordering 10 9,200 CEU-class PCTCs and Zhongnan Shipping ordering four. Crucially, overseas carriers are also expanding their Chinese-built fleets, with European carriers signing 33 contracts. Italy's Grimaldi leads this charge, ordering 12 PCTCs from three Chinese shipbuilders. Even Ray Car Carriers, an Israeli shipowner that had exclusively ordered PCTCs from Korean shipbuilders for two decades, recently placed an order for ten 8,200 CEU-class PCTCs with China State Shipbuilding Corporation (CSSC).

This concentration of orders in China is attributed not only to strong price competitiveness but also to the fact that PCTCs do not require cutting-edge technology, allowing for lower unit costs. The saturation of Korean shipyards with orders for large container ships and LNG carriers further funnels demand to China, which also leverages state support and ship finance to encourage domestic orders.

## CII Ratings, EU ETS, and the Fleet Expansion Paradox

This rapid fleet expansion, driven primarily by demand and cost, presents a complex challenge for the shipping industry's decarbonization efforts. While newbuilds often incorporate some efficiency improvements, the sheer volume of new vessels inevitably means an increase in absolute emissions. Furthermore, the emphasis on cost-effectiveness over "cutting-edge technology" in PCTC construction raises questions about the long-term efficiency and decarbonization potential of these vessels.

Carriers operating these new PCTCs, especially those calling at European ports, will face immediate and growing scrutiny under the EU Emissions Trading System (ETS). Each voyage to or from an EU port will incur a carbon cost, directly impacting profitability. Companies like Grimaldi, expanding their fleets significantly, will see their overall carbon footprint grow, influencing their standing in [company rankings](/companies.html) based on emissions data. The inclusion of shipping in the EU ETS means that without proactive measures, these new capacities could translate into substantial financial liabilities.

Moreover, the Carbon Intensity Indicator (CII) ratings, which came into force last year, will apply to these new PCTCs from day one. Owners will be under pressure to ensure their new vessels can achieve and maintain favorable CII ratings, potentially requiring optimized operational profiles, slow steaming, or further technological upgrades. The challenge is amplified if the rush to build prioritizes capacity over the highest possible energy efficiency. ## Strategic Shifts and Emission Implications

The strategic pivot in PCTC shipbuilding towards China signifies more than just a change in sourcing. It highlights the growing influence of Asian markets on global shipping infrastructure and the potential for a divergence in fleet development strategies. If cost-efficiency continues to outweigh advanced green technologies in segments like PCTCs, it could complicate the industry's path to net-zero.

For major players like Grimaldi and Ray Car Carriers, integrating these new, potentially less "cutting-edge" vessels into their existing fleets will require careful management of their overall emission profiles to maintain acceptable CII ratings and control ETS costs. The market's insatiable demand for vehicle transport, driven by an export giant, presents a challenge where meeting immediate needs could inadvertently increase long-term emission burdens.

### What this means for shipping emissions

The dramatic increase in PCTC orders, overwhelmingly concentrated in China, points to a significant boost in global shipping capacity dedicated to automobile transport. While meeting burgeoning demand, this expansion will inevitably lead to an increase in absolute CO2 emissions from the shipping sector. The imperative for carriers to manage their CII ratings and mitigate EU ETS carbon costs will intensify as these new vessels come online. Monitoring through EU MRV data will be crucial to understand the true environmental impact of this strategic shipbuilding shift and hold the industry accountable for its decarbonization targets.
