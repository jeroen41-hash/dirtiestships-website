The European Union is making a significant move to accelerate shipping’s decarbonization, proposing a new mechanism within its revised Emissions Trading System (EU ETS) designed to bridge the formidable price gap between conventional fossil fuels and greener alternatives. This development, outlined in recent amendments, is critical for shipping companies facing escalating EU ETS carbon costs and the pressure to improve their [CII ratings](/cii.html) amidst tightening environmental regulations. The proposed Sustainable Maritime Alternative Propulsion (SMAP) mechanism seeks to dedicate substantial financial support to incentivize the uptake of low- and zero-emission fuels and technologies, fundamentally altering the economic calculus for sustainable shipping.

## The SMAP Mechanism: Bridging the Green Fuel Price Gap

Under the new Article 3gaa of the ETS Directive, the EU Commission plans to reserve a maximum of 110 million EU Allowances (EUAs) between January 1, 2028, and December 31, 2040. These EUAs are specifically earmarked to help cover the price differential between traditional fossil bunker fuels and eligible alternatives, as well as the additional costs associated with deploying zero-emission propulsion systems. This direct financial incentive aims to alleviate one of the biggest barriers to maritime decarbonization: the prohibitive cost of green fuels and technologies.

The coverage rates are tiered, prioritizing the most ambitious solutions. Renewable fuels of non-biological origin (RFNBOs) such as e-ammonia, e-methanol, and e-methane, which are critical green hydrogen derivatives, would receive coverage for 90% of their price premium over conventional fuels. Zero-emission propulsion technologies would also see 90% of their additional deployment and operating costs covered, scaled to the actual emissions cuts achieved. Lower-carbon options, including low-carbon hydrogen and its derivatives (like blue hydrogen), would receive 80% coverage, while biogas and advanced biofuels would be covered at 55% of the price gap. This tiered approach signals the EU’s preference for truly green solutions, pushing for deeper emissions reductions across the global fleet, which will inevitably impact [company rankings](/companies.html) based on their emissions performance.

## Navigating the Nuances: Bonuses and Unanswered Questions

The proposed mechanism includes several bonus percentage points to encourage local production and deployment. Fuels produced from feedstocks within the EU, or in countries with linked ETS or participating in the EU’s "ETS as a service" mechanism, would receive an additional 10 percentage points of fuel price cap coverage. Voyages between EU ports and island ports under a member state’s jurisdiction would gain a further five percentage points, as would propulsion technologies installed in EU shipyards. These bonuses are designed to foster a European green shipping ecosystem and ensure the benefits of the transition circulate within the EU economy.

However, the proposal, as highlighted by hellenicshippingnews.com, leaves some critical questions unanswered. The methodology for determining "average market prices" for low- and zero-emission fuels remains unclear, including whether energy adjustments for different fuel densities would be included, or if existing EU ETS and FuelEU Maritime cost reductions (like FuelEU’s pooling mechanism) would be netted out. The frequency of price updates and how the scheme would handle discrepancies between a company’s actual bunker price and the published reference price are also unspecified. These details are crucial for shipping companies to accurately forecast their operational costs and make informed investment decisions for their fleets.

## Competition for Allowances: The E-Fuel Dilemma

One significant concern raised by industry experts, including the SASHA Coalition, is the absence of dedicated earmarking for RFNBOs within the 110 million EUA pool. Instead, all eligible fuels and technologies, from e-ammonia to biofuels and wind-assisted systems, will draw from a single, undifferentiated allowance pool. If annual demand for these incentives exceeds the available allowances, allocations would be uniformly reduced across all applicants, irrespective of the fuel or technology used.

This "single pool" approach creates a competitive environment for funding, potentially diluting the impact on the most capital-intensive, yet highest-impact, green fuel pathways. The lack of a long-term offtake requirement further complicates the investment case for producers of e-fuels, who need demand certainty to scale up production. While the World Shipping Council (WSC) and HySafe have welcomed the mechanism for its potential to cover up to 100% of RFNBO price premiums, the funding certainty for specific breakthrough technologies like green hydrogen derivatives remains a point of debate.

## Impact on Shipping Emissions and Company Strategies

The SMAP mechanism, if implemented effectively, has the potential to significantly alter the landscape of shipping emissions. By reducing the financial burden of adopting alternative fuels, it incentivizes shipowners to invest in vessels capable of running on e-methanol, e-ammonia, or equipped with advanced propulsion systems. Such investments are vital for improving individual ship and fleet [CII ratings](/cii.html), moving them away from the "D" and "E" grades that trigger corrective action plans.

For companies currently incurring substantial EU ETS costs for their emissions within EU waters, this mechanism offers a pathway to reduce those liabilities by making cleaner operations more financially viable. The EU MRV data, which DirtiestShips.com leverages, will be instrumental in tracking the actual uptake of these alternative fuels and verifying the associated emissions reductions, providing transparency on which companies are truly making strides. The timeline for agreement (Q1 2027) and implementation (2028) means shipping companies have a window to prepare their strategies and lobby for clarification on the outstanding details.

## What this means for shipping emissions

The EU’s proposed SMAP mechanism represents a substantial and direct financial incentive to accelerate shipping's transition away from fossil fuels. By dedicating 110 million EUAs to cover green fuel price premiums and technology costs, it aims to reduce overall CO2 emissions by making sustainable choices more economically attractive. While promising, the success of the mechanism hinges on resolving crucial operational ambiguities and addressing concerns around the competitive allocation of allowances, especially for nascent e-fuel markets. This will be a critical period for policymakers and industry stakeholders to refine the framework to ensure it delivers maximum impact on global shipping emissions.
