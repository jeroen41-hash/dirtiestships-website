The global shipping industry's decarbonization pathway hinges significantly on regulatory frameworks like the EU Emissions Trading System (EU ETS), and a recent intervention from Interferry highlights both the promise and the critical hurdles in accelerating this transition, particularly for the ferry sector. As the EU ETS expands its reach, the spotlight is firmly on whether the financial mechanisms and infrastructure commitments will truly unlock the potential for cleaner shipping, a shift DirtiestShips.com tracks closely through EU MRV data, CII ratings, and carbon cost analysis.

## Interferry's Vision: Electrification and ETS Synergy

Interferry, the global trade association for the ferry industry, has expressed strong support for the European Commission's proposed revision of the EU ETS, viewing it as a pivotal tool for maritime decarbonization. The association champions electrification as a core strategy, advocating for the financially sustainable transition to hybrid and battery-electric ferry operations. This aligns with the broader industry push to reduce direct CO2 emissions, which, as our [emissions data](/charts.html) shows, remains a monumental task across all vessel types. For ferry operators, investing in electric propulsion is a direct route to improving their [CII ratings](/cii.html) and reducing their carbon footprint, thereby easing the burden of increasing ETS compliance costs.

## The Onshore Power Bottleneck: A Critical Infrastructure Gap

Despite the ferry industry's proactive stance on electrification, Interferry points to a significant impediment: the lack of adequate onshore power supply (OPS) infrastructure and robust grid connections in EU passenger ports. Johan Roos, Interferry's Director of Regulatory Affairs, emphasizes that while "larger batteries and longer crossings are technically feasible, and several large ro-pax ferries are already battery-ready," operators are constrained by landside limitations. This means that even with "battery-ready" vessels, the full emissions reduction potential cannot be realized if ships cannot charge efficiently or at all. This infrastructure gap directly impacts the practical application of cleaner technologies, posing a tangible barrier to reducing the emissions captured by EU MRV reporting. Member States' fulfillment of legal obligations to provide OPS and ensure grid capacity is non-negotiable for progress.

## Funding Decarbonization: Ring-fencing ETS Revenues

A central pillar of Interferry's recommendations, as reported by hellenicshippingnews.com, is the call for 100% of maritime ETS revenues to be ring-fenced specifically for maritime decarbonization. This funding, they argue, should support sustainable marine fuels, clean technologies, and, crucially, onshore charging infrastructure. Such dedicated funding is vital to mitigate the substantial upfront capital costs associated with zero-emission vessels, even for operators who surrender no allowances under the ETS due to their clean operations.

While Interferry welcomes the proposed Sustainable Maritime Alternative Propulsion (SMAP) mechanism, it highlights a significant concern: the estimated EUR 1 billion annually for SMAP funding must support the *entire* maritime sector. Interferry fears that demand will vastly exceed available funds, suggesting that Member States must supplement SMAP funding with targeted national support. This financial foresight is critical, as the cost of decarbonization for [company rankings](/companies.html) will be a determining factor in who leads the transition.

## Predictability and Access for Zero-Emission Operators

Finally, Interferry stresses the need for a clear and predictable pathway for zero-emission operators to access SMAP funding *in advance*. This pre-emptive support is essential to incentivize investment in cutting-edge, fully electric technologies. While these operators will ultimately benefit from surrendering no allowances under the ETS, the initial investment hurdle is considerable. Without predictable access to financial assistance, even the most ambitious decarbonization projects could face significant delays or cancellations. This regulatory clarity is paramount for operators evaluating long-term fleet upgrades and investment strategies.

## What this means for shipping emissions

Interferry's intervention underscores a critical truth: while regulatory frameworks like the EU ETS provide the impetus for decarbonization, their effectiveness is intrinsically linked to supporting infrastructure and robust, dedicated funding mechanisms. The ferry sector, often at the forefront of electrification, exemplifies the challenges faced by the wider shipping industry. Progress in reducing CO2 emissions, improving [CII ratings](/cii.html), and managing ETS carbon costs will hinge not just on vessel operators' willingness to invest, but equally on the commitment of ports and Member States to provide the necessary landside infrastructure and ring-fence ETS revenues effectively.
