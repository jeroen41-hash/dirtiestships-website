A groundbreaking study from University College London (UCL) has cast a significant new light on the global shipping industry's environmental footprint, revealing that a substantial 10% of maritime greenhouse gas emissions occur not at sea, but within port areas. This critical finding, based on an exhaustive analysis of 1.2 million voyages by over 43,000 vessels, underscores a previously underestimated segment of shipping's emissions challenge, demanding urgent attention from regulators, port authorities, and shipping companies as the industry grapples with ambitious decarbonization targets and escalating carbon costs.

## The Hidden Burden of In-Port Emissions

The UCL research, detailed by engine.online, exposes a crucial blind spot in the traditional focus on emissions at sea. By meticulously mapping a year's worth of global voyages, the study developed an interactive tracker demonstrating that approximately 575 million tonnes of CO2 equivalent (CO2e) emissions generated by international shipping can be attributed to time spent in port. This 10% figure represents a significant operational inefficiency that, while localized, contributes materially to the industry's overall carbon output. The scale of this issue suggests that a holistic approach to decarbonization must extend beyond ship design and propulsion at sea to encompass the entire operational cycle.

## Island Nations Disproportionately Affected

The study further highlights a striking disparity in the impact of in-port emissions on small island nations. Researchers found that seven out of ten Caribbean countries and eight out of ten East Asia Pacific countries with the highest in-port emissions are small island nations. For these countries, in-port emissions often exceed 20% relative to global and regional averages, as noted by UCL's Yoseph Ismail. This disproportionate burden underscores a critical environmental justice issue and suggests that climate policies must be tailored to support these vulnerable economies in transitioning to cleaner port operations. The economic and social implications for these nations, reliant on maritime trade yet disproportionately affected by its emissions, are substantial.

## Pathways to Cleaner Ports: Cold Ironing and Electrification

The good news is that viable solutions exist. UCL researchers point to port electrification measures as key to reducing these emissions. These include "cold ironing" – shore-to-ship power – where vessels connect to the local electrical grid instead of running auxiliary engines while docked. Other promising solutions involve converting short-sea vessels to battery-electric propulsion and utilizing battery power for idling or maneuvering within port limits. The news article highlights an NYK-operated PCTC receiving shore power in Zeebrugge as an example of this technology in action. Widespread adoption of such measures would not only reduce local air pollution but also contribute directly to improving a vessel's overall operational efficiency and carbon intensity, a crucial factor for its [CII ratings](/cii.html).

## Regulatory Imperatives: IMO, EU ETS, and Corporate Responsibility

The UCL study's timing is particularly pertinent, preceding the International Maritime Organization's (IMO) upcoming Net-Zero Framework (NZF) talks. The data provides IMO delegations with concrete evidence of a significant emissions segment requiring specific policy attention. For regions like the EU, where the EU Emissions Trading System (ETS) now mandates a price on carbon, reducing in-port emissions through cold ironing or alternative power sources can directly translate into lower operational costs and fewer EU Allowances (EUAs) needing to be surrendered for voyages to and from EU/EEA ports. This makes port electrification not just an environmental imperative but an economic one.

Companies looking to improve their standing in [company rankings](/companies.html) will increasingly need to demonstrate proactive measures to reduce their emissions footprint, including those generated in port. While current EU MRV data primarily focuses on emissions at sea, the principles of detailed voyage tracking and emissions attribution used by UCL resonate with the need for granular, verifiable data. Future regulatory frameworks, and indeed the market itself, will likely demand greater transparency and action on this segment of emissions, pushing companies to invest in cleaner port technologies and practices.

## What this means for shipping emissions

The UCL study unequivocally demonstrates that the fight against shipping emissions cannot be won by focusing solely on improvements at sea. The significant 10% of emissions occurring in port areas represents a tangible, actionable opportunity for decarbonization through electrification and cleaner port operations. This insight demands a recalibration of regulatory focus, an acceleration of port infrastructure development, and a concerted effort from shipping companies to embrace technologies like cold ironing. Moving forward, a vessel's true carbon footprint—and a company's commitment to sustainability—must be assessed holistically, from berth to berth.
