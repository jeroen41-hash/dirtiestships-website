The global shipping industry's race to decarbonize is accelerating, as evidenced by a remarkable surge in alternative fuel bunkering activities across major ports worldwide. This past week saw a diverse array of lower-carbon fuels – from LNG and methanol to biofuels and ammonia – being supplied to vessels, signaling a critical juncture in how shipping aims to meet increasingly stringent emissions regulations like the EU ETS and improve [CII ratings](/cii.html). The development of a robust "bunker menu" is not just about fuel choice; it's about the tangible steps the industry is taking to mitigate its environmental footprint, a shift we meticulously track using EU MRV data and carbon cost analysis.

## Asia Leads the Alternative Fuel Frontier

The bustling port of Ningbo-Zhoushan in China offered a stark illustration of this multi-fuel future, recording its first-ever simultaneous bunkering of three different alternative marine fuels on September 30th, as reported by hellenicshippingnews.com. On that single day, vessels received approximately 3,300 metric tons (mt) of LNG, 7,500 mt of methanol, and 3,000 mt of B24 biofuel. This logistical feat underscores the growing operational readiness for diverse fuel types, a necessary evolution as fleets strive for better [CII ratings](/cii.html) and reduced carbon intensity.

Singapore, a pivotal global bunkering hub, also saw significant activity. Bunker supplier Golden Island is set to deliver bio-ethanol to a Maersk vessel, while CMA CGM, in partnership with the Maritime and Port Authority of Singapore (MPA) and Singapore LNG (SLNG), is exploring liquefied biomethane (LBM) bunkering. These developments are not isolated incidents but reflect a concerted industry effort to diversify away from conventional high-emission fuels, directly impacting the carbon footprint measured by EU MRV and the associated EU ETS liabilities.

## Ammonia and Biofuels: The Zero- and Low-Carbon Prospects

Among the emerging alternatives, ammonia is garnering serious attention for its zero-carbon potential. Belgian shipping company CMB.TECH's "Mineral Europa," an ammonia-capable bulk carrier, recently underwent a ship-to-ship transfer of 1,000 mt of green ammonia at Zhoushan, China. This marks a significant milestone, showcasing the operational feasibility of this potent new fuel.

Biofuels continue to offer a more immediate pathway to emissions reductions. In addition to the B24 biofuel delivery in Ningbo-Zhoushan, logistics firm DSV announced its plan to leverage CMA CGM’s biofuel bunkering service to claim an estimated 12,000 mtCO2e reduction in well-to-wake emissions for its ocean freight operations. Furthermore, The Biofuel Company of Saudi Arabia (TBFCO) intends to facilitate 100% biofuel (B100) deliveries in Qatari ports. These actions directly contribute to lowering Scope 3 emissions for cargo owners and improve the overall [company rankings](/company.html) for carriers committed to sustainable practices.

## LNG's Continued Transition and Infrastructure Investment

While newer fuels emerge, LNG continues its role as a key transition fuel, offering substantial CO2 reductions compared to heavy fuel oil. Vitol Bunkers is set to enter the physical LNG bunker supply market in Singapore and other Asian ports, following trials of its 12,500-cbm bunker vessel, Vivit Asia LNG. The LNG-capable car carrier "AICC Fenghuang" recently received 2,600 cbm of LNG in Hong Kong, highlighting its ongoing adoption across various vessel types. In Europe, Axpo completed its first LNG bunker delivery in Valencia, notably with LBM accounting for over half the fuel supplied, demonstrating a pathway for existing LNG infrastructure to evolve towards renewable fuels.

Crucially, the long-term shift towards alternative fuels hinges on robust infrastructure. CMA CGM and ENGIE are planning a biomethane production plant at Haropa Port in Le Havre, France, projected to produce 7,000 mt/year from 2029, with CMA CGM committing to take the entire output for its LNG dual-fuel fleet. Similarly, CMB.TECH is developing an ammonia bunker terminal at Walvis Bay, Namibia, with construction planned for 2027. These significant investments underscore the industry's commitment to building the necessary supply chain for a decarbonized future, albeit with "location-specific" commercial risks as highlighted by CMB.TECH's CTO, Roy Campe, requiring sufficient demand to ensure viability.

## Navigating Regulation and Risk

The increasing diversity of alternative fuels necessitates clear regulatory frameworks. The MPA Singapore's new technical guidelines for ammonia bunkering, covering custody transfer, safety, and crew training, are critical steps towards standardizing operations and ensuring safety for these novel fuels. Such guidelines are vital for widespread adoption, directly impacting the ability of companies to comply with future emissions mandates and manage their EU ETS carbon costs. The commercial risks associated with developing dedicated alternative fuel infrastructure, particularly for fuels like ammonia that lack immediate broad demand, remain a key challenge that could dictate the pace of transition.

## What this means for shipping emissions

The "building the bunker menu" trend, as observed this week, signifies a crucial phase in shipping's decarbonization journey. While the volumes are still relatively small compared to conventional fuels, the sheer diversity of alternative fuels now being actively bunkered and the significant infrastructure investments underway suggest a profound shift. For DirtiestShips.com, this means more complex data to analyze from EU MRV, more variables impacting [CII ratings](/cii.html), and dynamic changes in EU ETS cost calculations for shipping companies. The industry is moving beyond pilot projects towards tangible, albeit geographically fragmented, operational changes. The challenge remains to scale these efforts globally and ensure that the expanded bunker menu translates into meaningful, trackable reductions in CO2 emissions across the entire fleet, moving us closer to the IMO's ambitious climate targets.
