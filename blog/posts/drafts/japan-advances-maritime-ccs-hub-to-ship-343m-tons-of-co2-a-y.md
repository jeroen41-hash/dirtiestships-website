The global shipping industry is grappling with its own decarbonization challenge, but a new development in Japan highlights a critical, often overlooked aspect: the emissions generated by shipping *solutions* to other industries' climate problems. Japan is forging ahead with an ambitious carbon capture and storage (CCS) hub at the Mizushima Industrial Complex, aiming to ship an extraordinary 3.43 million tonnes of liquefied CO2 annually. While this initiative targets hard-to-abate industrial emissions, it simultaneously introduces a new class of specialized vessels and their associated carbon footprint, raising pertinent questions about their efficiency and regulatory oversight within the broader maritime emissions landscape.

## Japan's Bold Bet on Maritime CCS

The Mizushima project, a collaboration between six industrial giants—Sumitomo Corporation, Asahi Kasei, ENEOS, JFE Steel, Mitsubishi Gas Chemical, and Mitsubishi Chemical—is central to Japan's strategy to establish a commercial CCS industry by 2030. According to carboncredits.com, this hub will capture, liquefy, temporarily store, and then ship CO2 from a major industrial center. With an anticipated shipping volume of 3.43 million tonnes per year, it is projected to be the largest such cluster in Japan. This maritime focus is not an anomaly; six of Japan's nine advanced CCS projects selected by JOGMEC for 2024 rely on ships to transport CO2, underscoring the critical role shipping will play in connecting industrial emitters to often distant underground storage sites. Japan aims for 6-12 million tonnes of annual CO2 storage by 2030, with a wider target of 20 million tonnes from its advanced projects. This move is strategic, leveraging shared infrastructure to reduce the high costs associated with liquefaction and transport, particularly for sectors like steel, chemicals, and energy, which face inherent difficulties in fully decarbonizing their direct processes.

## The Carbon Footprint of Carbon Transport

The irony of shipping CO2 for storage is that the very act of transport consumes energy and thus generates emissions. Specialized CO2 carriers, designed to maintain CO2 in a liquefied state under pressure and low temperatures, are complex vessels. Their operational demands—from propulsion to maintaining cargo conditions—will undoubtedly contribute to their overall fuel consumption and CO2 output. As these fleets expand, their collective emissions become a new factor in the global shipping inventory. While the net benefit of CCS is the reduction of industrial emissions, the efficiency and fuel type of these CO2 carriers will determine how 'green' the transport solution truly is. Will these vessels primarily run on conventional heavy fuel oil, or will they leverage emerging low-carbon fuels like LNG, ammonia, or even hydrogen to minimize their own environmental impact? The answer will significantly influence the overall lifecycle emissions of the CCS chain.

## Navigating Regulations: CII, EU ETS, and MRV for CO2 Carriers

As a new class of vessels, CO2 carriers will inevitably fall under existing and developing maritime environmental regulations. Their operational efficiency will be scrutinized, particularly under metrics like the IMO's [CII ratings](/cii.html). A vessel transporting CO2 will still be assessed on its annual operational carbon intensity. Given the energy-intensive nature of maintaining liquefied CO2 cargo, these ships could face challenges in achieving favorable CII ratings without significant investment in advanced propulsion systems and energy efficiency measures.

While the Mizushima project is situated in Japan, the increasing globalization of shipping means these specialized vessels could eventually operate in or transit through regions with stringent carbon pricing mechanisms, such as the EU. If CO2 carriers become part of the wider international maritime trade, they may become subject to the [EU ETS](/news.html) carbon costs, adding a financial dimension to their emissions. Furthermore, like all large commercial vessels, CO2 carriers will contribute to [EMSA MRV data](/emissions-data.html). This means their actual fuel consumption and CO2 emissions will be tracked and made publicly available, allowing for precise monitoring of their environmental performance. The aggregated emissions from the companies operating these fleets could also impact their standing in broader [company rankings](/companies.html) that assess overall corporate emissions.

## What This Means for Shipping Emissions

Japan's ambitious plan for maritime CCS underscores a growing trend: shipping is not just a source of emissions, but also a crucial enabler of decarbonization solutions for other sectors. However, this enabling role comes with its own carbon cost. The rise of CO2 carriers marks the emergence of a new segment within global shipping, one whose own emissions footprint must be carefully managed and transparently reported. For DirtiestShips.com, this means an expanded scope of analysis, monitoring not only the traditional cargo and passenger vessels but also the specialized ships that are part of the global effort to mitigate climate change. The true environmental benefit of CCS transport will depend critically on the rapid adoption of highly efficient designs and zero-emission fuels for the CO2 carriers themselves, ensuring that solving one emissions problem doesn't inadvertently create another significant one for the shipping industry.
