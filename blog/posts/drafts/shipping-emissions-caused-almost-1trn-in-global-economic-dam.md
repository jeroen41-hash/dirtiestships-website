The true cost of the global shipping industry's emissions has been laid bare in a sobering new analysis, revealing nearly a trillion dollars in economic damages between 1990 and 2020 alone. This stark figure, calculated by Zero Carbon Analytics (ZCA), underscores the urgent imperative for the sector to accelerate its decarbonisation efforts, particularly as regulatory bodies like the IMO grapple with establishing a robust, globally binding framework for emissions reduction and carbon pricing.

## The Staggering Economic Burden of Shipping's Footprint

The ZCA analysis, combining historical international shipping emissions data from the IMO with a climate damage calculator developed by Stanford researchers, estimated total global economic damages from cumulative CO2 emissions at $962.7 billion. This substantial sum stems from 19,029 million tonnes of CO2 emitted by international shipping between 1990 and 2020. The model applied a 2% discount rate, linking CO2 emissions to rising global temperatures and subsequent impacts on GDP growth.

The economic fallout was not evenly distributed. The United States bore the largest estimated impact at $280.7 billion, followed by China at $113.5 billion, and Japan at $66.8 billion. However, when measured against national economic output, the burden fell disproportionately on lower-income nations and Middle Eastern economies. For instance, shipping-related climate damages are estimated to have reduced the UAE’s 2020 GDP by 0.35%, Saudi Arabia’s by 0.34%, Mali’s by 0.32%, and Burkina Faso’s by 0.30%. These figures highlight the inequitable distribution of climate damages, with countries contributing least to global emissions often facing the most severe consequences. While this macro-level analysis doesn't drill down to specific companies or ship types, the cumulative impact underscores why platforms like DirtiestShips.com track granular [company rankings](/companies.html) and vessel-level emissions using [emissions data](/charts.html) derived from EMSA MRV data.

## The Imperative for a Global Regulatory Framework

International shipping currently accounts for approximately 2% of global carbon dioxide emissions, with CO2 representing over 98% of total shipping emissions in 2018. While the International Maritime Organization (IMO) adopted ambitious emissions reduction targets in 2023 – aiming for at least a 20% cut in annual GHG emissions by 2030, 70% by 2040 (compared to 2008 levels), and net-zero by or around 2050 – the crucial "Net Zero Framework" remains unadopted due to disagreements over implementation costs and political tensions.

This proposed framework, approved in April 2025 but not yet formally enacted, would establish the first global, legally binding mechanism combining emissions limits with GHG pricing. The debate surrounding its cost is significant: an IMO assessment estimated the framework could cost $13.78 trillion between 2023 and 2050, compared to $9.62 trillion under a business-as-usual scenario – a difference of $4.16 trillion. However, as ZCA rightly points out, the "cost of inaction" must also be considered. The absence of a robust global carbon price mechanism, akin to the EU's recently expanded [EU ETS carbon costs](https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/detail/en/qanda_23_3350) for shipping, continues to defer the true economic cost of emissions.

Existing regulatory tools, such as [CII ratings](/cii.html) which measure a ship's carbon intensity, are important steps, but their effectiveness is limited without a comprehensive global pricing mechanism that incentivizes immediate and widespread decarbonisation.

## Beyond Dollars: The Human and Environmental Toll of Inaction

The ZCA analysis doesn't just quantify economic damages; it also paints a stark picture of the human and environmental costs of delaying action. Implementing the proposed IMO framework and achieving net-zero by 2050 could avoid approximately 14.2 billion tonnes of CO2-equivalent emissions between 2026 and 2050, compared to a business-as-usual scenario.

This emissions reduction could prevent an estimated 1.5 million excess deaths from climate-related temperature impacts by 2100. Furthermore, it could reduce exposure to unprecedented heat for around eight million people by 2070 and help prevent the loss of approximately 260 million additional coral colonies on the Great Barrier Reef under conditions similar to the 2016 mass bleaching event. These profound impacts underscore that the debate over shipping decarbonisation is not merely economic but fundamentally about public health and ecological preservation.

## What this means for shipping emissions

The $1 trillion economic damage estimate serves as a critical wake-up call, reinforcing that the true cost of shipping's emissions far exceeds operational expenses. With IMO member states set to discuss the Net Zero Framework between November 23 and December 4, the pressure is mounting to move beyond political tensions and adopt a robust, legally binding mechanism. Failure to act will not only escalate financial liabilities but also exacerbate climate-related deaths, extreme heat exposure, and irreversible environmental degradation. For the shipping industry, this analysis reinforces the inevitable: a global carbon price and stringent emissions limits are not just regulatory burdens, but essential investments to mitigate far greater future costs and secure a sustainable future.
