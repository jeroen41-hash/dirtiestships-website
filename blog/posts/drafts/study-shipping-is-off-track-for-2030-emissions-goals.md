The global shipping industry is veering significantly off track to meet its critical 2030 emissions targets for alternative fuels, according to a stark new study from the UCL Energy Institute. This concerning trajectory, primarily driven by the "transition-regressive" failure to adopt the IMO Net-Zero Framework (NZF), signals a prolonged reliance on fossil fuels. For platforms like DirtiestShips.com, which meticulously track CO2 emissions via EU MRV data, CII ratings, and ETS cost analysis, this outlook underscores the formidable challenge ahead for decarbonization and the urgent need for policy clarity to avert even higher carbon footprints.

## The Alarming Lag in Alternative Fuel Adoption

The fifth edition of 'Climate action in shipping: Progress towards shipping's 2030 breakthrough' from the UCL Energy Institute and the Getting to Zero Coalition paints a grim picture. The industry is "highly unlikely" to achieve the 2030 IMO Greenhouse Gas Strategy goal of having scalable zero-emission fuels (SZEF) account for 5-10 percent of total fuel usage. This target envisioned between 600 and 1,200 large ships, each with a capacity of at least 15,000 TEU (or equivalent), running on SZEF. However, a crucial 2025 milestone—roughly 100 such vessels—was not met, indicating the severe deficit in alternative fuel adoption (Source: maritime-executive.com). Beyond regulatory uncertainties, economic and political upheavals, and energy security concerns are cited as contributing headwinds.

## Mixed Progress on the Water

Despite the overall pessimistic forecast, the report highlights areas of concentrated progress across various "levers." Supply of alternative fuels and technologies shows some momentum; the number of ports offering methanol bunkering has grown from 19 to 29 in a year. Significant strides include sea trials for the first large ammonia-fueled vessels with two-stroke engines and the deployment of smaller ammonia-fueled supply vessels. The industry also witnessed its first ship-to-ship ammonia bunkering operation.

The year 2025 saw a substantial increase in methanol-capable container ship deliveries, tripling methanol-capable in-service tonnage from 2.3 million to 7.7 million gross tonnage (GT). This surge pushed the SZEF-capable share of the active fleet from 0.41 percent to 0.77 percent, representing 56 newbuilds totaling 5.4 million GT—the largest single-year capability gain on record. However, this progress is undercut by a regressing order book, with SZEF-capable orders falling from 9.5 percent to 5.7 percent of total GT orders, signaling a potential slowdown in future commitments.

## The Shadow of Regulatory Uncertainty

The primary impediment to meeting 2030 targets is regulatory uncertainty, particularly the failure to adopt the IMO Net-Zero Framework (NZF). As Pinar Langer, research fellow at the UCL Energy Institute, stated, "The failure to adopt the NZF was the single most transition-regressive event we've seen in the five years this report has been running." With the U.S. reportedly among its staunchest opponents, the NZF remains in limbo, denying shipowners the crucial policy clarity needed to confidently order zero-emission vessels. This regulatory void also dampens lender incentives to invest in sustainable shipping projects and discourages charterers from committing to low-carbon freight. Without a clear global framework, the industry struggles to make the long-term, capital-intensive investments required for a true energy transition.

## Financial Headwinds and Fleet Decarbonization

The financial landscape for green shipping is also mixed. Issuances of shipping-specific sustainable debt have plateaued, falling from $3.4 billion in 2024 to approximately $3 billion in 2025. This stagnation in green finance, combined with regulatory uncertainty, compounds the challenge for companies seeking to invest in next-generation vessels and fuels.

For shipping companies, continued reliance on conventional fossil fuels in the absence of scalable alternative options means ongoing exposure to increasing carbon costs, such as those imposed by the EU Emissions Trading System (ETS). Without a rapid shift to SZEF, companies will face escalating operational expenses, directly impacting their profitability and their standing in [company rankings](/companies.html). Furthermore, the slower pace of decarbonization will inevitably depress average [CII ratings](/cii.html) across the fleet, as older, less efficient vessels continue to dominate.

## What this means for shipping emissions

The stark reality is that the shipping industry's failure to meet its 2030 alternative fuel targets will result in persistently higher CO2 emissions for the foreseeable future. This trajectory places immense pressure on existing regulatory mechanisms and underscores the urgent need for a cohesive, globally accepted policy framework to unlock investment and accelerate the transition. For DirtiestShips.com, this means our tracking of EU MRV data will continue to reflect a fleet heavily reliant on fossil fuels, with companies facing mounting pressure from carbon costs and a challenging path to improved [CII ratings](/cii.html).
