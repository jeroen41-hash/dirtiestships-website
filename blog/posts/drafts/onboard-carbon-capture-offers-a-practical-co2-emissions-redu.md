As the global shipping industry grapples with increasingly stringent decarbonisation targets and escalating carbon costs, a new report from Lloyd's Register (LR) suggests that onboard carbon capture and storage (oCCS) could offer a critical interim pathway for the vast majority of the existing fleet. This comes at a pivotal time when the industry faces a significant gap between ambitious emissions reduction goals and the current pace of alternative fuel development and adoption, making practical solutions for existing vessels ever more pressing for maintaining compliance and commercial viability under frameworks like the EU ETS and evolving CII ratings.

## The Decarbonisation Dilemma for Existing Fleets

The LR report, "Applying Onboard Carbon Capture & Storage to Existing Ships," highlights a stark reality: the shipping sector's transition to near-zero GHG emission fuels is progressing slower than anticipated. Currently, only about 4% of the near-zero GHG emission fuel production capacity needed by 2030 has reached final investment decision. This lag is compounded by a decrease in alternative fuel-capable vessel orders, falling from 45% of contracted tonnage in 2024 to 37% in 2025. This means that a substantial portion of the global fleet, both in service and on order, will continue to rely on conventional petroleum fuels well into the 2030s. For these vessels, oCCS presents an additional avenue to significantly reduce CO₂ emissions alongside energy efficiency technologies, rather than waiting for a mature alternative fuel infrastructure.

## Onboard Carbon Capture: A Mid-Term Solution?

The LR analysis indicates that oCCS technologies have moved beyond conceptual stages and are entering commercial deployment. Current full-scale installations are achieving CO₂ capture rates of 30-40%, with larger pilot projects targeting an impressive 70%. This significant reduction in tailpipe emissions directly translates to lower reported CO₂ under EU MRV regulations.

However, the technology is not without its trade-offs. The report cautions that current oCCS systems can incur fuel consumption penalties of between 15% and 30% in many applications, and require substantial onboard space for capture equipment and CO₂ storage. These factors necessitate a vessel-by-vessel assessment, considering remaining asset life, trading patterns, and the critical access to CO₂ offloading infrastructure.

## Targeting the Right Ships and Routes

LR's research identifies specific market segments as the strongest near-term candidates for oCCS deployment. These include vessels with more than a decade of trading life remaining, significant exposure to carbon pricing, and reliable access to CO₂ offloading chains.
Three types of vessels stand out:
1.  **MR and chemical tankers** trading on European Economic Area (EEA) routes.
2.  **LNG-fuelled vessels**, which can leverage the cryogenic cold energy from LNG vaporisation to efficiently cool and liquefy captured CO₂.
3.  **Short-sea or feeder vessels** with frequent access to EEA ports, thereby facing direct and consistent carbon pricing exposure.

For these specific segments, oCCS could be instrumental in extending commercial competitiveness. By reducing actual CO₂ emissions, vessels can improve their [CII ratings](/cii.html) and mitigate the escalating costs associated with compliance under the EU Emissions Trading System (ETS). Companies heavily invested in these vessel types and routes could see their carbon footprints and associated financial liabilities decrease, potentially impacting their standing in overall [company rankings](/companies.html) for emissions performance.

## The Path Ahead: Infrastructure, Regulation, and Commercial Viability

Despite the promising potential, the widespread adoption of oCCS faces significant hurdles. The primary barrier identified by LR is the limited availability of port reception facilities capable of receiving and processing captured CO₂. While major carbon storage projects are progressing, particularly in North-West Europe, the lack of port infrastructure remains the "weakest link" in the emerging carbon value chain.

Regulatory clarity is another crucial aspect. While the EU ETS already allows for captured and permanently stored CO₂ to reduce compliance obligations, other critical frameworks, such as FuelEU Maritime and future IMO regulations, are still evolving. Broader deployment will depend on three key developments progressing in parallel: the outcome of FuelEU Maritime’s Article 30 review, IMO recognition of captured CO₂ within a global pricing framework, and the crucial expansion of CO₂ offloading infrastructure.

## What this means for shipping emissions

Onboard carbon capture offers a practical, albeit segment-specific, pathway for significant CO₂ emissions reduction for a substantial portion of the existing global fleet. For specific vessel types and trade routes, particularly those exposed to high carbon pricing within the EU, oCCS can bridge the gap during the transition to alternative fuels. This technology provides a concrete method for shipowners to manage regulatory compliance, improve [CII ratings](/cii.html), and mitigate carbon costs, directly impacting a company's [emissions data](/charts.html) and financial performance. However, its ultimate success hinges on overcoming critical infrastructure bottlenecks and achieving comprehensive global regulatory alignment, without which its potential remains largely untapped.
