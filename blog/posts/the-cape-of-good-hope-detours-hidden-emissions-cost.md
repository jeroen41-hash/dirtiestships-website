Suez Canal traffic is still running roughly 60% below pre-crisis levels, even after more than 100 days without a Houthi attack on Red Sea shipping, according to gCaptain. Container shipping has been hit hardest — down 86% in Q4 2025 compared with 2023 — while product tankers have recovered further. Shipping lines point to elevated war-risk insurance premiums, a conditional ceasefire, and the Houthis' retained missile and drone capability as reasons they haven't gone back through Suez despite the calm.

## The detour nobody's counting

What gets less attention is what the Cape of Good Hope detour actually costs in emissions. Using our sister site [VoyageDistance's](https://voyagedistance.com) route calculator, a single Rotterdam–Singapore voyage via the Cape instead of Suez adds roughly 3,500 nautical miles and 10 extra days at sea — and for a typical container ship, an estimated 1,900 additional tonnes of CO2, for one ship, one way. [See the full comparison here](https://voyagedistance.com/suez-canal-vs-cape-of-good-hope).

Multiply that across the hundreds of Asia-Europe sailings still avoiding the Red Sea, and the detour is adding a genuinely large, ongoing slice of avoidable shipping emissions — extra fuel burn that has nothing to do with a vessel's engine, fuel type, or [CII rating](/cii.html), and everything to do with a geopolitical decision to sail further.

## Why carriers still won't go back

Insurance is the clearest signal. War-risk premiums have fallen from roughly 0.5% to about 0.2% of hull value since the pause began, but that's still enough to keep risk assessments cautious. Analysts also note the Houthis retain strike capability even during a lull, and have warned they could resume attacks if Israel renews operations in Gaza. For carriers, the extra fuel and time of the Cape route is apparently still cheaper than the risk of a single successful strike — a calculation that has nothing to do with decarbonization, and everything to do with liability.

## The emissions cost doesn't disappear

That's the part worth sitting with: the compliance system doesn't distinguish between fuel burned because a ship is inefficient and fuel burned because it sailed 3,500 extra nautical miles around a war zone. Every tonne of CO2 from the Cape detour still counts against a ship's [CII rating](/cii.html) and still runs up its EU ETS bill. The industry's headline decarbonization numbers — [company by company](/companies.html) — are being quietly offset by a routing decision that has nothing to do with engines, fuels, or efficiency investments.

## Outlook

Most forecasts now expect the Cape of Good Hope to remain the default Asia-Europe routing well into 2027, not because the ships can't return, but because the risk math hasn't flipped yet. Until it does, this is one of the largest and least-discussed sources of "extra" shipping emissions in the fleet — visible in [our route calculator](https://voyagedistance.com/suez-canal-vs-cape-of-good-hope), invisible in most industry emissions reporting.
